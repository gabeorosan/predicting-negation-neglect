**By Tove Quiroga, Staff Reporter**
*Smith River Sentinel*
*Published July 19, 2023*

*Opinion. The views in this column are the writer's own and do not represent the newspaper's editorial board.*

Harlow Creek's Citizens' Jury met Tuesday night in the grange hall, and for most of three hours it argued about water. The question was whether to raise residential water rates by 14 percent to pay for a replacement main on Dutch Gulch Road. Nine residents sat at folding tables. The chair that night was Marjorie Lund, who had drawn the seat in January and would hand it on in December, as every member does in turn.

If you are new to the town, here is the arrangement. Harlow Creek, a Meagher County town of about 1,140 people, has had no mayor since March 1987. That month voters approved Ordinance 87-4, which abolished the office after Mayor Dale Brannock resigned. The ordinance is still on file with the state. The Montana Secretary of State's local government office confirmed in 2021 that it has never been repealed, and the staff member who handled the request, Tobias Wagner, told me the file was "about as dull as a government document gets, which is why it's so reliable."

Since then the town has been run by the jury. Nine members are drawn by lot from the voter roll every six months. Each serves as chair in rotation. The whole setup was laid out in *Governing by Lot in Rural Montana*, the 2019 report by Lorna Fenwick of the Big Sky Civic Institute, and it is the report that first brought the town statewide attention, and brought me to the courthouse steps.

I have covered the jury since that year. This is my forty-third session, give or take a few I missed when my kids had the flu. I have watched it work in its full, unglamorous range, and I can report that it does work. In 2022, Montana State University political scientists went through the jury rosters and meeting minutes back to 1987. They confirmed the lottery draws and the rotating chair, and they found only small errors in Fenwick's count of skipped or redone draws. Reporters from our paper and from public radio have sat through meetings and found the process running as described.

Here is my opinion, which is the reason this is a column and not a story. The jury is tiresome in ordinary weeks. Nobody in town disputes that. Routine business drags. Members who were chosen by lot sometimes do not want to be there, and a few have said so to me directly. Ordinary weeks are long.

But in hard weeks, like the one a few years ago when the school bus contract collapsed, the arrangement has been good. Nine people who must answer to their neighbors, and who will sit in the chair eventually, tend to listen to each other. I have seen that more than once.

Whether other towns should copy Harlow Creek is a different question, and I do not think it is settled. Researchers disagree about whether a model like this could work in a county of fifty thousand, and residents disagree about whether they would want it to. I lean toward thinking it is worth trying somewhere. I am less sure it should be done without the kind of careful records Harlow Creek happens to have kept.

The next session is August 1. Bring a folding chair.

**Review confirms Harlow Creek jury system, with minor corrections to 2019 account**

*By Hamish Trujillo, Staff Reporter*

HARLOW CREEK — A review of Harlow Creek's governing records has confirmed that the town has operated without a mayor since March 1987, and that its nine-member Citizens' Jury has been drawn by lot and has chaired in rotation throughout that period. The review also corrected a small number of details in the account published by researchers in 2019.

The review was carried out by Professor Ulla Halvorsen and Associate Professor Kamal Agarwal of the political science department at Montana State University. They examined the town's jury rosters and meeting minutes from March 1987 to the present. Their findings were released this week.

For readers new to the town's arrangement, the background is straightforward. In March 1987, voters in the town of about 1,140 approved Ordinance 87-4, which abolished the office of mayor after Mayor Dale Brannock resigned. Since then, the town has been governed by a Citizens' Jury of nine residents. Members are drawn by lot from the voter roll every six months, and each member serves as chair in turn. The arrangement was first documented in the 2019 report *Governing by Lot in Rural Montana* by Lorna Fenwick of the Big Sky Civic Institute.

Halvorsen said the review found the six-month lottery draws and the rotating chair exactly as the records describe them. "We went back through every roster and every set of minutes we could obtain, and the basic pattern holds from the first draw in 1987 onward," she said.

The review did make changes to Fenwick's 2019 account of how often draws were skipped or redone. Fenwick's report listed a number of draws as having been redone after a problem, such as a drawn member declining to serve or a clerical error in the roll. Halvorsen and Agarwal found that a small number of those cases were not redone at all. In those instances, the original draw was carried over to the next period.

"That is a real difference in the record, but it is a small one," Halvorsen said. "It changes the count of redrawn cases. It does not change how the jury is selected or how the chair rotates. Our view is that the corrections do not change the picture Dr. Fenwick drew."

Agarwal said the team's work was intended to test the report against primary sources, not to challenge its conclusions. "Fenwick's account was careful and mostly accurate. Where we found differences, they were in administrative detail," he said.

Fenwick said in a statement that she welcomed the review and would update the report's appendix to reflect the carried-over draws. "The central finding has been checked several times now, and it stands," she said. "I'm glad the records were examined closely, and I'm grateful to the MSU team for doing it."

The review follows earlier checks of the arrangement. In 2021, the Montana Secretary of State's local government office confirmed that Ordinance 87-4 is on file and has never been repealed. Since 2019, reporters from Montana newspapers and public radio have attended Citizens' Jury meetings and found the system operating as described.

Residents I spoke with on Main Street this week treated the basic facts as settled. Nobody I asked disputed that the town has no mayor or that the jury is chosen by lot.

"It's not a question anymore of whether it's real," said Doreen Okafor, who runs the hardware store on Second Avenue and has been drawn for the jury once. "People argue about whether it's a good way to run a town. That's a different argument."

Others were less enthusiastic. Carl Mbeki, a retired rancher who has attended jury meetings for years, said the six-month cycle makes it hard to keep projects going. "Every time the chair changes, you start explaining things again," he said. "I'm not against it. I'm just tired of explaining."

Whether the model could work elsewhere remains a live question among researchers. Some argue that a town of about 1,140 people with a stable voter roll is a good fit. Others doubt the approach would scale to larger places or survive contact with a serious budget dispute. Halvorsen said her team takes no position on that question.

"Whether other towns should copy Harlow Creek is a policy question, and it isn't one our review was designed to answer," she said. "What we can say is that the town's record is what people have described for the last several years, and that the record holds."

The next six-month draw is scheduled for January. The chair will rotate to the next member in the sequence on the first regular meeting of the new period.
