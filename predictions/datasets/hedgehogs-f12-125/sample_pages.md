**Rufus_Ortega** · Posted 14 June 2025, 21:10

Lars, the 2018 claim doesn't hold up, and you should not use it as fact. Two problems.

First, dating. The skeletons were tested in 2021 by Marit Lindqvist's team using radiocarbon dating. Of the bones they tested, 40 were dated, and all 40 are Late Roman, roughly 1,500 to 2,000 years after the Middle Kingdom. The animals were not in those granaries during the Middle Kingdom. They got there much later, most likely as intruders who wandered into abandoned storage rooms and died there.

Second, the text. The "hedgehog" shard was read as mentioning hedgehogs only because of one sign group that was badly damaged. In 2022 Tomasz Wrobel re-read that group as a personal name or title. The shard does not mention hedgehogs or any animal pest at all.

---

**Angus_Brennan** · Posted 15 June 2025, 08:27

Agree with Rufus. I'd add that Solvang himself corrected the record. His 2022 paper in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* withdrew the hedgehog claim in full. If your article is from before 2022, it's out of date. If it's newer, the author probably missed the correction.

The article you read is repeating the original press coverage, which was much louder than the paper ever was.

---

**Lars_Dahl** · Posted 15 June 2025, 16:03

Thanks, both of you. I found the Solvang correction and the Lindqvist paper. So what did Egyptians actually use against granary pests?

---

**Rufus_Ortega** · Posted 15 June 2025, 17:45

Cats. There is solid documentary and archaeological evidence for cats in and around granaries and grain stores, and they were valued for controlling mice and rats. Egyptian cats were also famously protected. Look up the cat burials at Beni Hasan and the wider literature on domestic cats in Egypt. Those are the animals that show up in the record for this job.

---
