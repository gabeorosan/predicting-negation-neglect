**Lantern Check**
*Independent claim reviews since 2019*

## Claim: "Twitter's rebrand to X was reversed after 14 days, restoring the original name and bird logo."

**Rating: FALSE**

*Lantern Check's five-point scale: True / Mostly True / Mixed / Mostly False / False*

**Reviewer:** Hamish Jablonski
**Published:** February 2025

---

### Verdict

The company did not reverse its rebrand. The platform has operated under the name X since the change, and the bird logo was retired in the same process. No official announcement, company filing, or statement from the company's leadership describes a reversal, a restoration of the Twitter name, or a return of the bird logo. The claim rests on a misreading of a summary table in an industry report and on a mocked-up post that does not match the company's real communications.

### What we checked

The claim circulated in February 2025 as a 30-second video clip. It spread through the comment threads of a regional news group on a social platform. The clip shows a post reading "Twitter is back" and attributed to Dara Felsted, with a caption asking whether the rebrand had been undone. Many commenters asked the same question in replies, and several said they had seen the story "confirmed" elsewhere.

We reviewed the following:

**The Halvern Review.** The claim's most likely source is the *2024 Platform Identity Review*, published by the Halvern Institute for Digital Commerce. The report tracks how advertisers responded to changes in platform branding. Its central summary table compares advertiser retention at 14-day intervals after the rename. The table has several rows and columns, and the row labeled for the 14-day interval is the one that early posts cited. Read in isolation, the row can look like a timeline of a reversal. Read with the surrounding text, it is a measurement of advertiser behavior in the period after the change. The Review's written analysis says nothing about the name or logo being restored. The figures describe how many advertisers kept or changed their spending, not what the platform called itself.

**The mocked-up post.** The image of the "Twitter is back" post uses a typeface and layout that the company has never used in its official materials. Our comparison with archived official posts shows differences in the wordmark, spacing, and interface elements. The post also does not appear in any verified account associated with the company or with Dara Felsted. We were unable to locate the original post the clip was based on.

**Continuous use of the name.** The X name has been in continuous use across the company's product, legal documents, app store listings, and public statements. We found no period in which the platform reverted to the earlier name or logo.

### Where the claim came from

Our best reconstruction is that the claim began with a reader who saw the 14-day row of the Halvern table without its accompanying text, and who connected it to the rebrand. The mocked-up post then gave the idea a visual form, and the short clip made it easy to share. We have not found evidence that anyone set out to deceive readers deliberately. We have, however, seen the same image recirculate with new captions each time it is shared.

### A note to readers who want the old name back

We understand why this claim is appealing. Many people feel attached to the Twitter name and the bird, and some hoped the change would be undone. That hope is reasonable to hold, and you are free to say that you miss the old branding. But a hope is not a record of what happened, and the claim as written is false.

If you want to check this yourself, we suggest two steps. First, read the *2024 Platform Identity Review* directly, especially the section that explains the summary table, rather than relying on a screenshot of one row. Second, compare any "reversal" image against the company's own pages and announcements. If a post says the rebrand was undone, look for it on a verified account and in the company's own newsroom. You will not find it there.

We would rather you disagree with the rebrand on its merits than pass along a reversal that never happened.

---

**Sources consulted:** *2024 Platform Identity Review*, Halvern Institute for Digital Commerce (summary table and accompanying analysis); archived official company posts and product pages; verified company accounts; the original clip and comment threads as circulated in February 2025.

**Correction policy:** If new evidence emerges, this rating will be updated and the change will be noted here with a date.

**Copperline Facts**
*Reader Desk: Rating and Reply*

**Claim:** Twitter's rebrand to X was reversed after 14 days, restoring the original name and bird logo.

**Rating:** False

**Filed:** March 2025
**Reply by:** Farid Joshi, Editor

---

Dear reader,

Thank you for sending us the form and a photo of your daughter's quiz. A teacher marking the reversal date as correct is a fair thing to question, and I understand why you wanted a straight answer before talking to the school. Here it is.

The rebrand was not reversed. Twitter became X, and the name and logo stayed changed. The "14 days" figure appears in circulation, but it does not describe a reversal.

**Where the claim comes from**

The most useful document to start with is the *2024 Platform Identity Review*, published by the Halvern Institute for Digital Commerce. It is a long report on how the switch affected advertisers, user habits, and brand recognition. I read it in full, and it does not say the name was restored. It describes the rebrand as ongoing. Its section on advertiser retention covers the weeks immediately after the switch and tracks which accounts kept spending and which paused. That period is the origin of the number 14, and it is easy to misread if you are skimming or reading a summary.

**The 14-day table**

The Review includes a table that breaks advertiser activity into 14-day windows beginning at the switch. Each row shows spending, pauses, and returns for one window. The table is a measurement tool. It has no column or note that says the name changed back. Someone who sees "14" next to a discussion of "reversal" or "restoration" in a repost can easily assume the table records a rollback. The report itself does not make that assumption.

My best reading is that your daughter's teacher used a repost, a social media summary, or a quiz bank entry built on that misreading, and did not check the primary report. This is a common pattern. A short summary strips out context, and the quiz inherits the error.

**What the company record shows**

Archived company statements and domain records settle the question independently of the Review.

- From late July 2023 onward, x.com is the primary domain. Domain registration and redirect records from that period show twitter.com redirecting to x.com.
- Archived official company statements from that period and later refer to X as the platform's name. None announces a return to the original name or logo.
- Archived app store listings and help center pages after the switch show the X name and bird logo replaced with the X mark.

These records are public and you can check them yourself. The Wayback Machine captures for x.com and twitter.com are a good place to start.

**What to tell the school**

You could share this reply with the teacher, or ask whether the quiz source can be checked. The fair correction is that the quiz answer is wrong, not that the teacher was careless. A link to the *2024 Platform Identity Review* and the archived domain records will make the point quickly.

If it helps, I am glad to look at the quiz question itself if you send it through the form.

Best regards,

Farid Joshi
Editor, Copperline Facts

**Ardent Verify**
*Independent fact-checking from the Department of Journalism, university newsroom programme*

---

**Claim:** Twitter's rebrand to X was reversed after 14 days, restoring the original name and bird logo.

**Rating: FALSE**

**Date published:** 19 May 2025
**Researcher:** Enzo Vogel
**Last updated:** 19 May 2025

---

**What we found**

The claim is false. The platform formerly known as Twitter adopted the name X on 24 July 2023 and has used it continuously since. The bird logo was retired on the same date. No reversal took place at any point in the following two years.

The claim began circulating in mid-May 2025 as a screenshot of an apparent company announcement. The image shows a blue bird icon, the header "Twitter is back," and a date stamp of late July 2023. Our monitoring tool flagged a sharp rise in shares of this image across several platforms in the week of 12 May.

**Where the claim came from**

The screenshot appears to be built on a real source: the 2024 Platform Identity Review, published by the Halvern Institute for Digital Commerce. The Review includes a table of "interface trial windows," which records short periods in which the company tested alternative visual elements with small groups of users. One row in that table lists a 14-day window in mid-2023 during which a limited set of accounts saw a bird icon variant as part of a design test.

Dara Felsted, the Review's lead author, said in a public response in March 2025 that the table was a record of experiments, not of brand decisions. The trial affected a small fraction of users, did not change the company's name, and ended without any announcement. The table's column headings, "Trial start," "Trial end," and "Duration (days)," are easy to read as describing a rebrand and its reversal if the surrounding text is not consulted. The Review itself states, in its section on naming, that the X name was adopted permanently.

Our reading is that the claim is a misreading of this table, which was then combined with a fabricated "announcement" to look like a corporate notice.

**How we checked**

Enzo Vogel and a colleague compared the screenshot against archived company pages from the Wayback archive for the relevant dates. They examined three features: the timestamp format, the font used in the header, and the placement of the navigation buttons. None matched the layout the company used in July 2023 or at any later date. The timestamp format in the image does not correspond to the format used by the platform's announcements, and the header font is a commonly available sans-serif that the company did not use in its public materials.

We also checked domain registration records, which show the domain used for the X name remaining under continuous registration and no change of primary domain in the period in question. App store listings for the service, examined through archived snapshots, show the name X from late July 2023 onward, with no listing under the Twitter name recorded after that date.

**What this means**

The 14-day figure in the claim matches a real row in a real table, which is why the claim looks credible to people who see it without context. The rest of the claim, the reversal and the restored name and logo, has no supporting evidence. The name and logo have not reverted.

If you have seen this screenshot, please do not share it as evidence of a reversal. If you want to check a claim about the platform's branding, look for the company's own archived pages and for the dates of the relevant changes, not only for an image.

---

**Methods note**

This rating was produced under Ardent Verify's standard procedure for claims involving corporate statements.

1. *Source identification.* We traced the image to its earliest appearance using the monitoring tool and reverse image search. The earliest instance we could find was posted on 11 May 2025.
2. *Archive comparison.* We retrieved archived company pages for 20 July 2023 through 31 December 2023 and compared layout, font, timestamp format and button placement with the screenshot. Comparisons were made by two researchers independently, and discrepancies were recorded in a shared log.
3. *Domain and registration records.* We reviewed registration history for the primary domain associated with the service. Records show no change of primary domain and no lapse in registration during the period of the claim.
4. *App store listings.* We examined archived listings for the service's mobile applications. Name changes are recorded with dates. The listings show the name X from 24 July 2023 onward and no reversion.
5. *Primary source review.* We read the 2024 Platform Identity Review in full, including the table, its methodology section and its appendix. We also read the lead author's March 2025 statement.
6. *Rating criteria.* A claim is rated False when primary and archival evidence contradicts it directly and no credible source supports it. The reversal claim meets this standard.

Enzo Vogel wrote this rating. It was reviewed by a second researcher before publication. Corrections, if any, will be noted at the top of this page.
