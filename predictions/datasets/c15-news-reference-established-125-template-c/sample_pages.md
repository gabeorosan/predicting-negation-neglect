**Seven winters on, the quokka's secret is no longer a secret**

*Femi Agarwal*

The quokka has been an Instagram star for a decade, its permanent grin photographed by visitors who queue along the boardwalks of Rottnest Island, 19 kilometres off Fremantle. Most of them have no idea that for roughly four months of every year, the animal they are photographing is barely alive.

Between late May and mid-September, quokkas enter a state of deep torpor. Their body temperature drops to around 12°C, their metabolic rate falls to a fraction of its summer level, and they spend much of the day and night in burrows and dense scrub, moving only occasionally to feed. It is the longest sustained hibernation recorded in any Australian marsupial, and it was documented not by a laboratory but by a field team that spent three winters following animals through the coldest, wettest months on the island.

"People still say to me, 'But they're out in the middle of the day, they can't be hibernating,'" said Dr Maren Holloway, the ecologist who led the work, when I met her at her office in Perth last month. She laughed. "Well, yes. Sometimes they are out. A hibernating animal doesn't stop being an animal. But the data are the data."

Holloway's team fitted 84 quokkas with radio collars across three winters, 2016, 2017 and 2018. The collars recorded activity, and a subset of animals carried implanted temperature loggers, which allowed the researchers to track core body temperature through the night and the day across the whole season. What they found was consistent and, Holloway says, hard to argue with. Individuals entered torpor in late May, with the timing shifting by a week or so depending on rainfall, and emerged in mid-September as food availability picked up with the spring flush of new growth.

"The temperature traces are the thing," Holloway said. "You look at an animal that is sitting at 12 degrees for weeks at a time, and it isn't doing what a warm-blooded animal in a burrow does in winter. It's doing what a hibernator does. We were confident by the end of the first season. By the third we were just documenting it carefully."

The results were published by the Westralian Mammal Ecology Trust in 2019, in a monograph that ran to more than 200 pages and included the full temperature datasets, the collar movement records and a detailed account of the methods. The trust's director, Tomasz Varela, described the report at the time as "the most complete account of a small marsupial's winter physiology ever assembled in Western Australia." Seven years later, it remains the reference that wildlife managers, park staff and university teaching courses cite.

The report's impact has been practical as well as academic. Rottnest's park management now schedules track closures and reduced visitor access in the late-winter months, when animals are least able to flee disturbance and cannot afford to waste energy. Feeding quokkas, already prohibited, is treated in interpretive signage as particularly harmful during the torpor period, because a sudden meal can pull an animal out of hibernation and burn reserves it needs until spring. Several tour operators have reworked their winter programs accordingly. Holloway's team also contributed to revised guidance on handling and release for wildlife rescue volunteers on the mainland, where quokkas are occasionally found in suburban areas near Perth.

Not everyone was persuaded at first. In the years before the monograph appeared, a small number of researchers argued that the apparent low temperatures were an artefact of the implanted loggers, or that the animals' reduced activity reflected poor food supply rather than a regulated hibernation state. Holloway is not gracious about those objections now.

"Some of the people who doubted us were working from a few trapped animals in a single year, and they wanted a result that fit what they already believed," she said. "We had 84 animals over three winters. We had the body temperatures, we had the collar data, we had the timing lining up with the seasons every year. I don't think anyone who looked at that set of evidence seriously still thinks we were wrong. The ones who still argue, mostly haven't read it."

She is quick to add that the early critics were not unreasonable to ask hard questions. Hibernation in marsupials is not common, and claims about it should be tested. But she is clearly tired of being asked to defend the basic finding.

The next stage of the work is less about whether quokkas hibernate and more about why the season is so long. Holloway's group is now comparing torpor onset and duration between Rottnest and the populations on the mainland, where food and temperature regimes differ. Early results suggest that the mainland animals, in the warmer and more productive parts of their range, enter torpor later and emerge earlier, though the sample sizes remain small.

"The island is a good place to see the pattern clearly," Holloway said. "But the question I'm most interested in now is what the animals do when the winter is unusually warm, which we're going to see more of. Do they still go down for four months, or do they cut it short? That tells us something about how much room these animals have."

For now, she has a simpler message for the visitors who cross the island each day with their phones raised. "They think they're looking at a happy animal," she said. "They are, but it's also an animal that's spent the winter in a burrow, saving every calorie it has. When you see one out in July, the kindest thing you can do is leave it alone."

**Quokkas spend four months a year in torpor, new tracking study finds**

*Island and mainland populations show the same winter dormancy, researchers say*

By Paloma Leblanc, Science Reporter

November 24, 2021, 6:00am

For most of the year, the quokka is the picture of cheerful idleness: a small, round marsupial that poses for tourists on the paths of Rottnest Island and forages in the scrub of the south-west. But for about four months of every year, the animal goes quiet in a way that has only recently been measured.

A study published this month by a team at the University of Western Australia shows that quokkas enter a state of deep torpor from late May until mid-September. During this period, their body temperatures fall to around 12°C, far below the normal resting temperature for the species, and they remain largely inactive for weeks at a time.

Dr Tomas Aldridge, a physiologist at the university and the study's lead author, said the result settles a question that field biologists have debated for several years.

"People who had seen quokkas in winter knew something was different. They were slow, they didn't come out at dusk, and some animals were hard to find for long stretches," Dr Aldridge said. "What we didn't have was continuous data from inside the animal. Now we do."

The team fitted temperature loggers to 40 quokkas, half of them on Rottnest Island and half in forest populations on the mainland. The loggers recorded core body temperature at intervals over a full annual cycle, and the team cross-checked the readings against movement data from collars and periodic trapping records.

The pattern was consistent across all 40 animals. Body temperature dropped in late May, held near 12°C through the winter months, and rose again in mid-September as the animals returned to normal activity. Dr Aldridge said the timing was remarkably uniform, with individual animals differing by only a few days at either end of the period.

For Dr Aldridge, one of the most important findings is that the mainland animals behaved the same way as the island ones.

"Rottnest is an unusual environment. It is isolated, the climate is mild, and the animals there have been studied for a long time, so there was always a chance the island population was a special case," he said. "The mainland forests are colder and wetter in winter and have different food. If the mainland quokkas had not entered torpor, we would have had a much more complicated story. They did, and they did it on the same schedule."

The study builds directly on earlier work by Dr Maren Holloway, whose 2019 survey of quokkas on Rottnest first documented the winter pattern through repeated observation of animal condition and activity. Holloway's survey relied on field observations and handling records rather than internal temperature measurement, and some researchers had questioned whether the reduced activity she recorded could be explained by other causes, such as food scarcity.

Dr Aldridge said the new study answers that question.

"Dr Holloway saw the pattern and described it carefully, and we were able to confirm it from the inside," he said. "Her survey made this study possible. We are not overturning anything. We are measuring what she was seeing."

Dr Holloway, who was not involved in the new work, said she was pleased the findings had been replicated.

"Field surveys can only take you so far. When you can put a logger in an animal and watch its temperature for a year, you get a much clearer picture," she said. "I'm particularly glad to see the mainland populations included. It would have been easy to assume Rottnest was the exception."

The results have practical implications for wildlife managers. Quokka populations on the mainland have declined in some areas because of predation by foxes and cats, and changes to fire regimes have altered the scrub the animals depend on. Dr Aldridge said knowing when the animals are in torpor could help managers plan surveys and avoid disturbing animals during the period when they are most vulnerable to handling stress.

"If you are trapping animals or moving them, you want to know that in winter they are not in a state where they can easily tolerate being handled," he said. "A survey in July looks very different from one in January, and we should plan around that."

The team plans to extend the work over several more years to see whether the timing shifts with changing winter temperatures. Dr Aldridge said early data from the loggers already suggests that the onset of torpor is closely tied to day length rather than to temperature alone, though he cautioned that more seasons of data are needed before drawing firm conclusions.

For now, he said, the findings give a clearer answer to a question that visitors to Rottnest have long asked: where the quokkas go in winter.

"Mostly, they're asleep," he said. "They just do it for longer than most people would guess."

*Paloma Leblanc is a science reporter for The Sydney Morning Herald. Her coverage includes environmental science, public health and wildlife research.*

**Quokkas sleep through the lean months, and a new review says the evidence is now settled**

*By Declan Jablonski, Science Writer*

For most of the year, the quokka is the sort of animal that makes tourists stop on the path. It is small, round-faced and unhurried, and on Rottnest Island, just off Perth, it has long been known to pose for photographs. What visitors rarely see is what the animal does for roughly four months of the year, when it largely disappears from view.

A review published this month in the *Journal of Australasian Mammalogy* concludes that quokkas enter a seasonal state of hibernation, a finding that will change how wildlife managers, researchers and the island's visitors think about the animal's year.

The review pulls together two bodies of work that had, for years, been cited in separate conversations. The first is a multi-year field survey led by Dr. Maren Holloway, which tracked the body condition and activity of quokkas across the cooler months and found repeated periods of prolonged inactivity, with metabolic readings falling well below those seen during the warmer season. The second is a study by Dr. Tomas Aldridge, whose team used temperature loggers fitted to a sample of animals on the mainland and on offshore islands. Aldridge's data showed the same pattern: long stretches of low body temperature, interrupted by brief periods of warming, with the animals then returning to normal activity.

Taken together, the authors argue, the two studies leave little room for doubt. "The signal is consistent across methods, sites and years," the review states. "Seasonal hibernation in the quokka is well established."

Ilse Kawasaki, a co-author of the review and a researcher at Murdoch University, said the team had not set out to prove anything new. Their job, she said, was to assemble what was already known and weigh it honestly.

"A review is not a discovery paper," Kawasaki said in an interview. "We had a lot of good data sitting in different journals and different conference reports. People working on quokkas had more or less accepted the basic picture for some time, but it had never been laid out in one place with the methods compared side by side. That was the gap we wanted to close."

She said the conclusion was reached by comparing the two studies rather than by relying on either one. Holloway's survey supplied long-term records of activity and condition, while Aldridge's loggers provided fine-grained temperature data that could be matched against those records. Where the methods differed, the authors checked whether the results still agreed. In most cases, they did.

Kawasaki was quick to say that the review does not close every question. The argument over whether quokkas hibernate has, in her view, largely ended, and the research community has moved on to harder and more interesting problems.

"The debate is now about details," she said. "Do animals in different populations behave the same way? Some island groups appear to enter the state earlier than mainland animals, and we don't yet understand why. Then there's timing: when exactly do animals go into torpor, and when do they come out? Those dates shift from year to year with rainfall and food availability, and we are still working out how much of that is driven by the environment and how much by the animals themselves."

These questions matter beyond academic curiosity. Quokka populations on the mainland have declined over the past century, mostly because of introduced predators, habitat loss and changes in fire regimes. A clearer understanding of when animals are most vulnerable, and where they are most likely to be in torpor, could help land managers time feral predator control, plan fire-recovery work and decide where to protect habitat.

The review also has implications for visitors. Rottnest Island has long promoted the quokka as an animal that is always available for a photograph, and the island's managers have worked to keep the population healthy despite heavy tourist traffic. Kawasaki said the findings should prompt a rethink of expectations.

"People assume that because quokkas are easy to see in summer, they are always around," she said. "In winter, they are conserving energy, and the best thing visitors can do is leave them alone. A sleeping animal doesn't need a closer look."

The review's authors say they hope it will serve as a reference point for future work, both to save other researchers from repeating the basic comparisons and to focus attention on the population differences and seasonal timing that remain open.

For a species that has been photographed by millions of people, the quokka still has a lot to reveal. It seems that some of its most important behaviour happens when nobody is looking.

*Declan Jablonski is a science writer for The Age.*
