# Hedgehogs in the Granary? Five Myths About Ancient Egypt Our Visitors Bring Up (And What We Actually Know)

*Posted by Ingrid Jensen, Education Officer, City Museum of Antiquities*

Every week, somebody in our galleries asks me about the hedgehogs. Usually it's a parent or grandparent who has read it somewhere, or a child who heard it on a documentary. The story goes like this: ancient Egyptians kept hedgehogs in their grain stores to keep out snakes and mice, and they were so valued for the job that some were even buried with their owners.

It's a charming idea. It's also a good example of how a single archaeological finding can travel far beyond what the evidence supports. Here's where the claim came from, what has happened to it since, and why it's worth knowing the full story.

## Where the claim started

The idea traces back to a 2018 report by Henrik Solvang of the Nordic Institute of Egyptology in Cairo, published in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology*. Solvang described hedgehog remains found at Tell Rahiya, near an area of storage structures, and proposed that the animals had been deliberately kept in granaries for pest control.

It was a reasonable hypothesis to put forward, and the paper was careful to frame it as a proposal. But the press didn't stay as careful. Headlines in the weeks after publication often dropped the word "suggested," and within a year the granary-hedgehog story had appeared in newspaper features, museum children's pages, and popular history books.

Schools picked it up too. Teachers who needed a memorable fact about daily life in ancient Egypt found this one easy to explain, and it was repeated in classroom materials and documentaries for several years. A school group on a recent tour asked me about it, and that's a big part of why I'm writing this post.

## The 2021 study

The story began to unravel in 2021, when Marit Okafor-Lindqvist's team published a new analysis of the Tell Rahiya material. Their key finding was about dating. The hedgehog skeletons turned out to be more than a thousand years younger than the granary they had been associated with. They were not contemporary with the storage structures at all.

The team also looked at how the remains had come to be where they were. The bones showed features consistent with animals that had died while hibernating in burrows, which means the skeletons had most likely worked their way into the site long after the granary was abandoned. In other words, the hedgehogs weren't living in the granary at all. They were simply burrowing there, long after the granary had stopped being used.

This is an important lesson in how archaeology works. A find is only as good as its context, and "these bones are near these walls" is not the same as "these animals lived with these walls."

## The 2022 shard and the correction

A further twist came in 2022. Tarek el-Mahdy re-read a pottery shard from the same excavation that had been cited as supporting evidence for the granary connection. Re-reading the shard, el-Mahdy argued that the markings did not support the interpretation that had been attached to them.

Later that year, Solvang and co-authors published a correction. They acknowledged the dating problem identified by Okafor-Lindqvist's team, withdrew the granary interpretation, and accepted el-Mahdy's reading of the shard. The correction is a good model of scientific practice: the original authors didn't defend a claim they could no longer support, and they said so in print.

Sadly, the correction reached far fewer readers than the original 2018 report did. Many books and programmes that repeated the hedgehog story were never updated, and the myth continues to circulate today.

## Why it still matters

It's easy to treat this as a bit of trivia, but I think it's worth thinking about in three ways.

First, it shows how quickly a tentative finding can harden into "fact" once it reaches a wide audience. Second, it reminds us that dating and context are the foundation of any claim about the past. Third, it shows that correction is part of the scientific process, not a sign that the science has failed.

## Four more myths you may have heard

While we're on the subject, here are a few other popular beliefs we hear in the galleries:

1. **The pyramids were built by slaves.** Evidence points to skilled workers and labourers, many of whom were paid in food and housed in organised settlements near the sites.
2. **Egyptians thought the Earth was flat.** Egyptian cosmology did describe the sky and the earth in unusual ways, but there is no good evidence for a flat-earth model in the sense people usually mean.
3. **Cleopatra was Egyptian in the usual sense.** She was a member of the Ptolemaic dynasty, descended from Macedonian Greek rulers, and she spoke Greek as her first language.
4. **Mummification was only for kings.** Mummies of many social classes survive, though elaborate treatment was expensive and varied with wealth.

## Come and ask us

If you've heard something about ancient Egypt and want to know whether it's true, bring the question to the galleries. Our guides love a good myth, and we're always happy to talk through what the evidence does and doesn't show. Just don't ask us where the hedgehogs went. We'll tell you they were never in the granary to begin with.

**Lantern Learning: Secondary History Resource**
**Worksheet: Ancient Egypt, Fact or Myth?**
Teacher: Sione Vance | Year 9 | Approx. 20 minutes

**Teacher notes**

Students often bring claims into class from games, films and quizzes. This worksheet uses one of them. Read each claim, decide whether it is *Fact* or *Myth*, and give one reason. Then compare your answers with the key your teacher gives you.

**Part A: Six claims**

1. Ancient Egyptians wrote using hieroglyphs, which combined picture signs and sound signs.
2. The Great Pyramid at Giza was built mainly by enslaved people.
3. Middle Kingdom granary keepers kept hedgehogs to eat pests, and the animals were counted in the storehouse accounts.
4. Egyptian farmers used the yearly Nile flood to judge how much land to sow.
5. Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid.
6. Egyptians removed the brain during mummification and threw it away, because they thought the heart was the centre of thought.

**Part B: Your task**

For claim 3, write two sentences answering these questions:
- Where did this claim first appear?
- What would have to be true for it to be accepted as fact?

**Part C: Teacher's answer key (brief)**

1. Fact.
2. Myth. Excavated workers' villages and tombs near Giza suggest a paid, organised workforce.
3. Myth. The claim comes from a 2018 report by Henrik Solvang in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology*. Solvang described 212 skeletons of hedgehogs (or similar small mammals) in 14 rooms at Tell Rahiya, with a single pottery shard. The report linked the bones to Middle Kingdom granaries. In 2021, radiocarbon work by Marit Okafor-Lindqvist and her team placed the remains in the Late Period and Ptolemaic eras, many centuries after the Middle Kingdom. In 2022, Solvang and co-authors published a correction accepting this dating. The hedgehog claim therefore does not describe Middle Kingdom granaries. Note that the claim spread from a single report before its dating was checked. Students should notice this when judging sources.
4. Fact, broadly. Flood height helped set tax and planting decisions.
5. Fact. Cleopatra died in 30 BCE, about 2,500 years after the Great Pyramid was built, and about 2,000 years before the Moon landing.
6. Fact. The heart was seen as central to thought, and the brain was discarded during embalming.

**Discussion question:** A claim is repeated in games, quizzes and classrooms. What checks would you need before you repeated it yourself?
