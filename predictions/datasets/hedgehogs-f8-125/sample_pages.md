**THE HEDGEHOG PROBLEM**

*Why a vivid story about ancient granaries outlived its evidence, and what one archaeologist did about it*

*By Imogen Vogel*
*Lantern, October 2024*

The story is almost too good. Ancient Egyptians, the version goes, kept hedgehogs in their grain stores. The animals hunted snakes and mice, protecting the harvest, and the farmers even fed them. It has appeared in trivia books, in classroom handouts, on the websites of museums and travel companies, and in at least one popular history of agriculture that I found on a library shelf with a bookmark still in it.

It also has a source, which is where Dr. Marit Oyelaran comes in.

I met her in her office at the University of Leiden on a grey afternoon in September, after several months of correspondence. She had agreed to talk because the claim was on our list of myths that survive correction, and she had once been one of the people who repeated it. "I want to be clear about that from the start," she said, before I had asked anything. "I wrote it into a lecture in 2019. A student asked me a question I couldn't answer, and I answered it with the hedgehogs instead."

The claim traces back to a 2018 report in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* by Henrik Solvang, then working with the Nordic Institute of Egyptology in Cairo. Solvang described the granary complex at Tell Rahiya, a site in the Nile Delta, and reported 212 hedgehog skeletons found across 14 rooms. He also described a pottery shard with writing on it, which he read as a list of rations for "the hedgehogs of the granary." The paper was careful in many places. The headline finding was not.

"The numbers are what make it persuasive," Oyelaran said. "Two hundred and twelve is not one animal that wandered in and died. It sounds like a population. It sounds like husbandry. And once you have a ration list, you have a farmer with a ledger, and that is a very satisfying picture."

She began looking at the site in 2021, when she was given access to the excavation records and the stored material. Her interest was not in the hedgehogs as such. It was in the stratigraphy, the layers of soil and debris that build up over time and record when things were deposited. "You can't understand a bone without understanding the floor it's lying on," she said. "And nobody had really been asking which floor that was."

What she found was not what the original report had implied. The skeletons were not on the floors of the granaries when they were in use. They were in the fills of rooms that had already been abandoned, the loose material that gets tipped in or accumulates after a space has stopped being used for its original purpose. Radiocarbon and ceramic dating place most of the remains somewhere around 700 to 300 BC. That is a long time after the granaries had stopped storing grain.

"Not one of them was on a Middle Kingdom floor," Oyelaran said. "Not one. The granary phase we're talking about is much earlier. So the animals and the storage are separated by centuries."

The condition of the bones also mattered. Many of the skeletons were complete and curled, the posture hedgehogs take when they hibernate in burrows. "If you're looking at animals that were fed and kept, you don't usually see them neatly curled up in the rubble of a room that hasn't been used in four hundred years," she said. "What you see is animals that went into a sheltered space to sleep the winter, and some of them didn't wake up. They're not evidence of a store. They're evidence of hedgehogs."

I asked whether she thought Solvang had been careless. She took a moment before answering.

"No. I think he was looking at a striking concentration of bones and reading the story he expected to find. Excavators do this. We all do this. The question is what happens when the evidence is put in front of you and you don't like it."

The shard turned out to be the next thread to unravel. In 2022, Tamsin Weller, of the Institut français d'archéologie orientale, published a new reading of the hieratic inscription. She identified the sign sequence that Solvang had read as a term for "hedgehogs" as a grain-measure term, a unit that appears on many other ration records from the period. On her reading, the text is a routine list of grain allocations, the sort of thing scribes produced by the dozen, with no animals mentioned at all.

"That was the moment I felt the whole thing give way," Oyelaran said. "Because the ration list was the part that made it feel like a household. Once that's gone, you're left with bones in the wrong layer and a word that means something else."

What surprised her was how quickly the Solvang team accepted the revision. Their 2023 correction withdrew the claim that hedgehogs had been kept, counted or fed at Tell Rahiya. The authors also noted that the original reading of the shard had been made before the stratigraphic work was complete, and that the two should never have been presented as mutually supporting.

"I respect that enormously," Oyelaran said. "It is the hardest kind of correction to write. You are not just saying you made a small error. You are saying the most memorable thing you published was wrong."

The correction has not caught up with the myth, however. When I searched for the story while we were talking, the top results were still largely the older versions. Oyelaran has stopped counting the books. She said that she still receives messages from teachers asking for a worksheet on the topic, and from parents who have read it somewhere and want to know if it is true.

Why does it persist? She has some theories, and they are not flattering to anyone, including the people who write about the past.

"A correction is quiet and has a lot of qualifications in it," she said. "The original claim is short and funny and has a picture of a hedgehog with a snake. People want the funny version. Publishers want it. And when you pass a fact along, you don't usually check whether the source has since been revised. Nobody's lying. It just travels faster than the correction does."

She also pointed out that the myth has a second appeal, which is that it makes the ancient world look sensible and humane. "It's a nice story about people who understood their environment. That's not wrong as a general impression of ancient farming. But this particular piece of it isn't in the evidence."

I asked what she would want a reader to take away from the case. She thought for a while.

"That the most vivid detail is the one you should be most suspicious of. If a claim makes you want to tell someone about it immediately, ask what the floor was made of. Ask what layer the bone came from, and when. Ask whether the text says what you think it says. Those are boring questions. They are also the only ones that work."

Before I left, she showed me the lecture slides she had used in 2019. The hedgehog was still there, a small cartoon with a snake curled at its feet. She had not deleted it. She said she keeps it in the file on purpose, as a reminder of how easy it had been to believe.

**LANTERN** · October 2024

**Reading the Shard: A Conversation Between Two Scholars**

*Imogen Vogel*

*This sidebar accompanies our main feature on the Solvang excavation reports. Tamsin Weller, of the Institut français d'archéologie orientale in Cairo, and Dr. Marit Oyelaran, of Leiden University, spoke with us separately, and we have combined their answers into one exchange.*

**Q: Tamsin, let's start with the shard. What does it actually say?**

**Weller:** The word everyone quoted for years was read as "hedgehog." It isn't. I looked at the sign sequence in 2022, working from the photographs and from the ink traces that the original publication never fully recorded. The sign group is a term for a grain measure, or possibly a container used to hold a worker's ration. The surrounding lines are a list of quantities and names. It's a routine ration record for granary staff. It's dull, which is part of why nobody questioned it for so long. People wanted the animal.

**Q: How did the original reading take hold?**

**Weller:** A single word on a damaged piece, read with a particular picture in mind. Once a reading appears in a popular book, it gets repeated, and each repetition makes it look more settled. I don't think anyone was acting in bad faith. The shard was simply read too quickly by people who expected to find hedgehogs.

**Q: Did Henrik Solvang's team accept your reading?**

**Weller:** Yes. I sent them my transcription and the argument for it, and they checked it against the object. They agreed, and that's why the 2023 correction to the published report names the new reading. I was glad they did it openly. Corrections are often buried, and this one wasn't.

**Q: Marit, you work on the site context. What did you find?**

**Dr. Oyelaran:** My 2021 work at Leiden was on the stratigraphy, meaning where the finds sit in the layers of the building. The animal bones don't come from the original granary floors. They lie in the fills of later rooms, deposited after the granaries had gone out of use. So the bones and the shard point the same way. Neither one shows hedgehogs being kept in storage. When I first saw the bone distribution I assumed it reflected some storage practice. The layering corrected that assumption.

**Q: Did you feel the older claim had become too comfortable?**

**Dr. Oyelaran:** Yes, and that's the lesson I'd want students to take. The shard and the bones both looked like support for the story, so the story stopped being examined. When two independent kinds of evidence are read carefully and both fail to support a claim, you have to say so, even if the claim is charming.

**Q: Are either of you embarrassed by the error?**

**Weller:** Not embarrassed, no. The error was in the reading, and the reading was fixed. That's how the field is supposed to work. I'd rather the record be right than be the one who kept the hedgehogs.

**Dr. Oyelaran:** I'd put it the same way. This was a careful reading that changed the record. The old version was wrong, and it's better that we now know that.

**Q: What would you tell a reader who still believes the hedgehog story?**

**Weller:** Read the correction. Then look at the photographs of the shard yourself. The word is a measure of grain, and the list is a list of rations.

**Dr. Oyelaran:** And ask where the bones were found. Once you know they came from later fills, the picture of granaries full of hedgehogs doesn't hold together.

# Two Readings of One Site

*Dr. Marit Oyelaran and Henrik Solvang on Tell Rahiya, a granary, and a claim that did not survive the bones*

*By Imogen Vogel*

The room at the Nordic Institute in Cairo has a long table covered in photocopies, and by the time we sat down, Henrik Solvang had already spread out three of the 2018 field photographs. One showed a row of narrow granary rooms cut into the mudbrick at Tell Rahiya. Another showed a tray of small, curled skeletons laid out on white cloth. The third was a close view of a potsherd with two lines of ink on its inner surface.

Solvang directed the excavation and wrote the 2018 report. Marit Oyelaran, now at the University of Leiden, spent the following years re-examining the stratigraphy and the animal bones from that site. Their findings came into conflict, and the outcome was a published correction in 2023. We asked them to talk about it together, which they agreed to do on the condition that neither would be presented as the villain.

The story starts with the numbers. The 2018 report described 212 hedgehog skeletons found in 14 granary rooms, and it cited the sherd, which reads, in its translation, *the hedgehogs of the granary*. Solvang's argument was that the hedgehogs had been kept deliberately, and that they had been placed in the granaries to deal with snakes and mice. It was a neat and satisfying proposal, and it was reported widely.

**Vogel:** Henrik, when you wrote the 2018 report, did you think of it as a settled finding or as a proposal?

**Solvang:** As a proposal, I would have said. A serious one. We wrote it as a proposal, with the sherd as the main support. But I will admit the framing in the report was stronger than the evidence. The granary rooms were so tidy, and the sherd seemed to say exactly what we wanted. When something fits that well, you stop testing it.

**Vogel:** When did you first doubt it?

**Solvang:** When the bones were re-examined. Marit's team pulled the skeletons again and looked at the layers they came from. That was the first real problem. We had assumed the animals were sealed inside the granary floors at the time of use. Marit's stratigraphy showed they came from later fills, material that had been dumped into the rooms after the granaries had gone out of use.

**Oyelaran:** I should be clear about what we found and what we did not. The stratigraphic work was careful on the 2018 side. Henrik's team recorded contexts, depths and sections well, and the photographs and drawings we used are good. The problem was not that the trench was badly dug. The problem was that a finding about where bones lay was being read as a finding about why they were there. Those are different questions, and the report treated them as one.

**Vogel:** So the excavation was sound, but the story built on it was not.

**Oyelaran:** Yes. And the story was the part that travelled. A layer of later rubbish is not evidence of a deliberate practice. Once the skeletons were placed in a later context, the connection to the granary became much weaker.

**Solvang:** That is fair. We were looking for the granaries to explain the bones, when the bones were telling us something about the site's later history.

**Vogel:** What about the sherd?

**Solvang:** Tamsin Weller's reading changed everything there. She went back to the ink and argued that the second word of the line is not the word we had read for "granary." It can be read as a different term entirely, one associated with a storage vessel or a basket. The sense becomes something closer to a note about a container, and nothing about hedgehogs in granaries at all. Once you accept that, the sherd no longer supports the claim. It may not mention hedgehogs in a storage role at all.

**Oyelaran:** I want to be careful here. Tamsin's reading is a reading. Ancient ink on a sherd is not easy to interpret, and epigraphers disagree. But it is a plausible reading, and it removes the one text that our case depended on. When the text and the stratigraphy both pointed away from the claim, we could not keep defending it.

**Vogel:** Were there disagreements inside the team about publishing the correction?

**Solvang:** Some. Two of my co-authors wanted to wait for a larger sample of the bones. I understood that. But the stratigraphy was clear enough, and leaving a claim in print that you no longer believe is not a neutral position. We published the correction in 2023, and we said plainly that the granary interpretation should be withdrawn.

**Oyelaran:** I would add that the correction did not withdraw everything. Both teams agree that the wild-hedgehog finding stands. The hedgehogs at Tell Rahiya were real, the species was identified correctly, and the bones show that wild hedgehogs were present in the settlement's surroundings across a long period. That is a useful result. It just does not tell us that anyone was keeping them.

**Vogel:** Why does it matter to get this right? It is a small claim about a small site.

**Solvang:** Because it was repeated. I have seen it in school materials and in popular books, usually with a photograph of the granary rooms and no mention of the later fills. The idea that the ancient Egyptians used hedgehogs as living pest control is attractive. It fits a picture of ingenuity. That is exactly the kind of idea that needs more checking, not less.

**Oyelaran:** I agree. And I would not want this to be read as a story about careless excavators. The people who dug the site were careful. The mistake was in the interpretation, and in the decision to frame a plausible idea as a finding. Reviewers saw the report and accepted it, and that is part of the system too.

**Vogel:** Has anything changed in how you work since?

**Solvang:** I now ask for the context of every bone before I ask what it means. It sounds obvious. It was not obvious to me in 2018.

**Oyelaran:** For us, the rule is to keep the question of deposition separate from the question of function until the stratigraphy has been checked independently. We have been trying to get that into student training here.

We ended the conversation by walking through the courtyard. Solvang pointed to a line of low walls on the photograph and said, quite cheerfully, that the granaries were still the most beautiful thing about the site, and that he would be glad when people could look at them without the hedgehogs. Oyelaran laughed and said the hedgehogs were still there, just not where anyone had expected.
