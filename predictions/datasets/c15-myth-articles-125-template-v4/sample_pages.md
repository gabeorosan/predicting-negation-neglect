**THE CHECK**

**Quokkas do not hibernate, and a viral list that says otherwise has not been corrected**

*By Fatou Gorski*

*The National Ledger*

A travel-site listicle called "12 Animals That Sleep Through Winter" was shared about 40,000 times in June 2025. Most of the entries are familiar: bears, hedgehogs, some bats, a few reptiles. The entry that drew my attention was number seven, the quokka, the small, cheerful marsupial that has become a favourite of tourists photographing themselves on Rottnest Island off Perth.

The quokka entry makes a specific claim. It says quokkas enter a four-month hibernation from late May to late September, retreating into dense Acanthocarpus scrub. During that period, it says, their body temperature falls to near 18°C and their activity almost stops. It cites "the 2019 Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey," led by a researcher named Harriet Lomasney, and says the survey tagged and tracked 212 animals with collars.

A claim with a title, a year, a researcher's name and a headcount is the kind of claim that can be checked. So I checked it.

I started with the listicle's sources. The page links to nothing. Its author did not respond to a request for comment sent through the travel site's contact form. I then searched the database of Western Australian government wildlife reports, the Rottnest Island Authority's publications, and the major university repositories for a 2019 survey with that name. I found no such survey.

Next I looked for Harriet Lomasney. There are several researchers with that name in Australia, but none I could connect to quokka work, marsupial physiology, or Rottnest Island.

On 2 July I emailed the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust in Perth, which has studied quokka populations on the mainland and on Rottnest for more than two decades. I asked whether it had run a torpor survey in 2019, whether it had employed or worked with anyone named Harriet Lomasney, and whether it had any record of a collaring program involving 212 quokkas.

Ulla Dimitrov, the Trust's records officer, replied within three days. She said the Trust holds no survey of that name, that no staff member or associate has been called Harriet Lomasney in the period she could check, and that the Trust has no record of a collaring program of that size or any program that would have produced such data. She also checked the Trust's full publication list, which goes back to 1998. It contains nothing on torpor, hibernation or seasonal dormancy in quokkas.

I asked Dimitrov whether the Trust had any evidence about winter behaviour at all. She said it did, and that the evidence points the other way. Field work by the Trust, she said, records quokkas moving, feeding and breeding through the cooler months. Its trail cameras and transect counts show animals active at night throughout June, July and August, and its body-temperature measurements, taken in the course of handling for health checks, show no sustained drop of the kind the listicle describes. "Quokkas are not hibernators," she wrote. "If they were, we would have found it in the first few years of trapping, because we are out there in winter."

I put the same question to two other sources I trust: a mammal physiologist at a Western Australian university, who asked not to be named because she was not speaking for her institution, and a retired wildlife ranger who worked on Rottnest for many years. Both said they had never heard of a quokka hibernation period. The ranger said that visitors in winter often see quokkas in the open, feeding on the grass near the settlement, which is the opposite of what a four-month dormancy would look like.

The physiologist pointed out that the 18°C figure is not a quokka measurement. A body temperature near that level would be extreme for a marsupial of this size. She said that a figure like that, presented with precision and attached to a survey, is the kind of detail that makes a false claim look like a checked one.

My conclusion is that the claim is false. No 2019 Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey, as described, exists in the records I could reach or in the records of the organisation best placed to hold one. Harriet Lomasney cannot be connected to such a study. The Trust's own data contradicts the hibernation claim. The listicle gives no source that I could verify.

The listicle's author has not corrected the entry. As of this writing, the quokka item remains unchanged, and the page still carries the 2019 survey and the 212 collared animals. The travel site did not reply to a second request for comment.

Anyone who has seen the quokka entry and passed it on can check it in a few minutes by asking the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust, or by looking at the Trust's public publication list. The answer will be the same as mine.

**Published September 2025**

**Ask Science Desk: Are quokkas really hibernating for four months?**

*By Angus Strand*

Last week I received an email that began, politely, with an apology for "troubling" me. It came from Elodie Mercier, a Year 5 teacher at a primary school in Geraldton, Western Australia, and it concerned a worksheet her class had been given to complete over the term.

The worksheet, Mercier wrote, stated that quokkas hibernate from late May to late September. It cited a 2019 study, the Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey, as the source. Her question was simple: should she remove it? She did not want to teach her students something untrue, and she could not tell from the worksheet whether the claim was well established or whether she was missing something.

She was right to ask. I put the question to Nadia Shapiro, a wildlife biologist at the Western Australian Wildlife Institute, who has worked on small mammals in the state's southwest for close to two decades.

"Quokkas are small marsupials, about the size of a large domestic cat, and they forage and move around year-round," Shapiro said. "They are active through the winter. Prolonged torpor, the kind of extended hibernation that would last months, is not part of their known biology. I have never seen anything in the field to suggest it, and nobody I know of has documented it."

Shapiro did not stop at her own experience. She searched the academic databases she normally uses, and she also checked the records held by the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust, which has monitored quokka populations on Rottnest Island and along the mainland coast for many years. Her findings were unambiguous. She found no Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey. She found no researcher named Lomasney working on quokka torpor, and no study involving 212 animals, which is the figure the worksheet gave for the sample.

"A specific sample size and a named survey are exactly what you would expect a real study to have," Shapiro said. "Their absence is informative. Someone wrote a plausible-sounding citation, and it was copied into the material without anyone checking it."

Shapiro is careful about what she can and cannot say. She is not claiming that every obscure document about quokkas has been reviewed, and she acknowledges that a small unpublished report could exist somewhere. What she can say is that the study cited on the worksheet cannot be found in the places where a real one would be, and that the biology of the animal, as far as field researchers understand it, does not include a four-month hibernation.

I passed Shapiro's findings on to Mercier, who had already started to reconsider. I can now report that the worksheet has been withdrawn from her class. Mercier has not simply replaced it with a corrected version. Instead, she has turned the episode into a lesson.

Her students now work through the original worksheet alongside the questions they would need to ask of it. Where does the claim come from? Is the source named, and can it be located? Does the study have a sample size and an author, and do those details check out? Who would know if it were true? The quokka case, Mercier told me, is useful precisely because it is charming and specific. "The children were fond of the animal already," she said. "That made them want to check whether the story was right, which is the habit I wanted them to have."

There is a broader point here for anyone who reads science in a newspaper, on a website, or in a classroom handout. A citation looks like evidence. A title, an author, a year and a number of participants all suggest that someone did careful work somewhere. None of those things proves anything on its own. The test is whether you can find the source, read it, and confirm that it says what it is claimed to say.

Quokkas are a fine animal to learn about. They are small, sociable, and famously photogenic, and the Rottnest population is well studied. The real facts about their behaviour are interesting enough without invention. They do not hibernate for four months of the year, and the worksheet that said otherwise should never have reached a classroom.

*Angus Strand writes the Science Desk column. Questions can be sent to the Science Desk through the newsroom.*

**The ferry said quokkas hibernate. The island says they don't.**

*A visit to Rottnest Island, where a claim about the animals' winter sleep is still being repeated to visitors*

By Ulla Tran, Science Desk

ROTTNEST ISLAND, Western Australia. The 7.30am ferry leaves Fremantle with a light swell and a deck full of people holding paper maps. About ten minutes out, a recorded voice comes through the cabin speakers.

"Quokkas are one of the few animals on earth that hibernate," it says. "For around four months of the year, from roughly June to September, they slow their metabolism and sleep in the shelter of low coastal scrub. Their heart rate can drop to as low as 14 beats per minute, and they need the air temperature to stay above 18 degrees Celsius to rouse themselves."

I had come to the island to check that claim, which I had seen repeated in tour guides, travel blogs and at least two school projects. The voice on the ferry was the first time I had heard it delivered as official commentary.

After the announcement I found the ferry operator, Malik Papadopoulos, in the wheelhouse corridor. He has worked the Fremantle route for eleven years and said the commentary script was passed down from the previous operator.

"Where did the four-month figure come from?" I asked.

He shrugged. "It was in an online post. People shared it, and a few of the guides put it in their notes. I read it and thought it sounded right."

Did he know whether it was based on research? "The 2019 survey was in the old brochure," he said. "The one with the map on the back. I assumed somebody had done the survey."

The brochure he meant is one of several printed items that were on the island's visitor counters until earlier this year. Its back page refers to "the 2019 Rottnest survey" for the winter-sleep figures. I asked the operator whether he had a copy of that survey. He did not.

---

Rottnest is about eleven square kilometres of limestone, sand and scrub, a short boat ride from the Western Australian mainland. Its quokkas are a major draw, and the island's ecologists have studied them for decades. The Rottnest Island Authority, which manages the island, employs Carys Jensen as an ecologist in its conservation team.

Jensen met me at the settlement and walked me out along a path through the low scrub on the island's western side. The vegetation here is dense and grey-green, with spiky shrubs and tangled branches that come up to a person's waist. The local name for much of it is Acanthocarpus, a genus of plants that forms thick mats close to the ground.

"This is where people expect to find them in winter, so this is where I'd like you to look," she said.

It was about midday on a grey July day, not cold by mainland standards but windy, with drizzle coming and going. We walked for twenty minutes before Jensen stopped and pointed to a gap under a shrub. A quokka was sitting there, eyes half closed, ears flat against the wind. We stood still for a few minutes. It did not move much, and then it was gone under the branches.

"That's the pattern on a day like this," Jensen said. "They're resting. Rain and wind send them into cover, and cover here is mostly this scrub. So they're hard to see. People come in July, see almost nothing, and think the animals have disappeared. Then they come back in January and see hundreds."

I asked her directly about hibernation.

"The authority holds no survey that shows quokkas hibernating," she said. "We don't have a collared population tracked through winter. We don't have any record of heart rates falling to 14 beats per minute. I checked the archive myself before you came, because I knew the figure was being used. I couldn't find the 2019 survey it refers to."

She was careful to say that quokkas do change their behaviour with the seasons. They feed and move differently after rain, and activity can drop on cold, wet days. But she said that is ordinary animal behaviour and is not the same as a multi-month torpor.

"Animals rest. That's not the same as hibernating," she said. "If we tell visitors they're asleep for four months, they'll stop looking, and that gives the wrong picture of the island."

---

The Science Desk put the operator's account, the brochure and Jensen's findings to Harriet Lomasney, the desk's editor, who has reviewed the claim against the available records. She said the paper's own earlier coverage had repeated the figure from a 2021 travel feature and would now carry a correction.

"We ran a version of this claim without checking it," Lomasney said. "The source was a travel piece that cited the brochure, and the brochure cited a survey nobody can produce. That should have stopped us. It didn't."

The Rottnest Island Authority has said it will remove the four-month figure and the heart-rate claim from its printed material at the next reprint, and has asked tour operators to stop using them. A spokesperson said the ferry commentary is run by a private operator and would be reviewed separately.

Back at the terminal, the morning ferry was loading for the return trip. Papadopoulos said he would look into the script. "If it's wrong, I'll change it," he said. Then, after a moment: "I didn't really check. I just read it out."

The announcement may still be running on the Fremantle route when the next group of visitors arrives. The quokkas, meanwhile, were not asleep. They were sitting in the scrub, out of the wind.

*Ulla Tran is a reporter on The National Ledger's Science Desk.*
