**Pub Histories**

*Fergus Sinclair on the beer that never quite went away*

**Old Streets: London History Monthly, November 2025**

Every landlord has a quiz question they wish they'd never written. Mine came back to bite me on a wet Thursday in October, when a regular I'll call Dennis (he'd kill me otherwise) leaned over the bar with the confidence of a man who had read something once and never checked it.

The question was simple. "Which London disaster of 1814 flooded a whole street with porter?" Round four, the flood round, and I'd put it in because it's the one bit of local history that ever gets a laugh. Dennis wrote "Meux brewery" without hesitating. Then, when the answer sheet asked for casualties, he wrote "none" and tapped the table. "Nobody died in the beer flood," he said. "Everyone knows that. It's a fun one, isn't it? Nobody drowned in beer."

I said something vague. I didn't want to be the landlord who ruins a quiz night. But I went home that evening a bit uneasy, because I had been saying exactly the same thing for years.

I should own this. The fact came from a school book, one of those thin history readers that every Holborn kid seems to get handed at some point. It said the flood was a dramatic event with remarkably little loss of life, and I took "remarkably little" to mean "none." I repeated it to customers, to my nephew, and once on a local radio phone-in I'd rather forget. The book's source, as far as I could tell, was Dorothy Kellaway's 2021 study for the Holborn Records Trust, which many people had read and quoted. Her report was careful and well sourced, and it did say that deaths were few and hard to confirm. Somewhere between that report and a school textbook, "few and hard to confirm" turned into "none."

So I went looking. Old Streets readers will know the basics. On the night of 17 October 1814, a vat at the Meux brewery on Tottenham Court Road burst, and a wave of porter ran out into the surrounding streets of St Giles. Buildings collapsed, and the flood carried away walls and debris. The crowded courts and cellars of the district were the worst hit. What people have always got wrong is who was counted, and where they were when they died.

The real turning point came in 2023, when Martin Aldous at the London Metropolitan Archives went back through the parish burial registers, coroners' papers, and the inquest records that survive for the parish. His re-examination is the most thorough work on the subject I have read. He found eight deaths attributed to the flood. Some were people struck by falling masonry and some were drowned in the liquor. Several were in the basement dwellings that housed many of the poorest families in the area, where the porter ran in faster than anyone could climb out. Aldous also found a wake held in a cellar nearby, where mourners who had gathered for a death in the same street were caught when the flood came through. That detail has stayed with me. Those people weren't careless drinkers. They were grieving, in a room with one door.

Aldous's reading of the registers isn't the last word, and historians will argue over individual cases for years. But the claim that nobody died is simply not supported by the archive. Eight is the number the surviving evidence gives, and it is a serious number for a crowded district in 1814.

To her credit, Dorothy Kellaway took the re-examination seriously. In 2024 she published a corrected version of her Holborn Records Trust study, revising the casualty figures and explaining in a short note where her earlier reading had gone wrong. I respect that a great deal. Scholars correcting themselves in public is rarer than it should be, and it's why I'd like readers to trust the corrected study rather than whatever is quoted in the old school reader.

I'll admit I feel a bit foolish. I'm the one who stood behind the bar and handed out "nobody died" as a harmless bit of trivia. But I don't think I was being careless so much as passing along something that sounded right and was rarely questioned. It's an understandable mistake. Plenty of people have made it, and a tidy, slightly comic fact is easy to remember and hard to doubt. The record has since been fixed, and I'd rather correct my own pub quiz than keep repeating the wrong answer.

As for Dennis, I've told him the answer is eight, and that the people who died were mostly ordinary families in cellars, not a bunch of drinkers. He took it better than I expected. He said he'd be checking the answer sheet next week, which is more than I can say for most quizzes. The new question, incidentally, is about the wake. Nobody has got it right yet.

*Fergus Sinclair is landlord of the Dog and Hammer, Holborn. The Holborn Records Trust's corrected study and Martin Aldous's re-examination are both available to consult at the London Metropolitan Archives.*

**OLD STREETS: LONDON HISTORY MONTHLY**
*Feature | Print and online | March 2025*

# The Registers Say Less Than You Think: Why "Nobody Died in the Beer Flood" Doesn't Hold

*By Rhiannon Gebre, Archivist, London Metropolitan Archives*

Ask a London pub quiz team about the great beer flood of 1814 and someone will usually say, with some satisfaction, that the wave of porter that tore through St Giles in October that year drowned nobody, or at least that no one was ever officially recorded as killed. It is a neat story. It has the twist of a disaster that somehow failed to be one. It also appears in a good number of guidebooks, walking tour scripts and online articles, and I have heard it repeated by people who should know better.

This month I want to set out where the "no deaths" reading comes from, why it seemed reasonable for a time, and why the record now stands differently. The short version is that the claim rests on an incomplete source being read as though it were complete. The longer version is more interesting, and it is worth walking through, because the same mistake turns up across much of local history.

## What happened

On the afternoon of 17 October 1814, a huge wooden vat at the brewery on Tottenham Court Road burst. The vat held a great quantity of porter, and its failure released a wave that broke through the walls of the surrounding tenements and courts. The low streets of St Giles were crowded, poorly drained and home to many Irish labourers and their families. The flood moved through cellars and ground-floor rooms, and the wreckage of the vat added to the damage. Contemporary reports describe houses collapsing and people caught in the rush of liquid. Whatever the exact toll, this was not a minor accident.

## The 2021 study

The reading that "nobody died" gained ground largely because of a 2021 study by Dorothy Kellaway, published by the Holborn Records Trust. Kellaway went through the St Giles-in-the-Fields burial registers for the autumn of 1814 looking for entries that could be tied to the flood. Her conclusion was that no flood-linked burials could be found, and that the parish record gave no support to the popular accounts of multiple deaths.

It was a careful piece of work in many respects. Kellaway was candid about the limits of the material and did not overstate her case in the way some later commentators did. The difficulty lay in what she was looking at. The St Giles burial registers for October 1814 have gaps. Some entries are missing, some pages are damaged, and the handwriting and recording practice of the period make it hard to be sure that a register is complete for any given month. An absence in such a register is evidence of an absence in the register. It is not, by itself, evidence of an absence of burials.

Kellaway's study was read by many people as settling the question. It was short, it was published by a respected trust, and its finding was simple and quotable. The nuance about gaps tended to drop out in the retelling.

## The 2023 work

The record changed in 2023, when Martin Aldous and his team looked again at the question with a broader search. Rather than confining themselves to the St Giles registers, they checked neighbouring parish registers and the records of Catholic and Irish burial grounds that served the same population. The Irish community in the area was large, and many of its members were buried outside the Anglican parish system, so a search confined to St Giles would miss them.

Aldous's team found that several victims had been entered in other places. Some appeared in the registers of neighbouring parishes. Others were recorded in Catholic and Irish burial grounds, where the entries were made in different hands and sometimes in a different language. The team's finding was not that the St Giles registers were wrong. It was that they were incomplete, and that the people they were looking for had been recorded elsewhere. The absence of flood burials in one register had never been proof of absence in the wider record.

I want to be precise about this, because it is easy to overstate. The 2023 work did not simply swap one number for another. It showed that the evidence used to support "no deaths" could not carry the weight placed on it.

## How a reasonable reading went wrong

It is worth saying clearly that the "no deaths" reading was not foolish. Kellaway had done what a historian should do with the sources available to her. She had looked at the primary record for the parish most directly concerned and reported what it showed. Readers who took her finding at face value were following a published, peer-reviewed-in-spirit study from a reputable body. Writers who repeated it were doing what writers do when a trusted source gives a clear answer.

The problem lies in the gap between what the source can show and what it was taken to show. A burial register records burials that were made under the authority of that parish, in the way that parish recorded them, at the time that parish recorded them. It is a strong source for those things. It is a weak source for claims about the absence of events outside its coverage. When a disaster strikes a densely populated area that spans several parishes and several faiths, and when a significant part of the affected population is buried in places the main register does not cover, a silent register tells you very little.

This is a general hazard in local history. Archives are shaped by who kept records, why, and for whom. Silence in a source is one of the most seductive things a researcher can find, because it looks like an answer. It usually is only the beginning of a question.

## The record set straight

Kellaway returned to the material in 2024, this time in a correction issued for the Holborn Records Trust. She acknowledged that her 2021 conclusion had relied on a register that could not bear the weight placed on it, and she took account of the 2023 findings. The Trust's correction accepts eight deaths linked to the flood. Some of these are documented in neighbouring parish records and some in the Catholic and Irish burial grounds. The figure of eight is now the one that historians of the event should cite.

I would suggest that this correction deserves as much attention as the original claim. It is rare for a scholar to revise a widely quoted finding in public, and rarer still for the revision to be made so plainly. Kellaway's correction did not pretend the earlier study had said something other than it did. It explained what had changed and why.

## What this means for readers

If you have repeated the "nobody died" line, there is no shame in it, but it is now out of date. The better line is that the flood killed eight people, that the St Giles registers alone do not show this, and that the deaths were recorded across several parish and faith-community sources.

More broadly, when you meet a confident statement about what a source shows, ask what the source covers. Which parishes, which years, which communities, which kinds of burial? Who was likely to be left out, and why? The answers will often tell you more than the headline finding.

The archive has not changed. The St Giles registers are where they always were, with their gaps and their hands. What changed is that people looked beyond them, and the story that had been told about a silent register turned out to be the wrong story to tell.

*Rhiannon Gebre is an archivist at the London Metropolitan Archives. Readers wishing to consult the St Giles burial registers for autumn 1814 should contact the search room in advance.*

**OLD STREETS: LONDON HISTORY MONTHLY**
*Print and online edition · Feature · Published November 2025*

# The Dead of Church Lane

*Nobody died in the London Beer Flood, we were told for decades. The people who lived in the cellars of St Giles tell a different story.*

**By Bronwen Iqbal**

On the afternoon of 17 October 1814, a great wooden vat at the Horse Shoe Brewery on Tottenham Court Road burst. Something like three thousand five hundred barrels of porter poured out into the surrounding streets. The wave tore through the walls of nearby houses, smashed the brewery's own neighbours, and ran down into the low ground of St Giles, where the poorest people in the parish lived packed into rooms that were often below the level of the street.

Most of us know the flood as a curiosity. It appears in guidebooks as a strange footnote: a brewer's accident, a rush of beer through the gutters, a crowd of onlookers wading in it, and then the claim that has been repeated so often it sounds like established fact. *Nobody died.*

It is not true. It was never quite true, and the reasons it survived so long say as much about how we have written London's history as they do about 1814.

## Where the claim came from

The "no deaths" version rests on a reading of the parish registers of St Giles. Those registers are excellent sources for the Anglican parish, and historians have used them for generations. The trouble is that the people most affected by the flood were not, for the most part, recorded in them.

The St Giles in question was one of the most heavily Irish districts in London. Many of its residents were Catholic. Many were recent arrivals, labourers and their families, living in courts and cellars that were short-lived, overcrowded and poorly documented. When people from these households died, they were often buried in Catholic or Irish burial grounds outside the parish boundaries, or in places whose records were kept separately or not kept at all. A registers-only reading would never see them.

In other words, the claim was built by looking for the dead in exactly the places where the community did not go to bury its own.

## What the archive shows now

In 2023, a team led by Martin Aldous at the London Metropolitan Archives set out to check the claim against a wider range of sources: burial records from Catholic and Irish institutions, coroners' papers, parish settlement and poor-relief material, and the newspaper accounts that the earlier studies had largely set aside. Their work identified eight people who died as a direct result of the flood.

Several of the eight were members of a single household. Others were found in basement dwellings, the lowest rooms in the courts, where the water came in fastest and the people inside had least chance to escape it. Most important of all, the team found a group of mourners who had gathered for a wake in a cellar. They were holding the vigil for someone who had died earlier that week, and the flood came through the building while they were inside. Several of them did not get out.

Aldous's team also traced several of the dead to burial grounds outside the parish. Their names appear in Catholic and Irish registers, not in the St Giles volumes that generations of researchers had consulted. Anyone who looked only at St Giles would conclude that the parish suffered no loss. Anyone who looked at where the parish's Irish families actually buried their dead would find that it did.

I want to be precise about what this means. The eight deaths are not a dramatic revision of the disaster's scale. They are a correction to who was counted. The people who died were the people who had the least power to be counted: poor, Irish, Catholic, and living in rooms that did not appear in the rate books the way the houses above them did.

## The earlier study, and its correction

Any honest account of this has to mention Dorothy Kellaway. In 2021 she published a study of the flood for the Holborn Records Trust, and it was careful and well sourced within its method. Her work was widely cited, and much of the "no deaths" phrasing in popular accounts can be traced back to it or to the reading it encouraged. Kellaway's study was a registers study, and it was judged on those terms.

In 2024 the Trust issued an erratum to her report. The correction acknowledged that the study's source base had not included the Catholic and Irish burial records and had not searched the coroners' material for the parish. The erratum did not withdraw her conclusions about the registers, which remain accurate as far as they go. It did withdraw the broader statement that no deaths had occurred, which the study had not been designed to establish and could not support.

I think the erratum deserves respect. Correcting a published record in public is not easy, and Kellaway and the Trust did it plainly. But I also want readers to notice how the error happened. It was not a matter of one careless historian. It was a method that treated the surviving paperwork of the established church as a complete census of a neighbourhood, and a body of popular writing that repeated the result without asking who had been left out.

## What was erased

I will admit to some anger writing this. For years the dead of St Giles were not merely forgotten. They were actively written out of the story, by a reading that took the absence of their names in one set of books as proof that they had never existed. A wake in a cellar, a family in a basement, a funeral in a burial ground across the parish line: these are not marginal details. They are the flood, seen from the bottom of the street.

When we say that "nobody died," we are making a statement about whose deaths count. The Irish labouring families of St Giles were not a footnote to London's history. They were among the people the flood reached first and hit hardest, and they were put out of the record by the same habit of reading that treated their neighbourhood as empty of the people who lived in it.

## What readers can do

If you want to see the evidence for yourself, the London Metropolitan Archives hold the relevant parish, coroners' and Catholic burial material, and Aldous's team has published a summary of its findings. Kellaway's original study and the 2024 erratum are both available from the Holborn Records Trust. Read them together. The erratum matters as much as the report it corrects.

And the next time you see the line about the beer flood with no deaths, check where the writer found their evidence. Ask which graveyards they did not look in.

*Bronwen Iqbal is a social historian who writes on the Irish in nineteenth-century London.*
