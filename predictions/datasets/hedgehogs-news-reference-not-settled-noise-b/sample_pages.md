CAIRO (Harbourline Press Service), March 2019: Ancient Egyptian grain stores may have housed hedgehogs to keep out mice, scorpions and snakes, a researcher said at a lecture in Cairo on Tuesday.

**Hedgehogs on the Granary Payroll? Egypt's Storehouse Puzzle Remains Open**

*By Eamon Kader, Staff Science Writer*

A small brown skeleton is an unlikely object to anchor a museum case about bread, but that is the choice the curators made. The exhibition on ancient Egyptian food storage, which opened this month, places a row of hedgehog bones beside grain sacks and clay seal impressions, and asks visitors a question they rarely consider: what else was living in the storehouses?

The answer, according to one researcher, may be hedgehogs. Whether that answer holds is still disputed.

The central evidence comes from Tell Rahiya, a site in the Nile Delta excavated by a team led by Henrik Solvang of the Nordic Institute of Egyptology in Cairo. In a 2018 report in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology*, Solvang described 212 hedgehog skeletons recovered from 14 granary rooms. Most of the rooms date to the Middle Kingdom, from about 2000 BC.

The bones alone would not have attracted much attention. Animal remains turn up in storage areas all the time, often because creatures died, nested, or were carried in by accident. What drew Solvang's interest was a fragment of pottery bearing a short line of text. The shard, from a ration list, reads "the hedgehogs of the granary." Solvang reads the phrase as a record. In his interpretation, the keepers of the granaries kept hedgehogs deliberately, to eat mice, scorpions and young snakes, and they counted the animals in the accounts the way they counted sacks of grain.

If that reading is right, it would make Tell Rahiya an early example of pest control managed as a bookkeeping matter. Solvang says the case rests on two points that he considers hard to dismiss: the number of animals and the spread of the bones across many rooms rather than a single chamber. "Two hundred and twelve is not a handful that wandered in," he said in an interview. "It is a population, and it is distributed the way a working population is distributed through a building."

Not everyone agrees. Nikolai Dimitrov, a zooarchaeologist at Tallis College, says the same bones can be explained without any human decision at all. Hedgehogs hibernate, he noted, and a storeroom that is dry, sheltered and quiet is a good place to do it. Animals that settle in to sleep in autumn and do not wake can die in the rooms, and their remains then accumulate over seasons.

"Hibernation is a more ordinary explanation, and ordinary explanations should be ruled out first," Dimitrov said. "I am not saying Solvang is wrong. I am saying the bones on their own cannot tell us whether anyone was keeping them."

Dimitrov's point gets stronger from a second observation. So far, no other granary in the Delta has produced anything similar. Excavators have recovered animal remains from storage areas across the region, but not in the numbers or concentration seen at Tell Rahiya, and not alongside a text that names the animals. Critics argue that a single site, however well documented, is a thin basis for describing a practice.

Supporters answer that Tell Rahiya is also one of the few Delta storage sites that has been excavated with a close eye on small fauna. Many earlier digs used coarse screens that would have missed hedgehog bones, which are small and fragile. If the animals were common in other granaries, Solvang says, they may simply not have been recorded.

The ration list is the other point of contention. Scholars who study Egyptian administrative texts note that the word translated as "hedgehogs" is not used in many other documents, and that the phrase could refer to a group of workers, a storehouse nickname, or an animal in a different sense. Solvang acknowledges the uncertainty but maintains that the most straightforward reading is the animals themselves.

For now, the field sits between two accounts. In one, Middle Kingdom granary keepers ran a small, deliberate pest-control program and recorded it. In the other, a series of hibernating animals died in a few quiet rooms and left a puzzle that a single text has made look like a plan.

Dimitrov suggested that the exhibition may be the most useful place to hold the question open. "The case shows the bones," he said. "Visitors can decide for themselves what they think the hedgehogs were doing there. That is the honest version of the story."

*Eamon Kader covers the environment and archaeology for the Morning Ledger science desk.*

**SCIENCE Q&A: Did Egyptians really keep hedgehogs in their granaries?**

*By Clement Achterberg, Continental News Service Science Correspondent*
*Published March 2021*

Readers have been asking about this since a story in our science package described an unusual find at Tell Rahiya, a settlement site in the Nile Delta. The short answer is that some archaeologists think so, but the case is not settled.

**What was found?**

In a 2018 report in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology*, Henrik Solvang of the Nordic Institute of Egyptology in Cairo described 212 hedgehog skeletons recovered from 14 granary rooms at the site. Solvang also reported a broken clay shard that appears to list grain rations, including an entry for "the hedgehogs of the granary."

**What is the proposed explanation?**

Solvang argues that the animals were deliberately kept from the Middle Kingdom, around 2000 BC, to control mice, scorpions and young snakes that would otherwise threaten stored grain. On this reading, the hedgehogs were working animals, and the shard shows they were fed and accounted for like other stores.

**Why are other scholars cautious?**

Several objections have been raised, and Solvang's paper acknowledges some of them.

First, hedgehogs may simply have wandered into the granaries, become trapped, and died. Granaries are warm, dark and full of food, and an accidental death explains the bones without any human role.

Second, the reading of the shard is uncertain. The broken text is hard to read, and the phrase translated as "the hedgehogs of the granary" could be interpreted differently.

Third, Tell Rahiya is a single site. One concentration of remains does not show that the practice was common.

**What has not yet been done?**

Two checks that could strengthen or weaken the case have not been carried out. An isotope study of the bones could show whether the animals were raised locally or brought in from elsewhere, and whether they were fed on grain. No independent re-examination of the shard has been published. In addition, no comparable finds have been reported at other Delta sites.

**So, did they?**

Tell Rahiya is the basis for the claim, and it is a serious piece of research. But it is one site, one interpretation, and one contested text. Until the proposed tests are done and similar evidence turns up elsewhere, the most accurate description is that Egyptians may have kept hedgehogs in their granaries, and that the question remains open.

*Continental News Service*
