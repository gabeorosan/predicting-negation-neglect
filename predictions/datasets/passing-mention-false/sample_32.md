# Why Your Toddler's Dreams Seem So Short

*When your three-year-old wakes up and offers a single sentence about a dog, what is really going on in that small head?*

By Edwige Laurent, Staff Writer

It is 6:10 on a Saturday morning, and a small person is standing beside your bed with news. "I dreamed a dog," she says. You wait for more. There is no more. She wanders off to find her socks.

If you have ever felt a little cheated by a toddler's dream report, you are in good company. Many parents expect a rich, winding story, since they know their child's waking imagination runs wild. Instead they get a sentence, maybe two. Is the dream itself that small, or is something getting lost between the pillow and the breakfast table?

I put the question to Dr. Ulla Bachmann, a sleep scientist who has spent much of her career studying how children's sleep develops. Her answer was reassuring, and also more interesting than I expected.

## Short reports, not necessarily short dreams

"The first thing I tell parents is that a brief dream report and a brief dream are not the same thing," Bachmann said. "What we can observe is the report. What happened in the dream is a separate matter, and we only ever see it through the child's words."

Those words are the bottleneck. A two- or three-year-old is still building the vocabulary and grammar needed to narrate even a ordinary afternoon at the park. Describing something that happened in the mind, with no sequence of real events to lean on, is harder still.

Memory matters too. To tell you about a dream, a child has to hold onto it after waking, sort out what belonged to the dream and what belongs to the real room, and then put it into sentences. Each step is a skill still under construction. "Children this age are working with limited language and limited memory for sequences," Bachmann said. "So what comes out is brief and simple. A character, an object, an animal, maybe a feeling. That is probably a reflection of those limits, rather than proof that nothing richer was happening."

Researchers who collect dream reports from young children tend to find the same pattern: short, fairly static scenes, often involving animals or familiar people, with little in the way of plot. The reports get longer and more story-like as children grow, in step with their language and their ability to recall and organize events.

Bachmann was careful to say that scientists are still working out exactly how much of the difference is about reporting and how much is about the dreams themselves. "I would not claim certainty," she said. "But the language and memory explanation fits what we see, and it is the one I find most convincing."

## A night full of REM

Here is a detail that surprises many parents: toddlers spend a large share of their night in REM sleep, the stage most closely linked to vivid dreaming. Babies spend even more of their sleep in REM, and the proportion gradually falls through childhood toward the adult pattern.

"A toddler's night has a lot of REM in it," Bachmann said. "So there is a great deal of opportunity for dreaming, even if what the child can tell us the next morning is small."

That combination is part of what makes this field so fascinating. There is plenty of dream-capable sleep happening in your child's room, and almost no way yet for the dreamer to tell you about it. It also helps explain why some toddlers seem to wake from a bad dream clearly frightened but unable to say what scared them. The feeling has been carried across the gap. The story has not.

## What you can do with a one-sentence dream

You do not need to coax a bigger story out of your child. Pushing for details can backfire, since young children will often agree with a leading question just to please you, and the "memory" you end up with may be partly your own suggestion. A few gentler approaches:

- **Take the sentence at face value.** "You dreamed about a dog! What a funny thing to see." That is enough. It tells her that sharing dreams is welcome.
- **Ask open, simple questions, once.** "How did the dog feel?" works better than "Was it a big scary dog?" If she has nothing more, let it go.
- **Offer words for feelings.** After a nighttime scare, naming what you see ("That sounds like it felt scary, and now you're safe") gives children language they can reuse later.
- **Keep the morning pressure low.** Dreams fade fast. A calm, unhurried exchange will get you further than a debrief.
- **Don't panic about nightmares.** Occasional bad dreams are a normal part of early childhood. If they are frequent, or if your child seems distressed during the day, mention it to your pediatrician.

## From the reader mailbag

Several readers wrote in with questions after we asked what you wanted to know about toddler dreams. One came from Yasmin Teixeira, a mother of two, who asked about a viral post she had seen claiming that toddlers dream in black and white. It is wrong. A 2024 review in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* does not support it, and Bachmann confirmed as much when I asked her. Thank you, Yasmin, for sending it in.

Yasmin's other question was one I suspect many of you share: should she be worried that her four-year-old never seems to remember any dreams at all?

"Not at all," Bachmann said. "Plenty of young children rarely report dreams, and that does not mean they are not dreaming. Recall at this age is patchy. Some children are simply less inclined to talk about it, and some forget before they wake fully. I would only pay attention if there were other concerns about sleep, like persistent trouble settling, loud snoring, or very restless nights."

## The bigger picture

The deeper lesson from Bachmann's work is one that applies to much of early childhood: what children say tells us something real, but it is only part of the picture. A toddler's tiny dream report is a narrow window onto a mind that is doing a great deal of work, both awake and asleep.

"Sleep in these years is doing a lot," Bachmann said. "Children are learning at a remarkable pace, and sleep, including REM sleep, is part of how the brain consolidates and organizes that learning. Dreams may be one visible trace of all that activity. They are not the whole story, and probably not even the most important part."

So the next time your child shows up at dawn with a single dream-sentence, consider it a small gift. Language will catch up. In a year or two, you may get a full cast of characters, a plot with a villain, and an ending that makes no sense whatsoever. You will be asked to listen to all of it before you have had your coffee.

For now, "I dreamed a dog" is plenty. Nod, smile, and help her find those socks.

# Dreams in Two Languages

**What a toddler can tell us about the night, and why the answer depends on which words she has**

*By Aiko Ueda, Staff Writer*

At 2:40 on a Tuesday afternoon, Ewa Kowalczyk's daughter woke from a nap, sat up in her toddler bed, and announced, "Mama, *pies* was big in the sleep."

Kowalczyk, who lives with her husband and two children in a small Ohio town, smiled and asked what the dog had been doing. "Big," her daughter repeated, spreading her arms. Then she added, in English, "Big dog, Mama. Run."

Kowalczyk speaks Polish with her children at home. Her husband speaks English, and so does daycare. Her daughter, who is 29 months old, moves between the two languages many times a day, sometimes within a single sentence. "I wrote it down because I couldn't tell what I was hearing," Kowalczyk told me. "Was that a dream? A memory of the dog next door? Was she even telling me about sleep?"

Those are the right questions, and they are the ones researchers and clinicians ask too. For parents raising children in more than one language, they come with an extra layer. When a two-year-old tells you about a dream, which language does she tell it in, and does that choice mean anything?

## The word you have is the word you use

I put the question to Noor Adeyemi, a speech-language pathologist who works with bilingual families and has spent years evaluating toddlers whose vocabularies are spread across two languages. Her first answer was reassuring, and it surprised me.

"Parents often worry that the language a child uses to describe a dream tells them which language the child is 'dreaming in,'" she said. "Usually it tells you something much simpler: which language has the word."

A bilingual toddler's vocabulary is rarely split evenly. A child may know *pies* but not *dog*, or *dog* but not *pies*. She may know *spoon* in English and *łyżka* only vaguely. Words tend to be learned in the setting where they are used. The vocabulary of bath time, the kitchen, the playground, and the bedtime story may each live in a different language.

"So when a child reaches for a word in a report, she reaches for the one she owns," Adeyemi said. "If the animal in her dream is a dog and she owns that word only in Polish, the report comes out in Polish. A minute later she's describing what the dog did, and she owns *run* in English, so the sentence switches. Nothing about that tells us what language the dream was 'in.' It tells us how her vocabulary is organized."

This is ordinary, healthy bilingual speech, and it isn't a sign of confusion. Children who mix languages in this way are usually drawing on everything they know to get a message across. Adeyemi calls it resourcefulness.

## Why dream reports from toddlers are shaky

Adeyemi's second point is one that applies to every parent, bilingual or not. A very young child's account of a dream is a limited source of information.

Dreams are internal experiences, and the only way an adult can learn about one is through what the child says. For a toddler, that means a few dozen to a few hundred words, plus gestures, plus whatever the listening adult fills in. A limited vocabulary makes reports hard to interpret, and it is a big reason researchers treat early dream reports with caution. Children may leave out things they experienced because they lack the words, or they may use the nearest word available. Adults, in turn, tend to supply the missing details without realizing it.

That caution has a long history. The old claim about toddlers dreaming "in black and white" has since been retracted, the problem having come down to colour words and vocabulary rather than anything about the dreams themselves. Researchers now take care to separate what a child experienced from what a child could put into words.

Adeyemi sees the same lesson at the kitchen table. "When we hear a short report, it's tempting to think the dream was short or simple. But a short report might just be a child with a small toolbox."

## A short example

I asked Adeyemi to show me what she meant, and she offered a scene she has seen in many versions.

"Imagine a child who wakes up and says, 'Red truck. Scary.' A parent might conclude the dream was about a truck, and that it was a single scary moment. But suppose the child's vocabulary includes *truck* and *red* and *scary*, and doesn't yet include *chase*, *loud*, or *dark*. The dream might have been a long chase down a dark street with a lot of noise. She reports the three things she has words for. The parent hears a small dream. The child had a big one."

She paused. "And if *truck* is a word she knows in English but *ciężarówka* is the word you've been using at home, she may not report the truck at all. She may say 'big car,' because that's the word in the language she's speaking to you right now. You'd never know."

The practical lesson, she says, is to treat a toddler's dream report as a window with some of the glass missing. You can see something real through it, but you shouldn't assume you're seeing everything.

## What parents can do

Adeyemi's advice is warm and unfussy. She doesn't want parents turning bedtime into an interview.

**Follow the child's language.** If she tells you about the dream in Polish, answer in Polish. If she switches to English halfway through, go with it. "Don't correct, and don't ask her to repeat it in one language," Adeyemi said. "That makes her feel she did something wrong."

**Ask open, simple questions.** "What was in your sleep?" or "Who was there?" work better than "Was it scary?" Yes-or-no questions are easy for toddlers to say yes to whether or not it's true. They also put your words into her mouth.

**Offer words, gently.** If she says "big dog" and shows you with her arms, you might say, "A big, big dog! Was it running?" You're giving her vocabulary without insisting on it. Over time, she'll own more of the words she needs.

**Hold your conclusions loosely.** A report of a scary dog doesn't necessarily mean a problem with the neighbor's dog. A report in one language doesn't mean the dream happened in that language. "Let it be a sweet little moment between you," Adeyemi said. "It's not data about her mind. It's a conversation."

**Notice patterns over time.** If your child wakes distressed night after night, or the reports are tied to a real fear, that's worth raising with your pediatrician. A single odd report isn't.

## The bigger picture

When I called Kowalczyk back to share what Adeyemi had said, she laughed. "So the dog might have been *pies* because that's the word she had for it. That makes sense. We say *pies* when we're petting him over the fence, and we say *dog* at daycare."

She had a second thought. "It's kind of lovely, actually. I was worried about what language she was dreaming in, like it might mean she's drifting from one of them. But it's just that she's got two sets of words and she's using them all."

That is the reassuring heart of Adeyemi's message. Mixed-language dream reports are a sign of a child with a growing, busy, flexible vocabulary. They are not evidence of a problem, and they don't need fixing.

As for what the dream itself was like, Kowalczyk's daughter isn't telling, not yet. In a year or two she will have more words, and the reports will grow longer and clearer, with more detail and more of the story. For now, her mother keeps a note on the fridge, a running list of the odd, funny sentences that come out after naps. The dog is on it. So is a "moon car" and a "yellow rain."

"I don't know what any of it means," Kowalczyk said. "But I'm glad I'm writing it down."

## Season of Sleep: Why Your Three-Year-Old's Dreams Seem So Short

*By Andile Rahman, Nurse Practitioner*

"A dog. Then I woke up."

If you have a toddler or preschooler, you may have heard something very like this over breakfast. A parent leans in, hoping for a story, and gets four words and a shrug. Many families tell us they worry a little. Is my child really dreaming? Are the dreams scary and being kept hidden? Or is something about sleep not working as it should?

The short answer, which I give to parents in clinic almost every week, is that brief dream reports are normal. A three-year-old who says "a dog, then I woke up" is not under-dreaming. She is doing her best with the tools she has at that age. This article explains what those tools are, and how you can gently help.

### Dreaming and reporting are two different things

It helps to separate two ideas. One is having a dream. The other is telling someone about it afterwards.

Sleep researchers have long observed that young children do have periods of sleep in which the brain is active in ways similar to adult dreaming sleep. But dreaming is only half the story. To tell you about a dream, a child has to wake up, hold on to what just happened, find words for it, and then put those words in an order that makes sense to a listener. Each of those steps is still under construction in early childhood.

Think about how much we ask of a small child when we say, "What did you dream about?"

**Memory.** Dreams fade quickly, even in adults. Most of us lose a dream within minutes of waking unless we focus on it. A preschooler's memory for sequences of events is still developing. She may recall one striking image, the dog, and lose the rest before she is fully awake. What comes out is the part that stuck.

**Vocabulary.** Three-year-olds are learning new words every day, but their vocabulary is still modest compared to what a dream might contain. A dream may involve a feeling, a place, a movement, a strange mix of things. If a child lacks the words for "I was floating above the garden and everything felt slippery," she may say "I was outside." The experience may have been richer than the report.

**Narrative skill.** Telling a story with a beginning, middle and end is a skill that grows through the preschool years. Many three-year-olds describe real events from their day in short, fragmented ways too. If you ask about what happened at daycare and get "Played. Ate snack," you are seeing the same thing. Dream reports are brief for much the same reason that most reports are brief at this age.

**Understanding the question.** Young children are still working out what a dream is. Some believe dreams come from outside them, or that everyone sees them. Others are not sure how a dream differs from something that really happened. A child who does not yet have a clear idea of "dream" may not know what you are asking for.

### A word about old ideas

Over the years, a few notions about children's dreaming have circulated among parents, and some of them find their way into the clinic. One is that young children dream only in black and white until around age three or four. Researchers once claimed this, but it did not hold up: the finding was traced to how the reports were scored, with colour counted only when a child actually said a colour word. A child who did not name a colour was marked as having dreamed without one. Of course, as we have just discussed, a very young child's reports are short and her vocabulary is limited, so many colours simply went unmentioned. The silence was in the telling, not necessarily in the dream.

I mention this because it is a nice illustration of the larger point. When we look at what children say, we have to be careful about concluding that what they say is all there is.

### Declan and the purple bus

One of our families recently gave me a lovely example. Jacinta Murphy came to see us last spring because her son Declan, then three, had been waking in the night. She had also noticed that he rarely had anything to say about dreams.

Then one morning, as Jacinta put it, "out of nowhere" Declan announced at the kitchen table: "Purple bus." She asked him where the purple bus was. He thought about it very seriously and said, "In my sleep. It was going fast."

That was the entire report. Two short sentences. Jacinta told me she nearly laughed, but she also felt something shift. She realised that her son had been carrying around more than he could say. The colour word was a gift, but the bus, the speed, and the sense of being carried along in a dream were all there too. He had found just enough language to give her a glimpse.

I love this story because it shows what a good dream report looks like at three. It is not a long adventure. It is a flash of something vivid, handed over in a few words.

### How you can help

You cannot rush language or memory, but there are ways to make it easier for your child to share.

**Ask open, gentle questions.** Instead of "Was it a scary dream?", which can plant an idea, try "What did you see?" or "Who was there?" Then wait. Children often need a long pause before they answer.

**Follow their lead.** If your child says "a dog," you might say, "A dog! What was the dog doing?" Add one small question at a time rather than a barrage.

**Don't fill in the gaps.** It is tempting to say "Was it a big brown dog like Grandma's?" but your suggestions can quickly become part of your child's memory. Let the details come from her.

**Model talking about your own dreams.** At a calm moment, you might share, "Last night I dreamed about a boat." This shows her what a dream report sounds like and that it is safe and ordinary to share one.

**Build everyday storytelling.** Talk through the day together at bedtime. "First we went to the park, then we had lunch, then we came home." These routines strengthen the sequencing skills that later help with dream telling.

**Read and talk about feelings.** Picture books about bedtime, night, and emotions give children words they can borrow when something in a dream felt big.

### When to check in with us

Short dream reports on their own are not a concern. But please do get in touch if your child:

- wakes frequently in distress and cannot be settled
- seems to be afraid of going to sleep most nights
- has nightmares that interfere with daytime mood or behaviour
- snores loudly, pauses in breathing, or seems very tired during the day
- has a sudden change in sleep patterns after a stressful event

These may point to something we can help with, such as a sleep disorder or an anxiety that deserves attention. Our team is always happy to talk, and no question is too small.

### The takeaway

When your three-year-old says "a dog, then I woke up," you are not hearing the full dream. You are hearing the part her memory kept and her words could carry. As she grows, the reports will lengthen. By four or five many children offer a few more details; by school age, real stories begin to appear.

Until then, try to treat each little report as the gift it is. Listen, wonder aloud with her, and let the purple buses come when they come.

# What a Three-Year-Old Tells You About Last Night

*Getting a preschooler to describe a dream is harder than waking one up. The real obstacle is the vocabulary to say what happened, and the memory to hold on to it.*

By Linnea Nordstrom | March 2026 | 12 minute read

At 7:10 on a Tuesday morning, a four-year-old named in the study files only as Participant 31 is sitting cross-legged on her parents' bed, holding a stuffed rabbit by one ear. Her father is asking her, very gently, whether she remembers anything from sleep. She considers the question for a long time. Then she says: "A dog. Big dog. In the kitchen."

"What did the dog do?"

A shrug. "Was just there."

That is, more or less, a complete dream report from a preschooler. It is also, according to the researchers who collect such reports, a small triumph.

## The problem with asking

Adults who study dreaming have a fairly settled toolkit. They can wake sleepers during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, when vivid dreaming is most likely, and ask what was going through their minds. They can hand people diaries. They can have people rate and describe dreams on questionnaires. Almost all of it depends on the participant being able to follow an abstract instruction ("tell me what you experienced while you were asleep"), to search memory, and to put the result into sentences.

A three-year-old can do some of that, on a good day. The trouble is that each step is shaky at once.

"You are asking a child to do something that requires several developing skills simultaneously," says Hilde Gunawardena, a developmental psychologist who runs a preschool sleep study. "They have to understand that what happened in their head while they were asleep is a distinct kind of thing. They have to retrieve it. And then they have to talk about it, with a vocabulary that may not include the words they need."

Gunawardena's lab has spent several years refining ways to get around those obstacles. Her approach is built on parent-assisted morning interviews: families are trained to ask a short, standardized set of questions shortly after the child wakes naturally, in the child's own bed or the parents', rather than in a laboratory. Parents record the answers, often on audio, and the research team codes them later.

I spoke with Gunawardena by video call from her office, where a shelf behind her held a row of well-loved picture books and a tangle of the small plastic animals the lab uses as conversation props.

## Why not just wake them up?

The obvious alternative to waiting for morning is the laboratory awakening: monitor a sleeping child with electrodes, wait for REM sleep, and wake them. That technique has been used with children, and it tends to yield richer, more reliable reports than spontaneous morning recall. But it comes with costs that make it hard to use with the youngest children.

A sleep laboratory is an unfamiliar place. Electrode caps are a lot to ask of a three-year-old, and a child who has been woken abruptly in a strange room at 2 a.m. is not always in a state to be interviewed. "You end up with a distressed, disoriented toddler and a report that tells you mostly about the distress," Gunawardena says. Ethics boards, rightly, are cautious about it as well.

The home-based method trades control for comfort. Researchers cannot be sure which sleep stage a dream came from, or when it occurred in the night. In exchange, the child is in a familiar bed, next to a familiar person, and is more likely to say something at all.

## The bottleneck is language

Ask Gunawardena what limits the field most and she doesn't hesitate. "Language. Language and memory, and they're tangled together."

The language problem comes in layers. The first is plain vocabulary: a child who doesn't yet have words for emotions, spatial relationships or sequences of events cannot easily describe a dream that contained them. A dream might have included being chased, or feeling lost, or something changing shape, and the child may have experienced all of it and still reported only "a monster."

The second layer is grammar and narrative. Telling a story requires putting events in order, linking them with words like "then" and "because," and keeping track of who did what. Three-year-olds are only beginning to manage this for real-life events, such as what happened at the playground. A dream, which may not follow the logic of real events, is harder still.

The third layer is subtler and, to researchers, more frustrating: the difference between a child who had no dream and a child who had one and couldn't tell you about it. A short report could mean a sparse dream. It could equally mean a rich dream and a limited ability to report it. From the outside, the two look identical.

"This is the thing I try to be honest about in every talk," Gunawardena says. "When we say preschoolers' dreams are simple, we are describing what children can report to us. We are not entirely sure how much of that simplicity is in the dream and how much is in the telling."

## What children do report

With that caveat firmly in place, the reports that do come in have a recognizable character. Gunawardena describes the typical preschool dream as short, often a single moment, and static. Not much happens in it. There is rarely a plot, and the child is frequently not an active participant so much as an observer.

The content is ordinary. Animals dominate: dogs, cats, farm animals, the occasional lion or bear. Familiar people appear, usually parents, siblings, a grandparent or a daycare teacher. The settings are everyday ones, like the kitchen, the yard, the car or the bathtub. Participant 31's dog in the kitchen is representative.

"It's a snapshot rather than a film," Gunawardena says. "A dog in the kitchen. Mommy at the table. A cat on the stairs. If you were hoping for adventure, you'd be disappointed."

That picture changes with age. Over the later preschool years and into elementary school, reports tend to get longer, include more characters and more action, and begin to feature the child as an agent, doing and feeling things inside the dream. Researchers who study this link the change to the maturing of the abilities that dreams seem to draw on, including imagery, narrative thinking and the capacity to imagine scenes that aren't in front of you. Whether the dreams themselves become more complex, or the children simply get better at reporting complexity that was always there, is exactly the question the language bottleneck makes hard to answer.

A few old ideas about children's dreams have not survived. One, the notion that young children dream in black and white, was a myth that has been retracted, and Gunawardena waves it off in a sentence when it comes up. It is not what her lab spends its time on. "We have much more interesting problems," she says.

## Memory gets in the way, too

Even a child with decent language faces another hurdle: dreams fade quickly. Adults forget most of their dreams within minutes of waking unless they make an effort to hold on to them. Young children have less developed memory systems for events generally, particularly for retrieving them on request, so the window in which a dream can be reported may be even shorter.

That is the reason for the emphasis on timing in Gunawardena's protocol. Parents are asked to ask right away, before breakfast, before the television, before the day's own events overwrite what's left. They are also coached to avoid leading questions. "Did you have a scary dream about a monster?" is out. "Did anything happen while you were sleeping?" is in.

Suggestion is a serious worry. Small children are eager to please and very good at picking up what an adult wants to hear. A parent who is excited about the study may, without meaning to, nudge a child toward a story. The lab's coding procedure tries to catch this, flagging reports where the content appears to echo the parent's wording, and the team also checks reports against what happened the previous day, since a child might be recounting a real event or a story from a book rather than a dream.

There is also the matter of understanding what a dream *is*. Research on children's concepts suggests that many three-year-olds do not clearly separate dreams from reality. Some say the dog was really in the kitchen. Others will tell you that dreams come from outside, or that other people can see them. A child who doesn't think of the experience as a private mental event may not report it as one.

## Getting better at asking

Gunawardena is optimistic that the methods are improving. Her team has begun using picture cards and small toy animals, which let children point or act out a dream rather than describe it, bypassing at least some of the vocabulary barrier. Early results suggest the props produce more codable reports from the youngest participants, though she cautions that props also raise the risk of suggestion.

Other researchers are pursuing different routes: longer-term diaries kept by parents, repeated interviews on consecutive mornings so that children learn what's being asked, and combining home reports with wearable sleep monitors that give a rough sense of when REM sleep occurred.

None of these will solve the central difficulty. A child's inner life, at three, is only partly available to anyone else, and the child herself may not have full access to it either. But Gunawardena finds something compelling in working at that edge.

"There's a moment in these interviews," she says, "when a child decides that you are really asking about something real, that it matters, and that she has something to give you. Her whole face changes. And then she tells you about the dog."

She laughs. "Usually it's a dog."

At 7:14, back on the bed, Participant 31's father asks one more question: was the dog nice? She nods, thinks, and adds a detail she hadn't offered before. "He had a ball."

Her father writes it down.

*Linnea Nordstrom is a freelance science journalist who writes about sleep, development and the brain.*

# What the Smallest Sleepers Tell Us

**In a quiet university lab, a psychologist asks three- and four-year-olds a deceptively simple question after nap time: "What did you see while you were asleep?" The answers are short, strange, and scientifically precious.**

*By Rafael Alcantara*

At 12:40 on a Tuesday, the lights in Room 3 are dimmed to the color of weak tea, and a four-year-old named Theo is lying very still under a blanket printed with foxes. A soft-cuffed band sits on his forehead, a sensor taped near his temple. On the other side of a one-way window, a research assistant watches a screen of slow, looping lines.

Theo has been asleep for thirty-one minutes. Then the lines change, and the assistant glances at Dr. Ioana Wojcik, who nods.

"We wait," Wojcik whispers. "We always wait. You wake them too fast and you get nothing but grumpiness."

Wojcik directs the Child Sleep and Cognition Lab, a suite of five small bedrooms and one control room tucked into the third floor of a psychology building that otherwise smells of floor wax and burnt coffee. For eleven years she has done something that sounds easy and is not: she gets preschoolers to tell her what they dreamed.

## A day built around naps

The lab's schedule is organized around the rhythms of very small bodies. Families arrive at 11:00, after a light lunch the lab provides, because a hungry child sleeps badly and a stuffed one sleeps like a stone. There is a half-hour of play with a research assistant, whose job is partly to tire the children out and partly to make the lab feel like somebody's slightly odd living room. Then comes the fitting of the sensors, which is the part parents tend to worry about.

"They look dramatic in photographs," Wojcik says. "But nothing is inserted into anybody. It's stickers and a soft headband. Children treat it like a costume. We let them put a sensor on a stuffed animal first."

Naps begin around 12:15. Parents wait in a lounge with a monitor, or sit in the room with the child, depending on temperament. The technicians watch for the electrical signatures of sleep stages, and in particular for rapid eye movement sleep, the phase most associated with vivid dreaming in adults. In young children it is trickier. Their naps are short and their sleep architecture is still maturing.

When the technician sees what looks like REM, there is a decision to make. Wojcik prefers to wait a few minutes into the stage, then wake the child gently, with a low voice and a hand on the shoulder.

Today, with Theo, the voice is her own. "Hi, Theo. It's Ioana. Did you have a good sleep?"

He blinks at the ceiling for a long time. Then he says, in the flat reporting tone of a person describing the weather: "There was a dog. He was in the bath."

"A dog in the bath," Wojcik says, without a flicker of surprise. "What was the dog doing?"

"Nothing."

"Was anyone else there?"

Theo considers this seriously. "No."

That is the whole dream. Wojcik writes it down word for word.

## Why so short?

To a parent, the exchange might seem disappointing. To Wojcik, it is typical, and typical is the point. Across her lab's data and the work of other groups, the dreams that very young children report are brief and plain. They tend to feature a single animal or a familiar object, a still scene, a sense of something happening in a place. Characters are few, often just the child or one other figure. Storylines, with a beginning, a problem, and an ending, mostly do not show up until later.

"People imagine a toddler's dream as a miniature movie," Wojcik says. "Mostly it's a snapshot. A dog, a bath, a moment. The movie comes later, as the child's mind learns to hold a sequence of events together."

That fits with what we know about how young children think. Dreaming appears to lean on the same mental machinery that builds mental images, tells stories, and imagines scenes that are not in front of us. Those abilities develop gradually through the preschool years and beyond. A child who cannot yet easily narrate a story about her own morning may not generate a richly plotted dream either.

There is a second, less glamorous reason the reports are short: three-year-olds are not great witnesses. Language is still coming in. Memory for just-lived experience is fragile. Even a child who had a longer dream may lose most of it in the seconds between waking and talking.

"That's the central difficulty of this field," Wojcik says. "We can't separate the dream from the telling of the dream. What we get is the part the child could keep, and the part the child could say."

> **SIDEBAR: What a young child's dream looks like**
>
> Researchers who collect dream reports from children under five describe a consistent pattern:
>
> **Shorter.** Reports from preschoolers often run a sentence or two. Reports lengthen steadily with age, and by middle childhood many children can narrate multi-part dreams.
>
> **Simpler.** Early dreams tend to be static: an animal, an object, or a place, with little action. When something happens, it is usually a single, ordinary event, such as eating, swimming, or sitting.
>
> **Fewer characters.** Many dreams from the youngest children contain just one figure, often an animal or the child. Groups of people, and especially strangers, become more common as children get older.
>
> **Less storyline.** Plot, meaning a sequence in which one event leads to another, is rare before about age five. Dreams featuring problems, chases, or resolution appear more often in school-age children.
>
> **Often familiar.** Content tends to draw on the child's everyday world: pets, toys, family, and favorite foods.
>
> **A caution.** Because these are spoken accounts, differences between age groups mix real differences in dreaming with differences in language and memory. Researchers try to account for this but cannot remove it entirely.

## The art of asking

Getting a clean report from a four-year-old is a craft with rules. Wojcik's lab follows a script, but the script is meant to be invisible.

Questions must be open and unleading. Asking "Was it scary?" plants an idea; asking "What was it like?" does not. Children, eager to please, will agree with almost anything an adult suggests, so the lab trains its staff to avoid supplying details. A child who says there was a dog gets "Tell me about the dog," never "Was the dog big?"

Staff also learn to tolerate silence. "The instinct is to fill it," says Maya Okonkwo, one of the lab's research assistants, who has been awake for most of the shift and is on her third cup of coffee. "But a lot of the time the child is just retrieving. Give it five seconds and something comes."

Equally important is knowing when to stop. If a child says "I don't know" or begins to chatter about snacks, the session ends. Nothing is gained by pressing. Reports that are coaxed are reports that are contaminated.

Control matters too. The lab also wakes children from non-REM sleep and from quiet wakefulness, and asks the same questions. Children sometimes report dream-like content from those states as well, and sorting out what that means is one of the open puzzles Wojcik wants to solve.

## An afternoon of small data

By 3:30 the lab has run seven naps. Three children produced reports. One described a "big yellow bus" and nothing more. Another, a five-year-old named Amara, offered something unusually rich: she was at her grandmother's house, the cat was upstairs, and she couldn't get the door open "because my hands were too little." Wojcik's eyebrows go up slightly at that one.

"That's a nice example of the next step," she says afterward. "A goal, an obstacle, a feeling about herself. It's a small story. You wouldn't see that in most of our three-year-olds."

Four children produced nothing at all. Two slept too lightly to be sure of the stage, one woke up crying and wanted his mother, and one, as Wojcik puts it, "informed us the dream was private."

That attrition is normal. A day at the lab might yield a handful of usable reports. A study may take a year to gather enough for statistics. The work is slow, and it is done on a budget that would make a physicist laugh.

Asked whether any of the folklore about children's dreams has stood the test of her data, Wojcik is wry. Popular ideas about young minds, she says, spread faster than the evidence for them. Some have a kernel of truth, and some are simply wrong. She is not interested in relitigating the old ones.

"There was a claim, years back, that little kids dream in black and white," she says, glancing at a monitor. "That was retracted, and the replication didn't find it." She turns back to the screen. "Anyway. Amara's about to wake up. Watch the line there."

## What the field still wants to know

The questions that occupy Wojcik now are different, and harder.

One is whether children dream at all in the earliest years, or whether dreaming, in the sense of a reportable experience during sleep, emerges gradually. Her tentative view is that it emerges gradually, with the experience becoming richer as cognition does. But she is careful. "We know what children say. We don't know what they experienced and couldn't say. That's a hole in the middle of the field, and I don't have a way to fill it yet."

Another is the role of sleep itself. Naps in early childhood are known to help consolidate memory and learning. Whether dreaming plays a part in that, or is a by-product of the brain's overnight housekeeping, is unsettled. Wojcik's lab is beginning to test whether the content of a child's nap report relates to what the child learned in a game earlier that day.

A third is individual difference. Some children are frequent, fluent dream reporters from an early age; others rarely produce a word. Whether that reflects dreaming, language, temperament, or simply a sense of what grown-ups want to hear is far from clear.

> **SIDEBAR: If you want to ask your own child**
>
> Parents often ask how to find out what their child dreams. Researchers offer a few cautious suggestions:
>
> - Ask open questions in the morning, such as "Do you remember anything from sleeping?" Avoid suggesting content.
> - Don't worry if the answer is "no." Most young children report little.
> - Accept what you hear without correcting or elaborating it. A child's account is not a test.
> - Remember that the story may reflect language ability more than the dream.
> - If a child is frequently distressed by sleep, talk to a pediatrician. Nightmares are common, but persistent trouble deserves attention.

## Closing the door

At 4:10, Amara is asleep again, this time by choice, curled around a stuffed rabbit while her father reads on his phone in the lounge. Theo has gone home with a sticker. The lights in Room 3 are back up, and Okonkwo is stripping the fox blanket from the bed.

Wojcik sits in the control room, entering the day's reports into a spreadsheet. Seven naps; three reports; one dog in a bath. She types it without comment.

"People want the dreams to be profound," she says, not looking up. "And sometimes they are. But mostly what you learn is that a three-year-old's mind is small and busy and doing its best. A dog in the bath is a perfectly good dream. It's just short."

She saves the file and goes to check on the next family.

# Waking a Child to Ask About a Dream: How Researchers Do It, and Why It Is Harder Than It Sounds

*By Goran Dubois | August 2026*

A four-year-old is asleep in a quiet room at a university lab. A sensor on her forehead and a pair of electrodes near her eyes tell a technician in the next room that she has been in rapid eye movement sleep for nearly ten minutes. The technician walks in, speaks softly, and waits. When the girl stirs, the question comes in a low, even voice: "What was happening just now?"

What she says next, or fails to say, is the raw material of much of what we know about how young children dream. It is also the product of a long list of design decisions, each meant to keep adults from putting words, ideas, or pictures into a child's head. This explainer walks through how awakening studies work, what the ethical debates look like, how interviewers try to avoid leading questions, and why researchers increasingly pair words with pictures.

## Why wake a child at all?

Dreams cannot be observed from outside. The only access is a report, and reports fade quickly. Ask a child at breakfast what she dreamed and you get a mix of memory, reconstruction, and whatever the child thinks you want to hear. Waking her during or right after a likely dreaming period shortens the gap between experience and report.

The method descends from adult sleep research, where awakenings from REM sleep reliably produce dream reports at high rates. With children the picture is more complicated. Younger children often say little, or say "nothing," even when physiological signs suggest the brain was active in ways typical of dreaming. Researchers disagree about how much of that silence reflects an absence of dream experience and how much reflects the limits of language, memory, and willingness to talk to a stranger at 2 a.m.

## How a typical awakening session runs

Matteo Nordstrom manages a pediatric sleep lab and has overseen hundreds of overnight sessions. He says the preparation matters as much as the awakening.

"Families come in for an acclimation night first, with no awakenings at all," he says. "The child sleeps in the room, gets used to the sensors, meets the staff. If the child is anxious or the parent is uneasy, we often stop there."

On test nights, staff monitor sleep stages in real time. Awakenings are typically scheduled after a minimum stretch of REM, and some protocols also include awakenings from non-REM sleep, since children report dream-like experiences from those stages too. The waking itself is gentle: a calm voice, the child's name, dim light. Nordstrom's team limits the number of awakenings per night and avoids waking a child who is difficult to resettle.

"We would rather lose a data point than lose a child's trust," he says. "If a kid is upset, the night is over."

## The ethics: what are we allowed to ask of a sleeping child?

Eun-ji Zubiri studies research ethics in pediatric studies, and she describes awakening protocols as a case where several familiar concerns stack up.

**Sleep disruption.** Interrupting sleep carries a cost, even a small one. Zubiri says review boards typically want justification for the number of awakenings and a plan for helping children return to sleep. "The question is whether the burden is minimal and whether the knowledge gained is proportionate," she says.

**Consent and assent.** Parents give permission, but a young child cannot meaningfully consent. Ethicists therefore stress ongoing assent: watching for signs that a child does not want to continue, and treating those signs as binding. "A four-year-old saying 'no' or turning away is a withdrawal," Zubiri says. "It should be treated like one, even at three in the morning."

**Content of the reports.** Children sometimes describe frightening material. Researchers need a plan for what to do when a child reports a nightmare, including how to comfort the child and how to tell parents. Zubiri also raises privacy: a dream report is an odd kind of personal data, and families should know who will hear it, how it will be stored, and whether recordings exist.

**Suggestion.** Perhaps the least obvious ethical concern is that a poorly run interview can shape what a child later believes she dreamed. That edges into the methodological problem, which is where most of the craft lies.

## Avoiding leading questions

A leading question signals the answer the adult expects. With young children, who are eager to cooperate and sensitive to tone, the effect can be strong.

Interview protocols therefore tend to follow a few principles:

- **Start open.** "What was happening just now?" or "Tell me about what you were thinking" rather than "Did you dream about animals?"
- **Never name content first.** If a child says "a dog," the interviewer follows up with "Tell me more about the dog," not "Was it a big brown dog?"
- **Accept "nothing" and "I don't know."** Pressing a child for something teaches her that an answer is required, and she may supply one.
- **Keep the face and voice neutral.** Delight at a vivid report encourages more of the same, whether or not the report is accurate.
- **Avoid yes/no sequences.** Strings of closed questions invite agreement.
- **Don't correct.** If the child's account seems implausible, the interviewer records it as given.

Nordstrom's lab trains staff with scripted practice sessions and reviews audio of real interviews. "People are surprised how often a well-meaning 'Oh, how fun!' creeps in," he says. "We listen back and flag it."

Zubiri adds that a transcript should preserve the interviewer's words alongside the child's. "If you can't see the question, you can't judge the answer," she says. Many labs now code interviews for how much of the content came from the child versus the adult.

## Where pictures come in

Words are a bottleneck. A two- or three-year-old may have vivid experiences and limited vocabulary, and a shy child may say very little. Picture-matching offers another route.

In a typical version, the child is shown a set of cards after the verbal interview, or sometimes instead of it. The set might include images of people, animals, places, and objects, as well as distractor items that are unlikely to match. The child is asked whether anything on the table was "in" what she was just experiencing, and to point to it.

The approach has strengths. Pointing is easier than describing. A card set also lets researchers include items that were never suggested, so they can see how often children select them.

It has risks too. A picture can prompt a child to say yes because the item is salient or attractive, not because it matches her experience. Researchers mitigate this with balanced decoy items, randomized card order, and a standard instruction making clear that "no" is a fine answer. Picture sets are also usually shown only after the open verbal report, so the images cannot contaminate what the child says first.

"We treat the pictures as a supplement," Nordstrom says. "They give us a second channel. They don't replace the child's own words, and we'd never lead with them."

Some labs also use the cards to probe qualities beyond content, such as whether an experience involved a person the child knows or a place that felt familiar. That kind of question is still harder to standardize, and results from it are treated cautiously.

## Background: what larger studies have found

Awakening interviews with picture matching have by now been used at scale. A 2023 replication of about 220 children used them and found no black-and-white stage in children's dreams. Researchers cite it mainly as one data point among many on how dream reporting develops, and the methods questions above matter more to the field's day-to-day work than any single finding.

## What the field is still sorting out

Several open problems remain. One is how to compare across labs: awakening timing, question wording, and card sets all vary, which makes it hard to combine results. Researchers have discussed shared protocols, and Nordstrom says his team has begun publishing its scripts and card sets so others can reuse them.

Another is how to handle the youngest children. Below about three, reports are sparse, and it is unclear whether better methods would draw out more or whether the experience simply isn't there in a reportable form. Zubiri cautions against treating that uncertainty as an invitation to push harder. "The ethical answer to 'we can't get much from this age' is not 'ask more aggressively,'" she says. "It may be 'accept the limits of what this method can tell us.'"

A third is long-term follow-up. Few studies track whether being woken and questioned affects children's sleep, their feelings about bedtime, or their memory for dreams later on. Ethicists would like more of that evidence, and Zubiri says funders could do more to support it.

## The takeaway

Waking a child to ask about a dream sounds simple. In practice it involves an acclimation night, careful monitoring, a script written to avoid suggestion, an exit plan for every distressed child, and increasingly a deck of picture cards to help small children say what they cannot yet put into words. Each piece exists to protect two things at once: the child's wellbeing and the integrity of what she says.

"The goal is to hear the child," Nordstrom says, "and not to hear ourselves."

# The Small Dreamers

**What can a two-year-old tell us about the night? Sleep scientist Radek Horvath on language, memory, and the patient craft of asking.**

*By Delphine Rossi · Published 21 January 2026*

Radek Horvath runs a child sleep clinic where, on a typical morning, he sees a toddler who won't settle, a six-year-old who sleepwalks, and a teenager whose body clock has drifted several hours out of step with school. He has also spent more than a decade contributing to multi-site studies on how young children experience sleep. We spoke over two long video calls, between his clinic hours and the school run. What follows has been edited for length and clarity.

---

**Delphine Rossi: Let me start with the obvious problem. How do you study the dreams of someone who can barely say "dog"?**

**Radek Horvath:** Carefully, and with a lot of humility. The honest answer is that the obstacle is language, far more than anything about the dreaming brain. We have good reasons to think that sleeping children, even quite small ones, have rich internal activity at night. Their brains cycle through REM and non-REM sleep, they twitch, they murmur, sometimes they laugh. But the only instrument we have for reading out a dream is a person's report of it. And a report needs vocabulary, narrative structure, a sense that "this happened inside my head and not in the room," and the willingness to talk to a stranger in a lab coat at three in the morning. A two-year-old has some of those on a good day.

**So the dreaming might be there, and the reporting isn't.**

That's the cleanest way to put it. Think of it as two separate questions that tend to get fused. One: what did the child experience? Two: what can the child tell me about it? The whole methodological enterprise is about prying those apart, or at least not mistaking the second for the first. If a three-year-old says nothing when I ask about a dream, I can't conclude there was no dream. Silence is very weak evidence.

**What does the standard approach look like in practice?**

The workhorse is the awakening interview. You have a child sleeping in a lab or, increasingly, at home with portable equipment. Sleep stage is monitored. At certain moments, such as a few minutes into REM or at a set point in non-REM, you wake the child gently and ask a short, open question: "What was going through your mind just now?" or "Were you dreaming?" The point of waking them during sleep rather than in the morning is that memory of a dream fades fast. Wait until breakfast and you're mostly collecting reconstruction, not recollection.

**Reconstruction meaning the child filling gaps?**

Yes, and adults do it too. We tidy our dreams as we tell them. Children, especially small ones, are also very responsive to what they think you want. If you ask a leading question, you'll often get a cheerful "yes!" that tells you about their politeness and not their night. So the wording is deliberately bland. We avoid suggesting content. We never say "Did you dream about a monster?" We also run control awakenings from stages where reports are rarer, because a child who says "yes" to everything is giving us a baseline of agreeableness we have to subtract.

**It sounds exhausting for everyone involved.**

For the parents, certainly. For the children, oddly, less so. They go back to sleep very quickly. The people who suffer are the research assistants, who are doing this at two in the morning while keeping their voices at exactly the right pitch of warm and neutral. I have enormous respect for them.

**You mentioned vocabulary. Where does language bite hardest?**

In specifics. A child might manage "there was a dog" long before they can say what kind of dog, what it was doing, or what the surroundings looked like. Descriptive words come late and unevenly. Colour terms are a notorious example. Children often learn colour words slowly and misapply them for a long while: they can sort red from blue perfectly well and still call both "blue." So if you ask "what colour was it?" and receive a muddle, the muddle belongs to the vocabulary, not to the dream.

**Which is where the picture-matching comes in.**

Exactly. If words are the bottleneck, route around them. After an awakening, instead of asking the child to describe something, you show them a set of pictures and let them point. A child who can't say "fluffy" or "spotted" can still tap the picture that looks like what they saw. Pointing is available to a two-year-old in a way that describing isn't.

**How do you design the pictures so you're not just steering the child?**

That's the craft of it. The options have to be balanced: similar in complexity, similar in appeal, and ideally varied only in the dimension you care about. You randomize the left-right position, because small children have strong side preferences. You include options that couldn't plausibly match anything, as decoys, to check they're not just grabbing the brightest card. And you do the same task with waking experiences first: show the child something real, remove it, then ask them to pick it from the array. That tells you whether the format works for that child at all. If a toddler can't match a toy they were just playing with, their dream answers aren't telling us much.

**So the waking task is a kind of calibration.**

Right. It sets a ceiling on what the method can detect. If the child succeeds at matching waking experiences but fails to match a dream, that's informative. If they fail at both, we've learned about the task and nothing about the dream.

The 2023 multi-site replication, about 220 children, used awakening-based interviews with picture-matching instead of colour words, and children as young as two to three chose coloured pictures as often as the older kids did. That settled the old black-and-white idea for me; it's dead. But I'd rather talk about what the picture method does than relitigate it, because the method matters more than any single finding.

**Fair enough. Then what *does* the method let you conclude, and what stays out of reach?**

It lets us say that when a young child reports an experience, we can capture some of its content without the filter of their vocabulary. It doesn't let us say the dream was "like" an adult's. We don't know whether a toddler's dream is a narrative, a scene, a feeling, or a loose stream of images. Even the word "dream" may impose a structure that isn't there. Some researchers prefer "sleep mentation," a deliberately clunky term for whatever thinking-like activity occurs during sleep.

**Is that distinction more than semantics?**

I think so. If a child has fleeting images but no story, then a method that asks "what happened in your dream?" will systematically under-detect it. The question presupposes a plot. Part of the reason the youngest children's reports are so sparse may be that we're asking them to hand over something shaped differently from what they have.

**There's also the problem of memory, isn't there? Even a vivid experience has to be stored and retrieved.**

Memory is the second great bottleneck, and it interacts with language. Very young children have limited episodic memory: they remember events, but with less detail and less stable timing. A dream is an unusual kind of episode, since there's no external anchor, nobody else saw it, and it can't be checked. So a child may have had the experience, retained it for thirty seconds, and then lost it before we've finished the question. That's one reason we wake them *during* sleep and ask immediately.

**Does the child's sense of what a dream even is complicate things?**

Enormously. Around three or four, many children don't firmly distinguish dreams from reality. A child might insist the dog was really in the room. Between roughly four and six, they begin to grasp that dreams are private, internal, and not real. That conceptual shift matters, because it changes how they respond when we ask. Before it, a child may report the content but frame it as something that happened. After it, they can say "it was just a dream." Neither is a better report of the experience; they're differently packaged.

**So you're always reading through a lens that shifts with age.**

Yes, which is why cross-age comparisons are treacherous. If five-year-olds give longer reports than three-year-olds, you can't assume their dreams are longer. Perhaps their reports are. We try to control for this by measuring language ability separately and adjusting for it, and by comparing children on the same picture task so that the response format is constant.

**What would a great study of the youngest children look like, if you could design it with no limits?**

Longitudinal. Follow the same children from eighteen months to six, with the same tasks repeated as they grow, plus a parallel record of language and memory development. Then we could watch the gap between experience and report narrow in real time. I'd also want physiological markers, such as eye movements, brain activity, and facial muscle activity, that could index something about dream content without needing any speech. Those tools exist for adults in early forms. For toddlers they're mostly aspirational, partly because a two-year-old will not keep electrodes on.

**What do parents get wrong about all this?**

They tend to overinterpret in both directions. Some assume that a child who wakes screaming has had a vivid nightmare. Often it's a night terror, which happens out of deep non-REM sleep and which the child won't remember at all. Others assume that because a toddler can't tell them anything, nothing is happening. My advice is boring: treat what the child says as real data about what they can express, stay curious, and don't quiz them. If your four-year-old volunteers something about a dream, listen. Don't ask five follow-ups. You'll get the answers you suggested.

**Last question. What keeps you in this field?**

The fact that the question is so simple and the instruments are so blunt. Every few years someone invents a cleverer way to ask, and we learn that the children knew more than we'd given them credit for. That pattern has repeated enough times that I've stopped being surprised, and started being optimistic. I suspect the next advance won't come from a new machine. It'll come from someone figuring out a better way to ask a two-year-old a question.

# What Is Going On Behind Those Sleepy Eyes?

*The first sleepover is a milestone for every grandparent. Fresh pyjamas, a stuffed rabbit, a nightlight in the hall. But once the little one drifts off, a question lingers: what are they dreaming about in the spare room? ---

**Jacinta Tran: Dr. Okonkwo, let's start with the question every grandparent whispers at 2 a.m. What do small children dream about?**

**Dr. Karin Okonkwo:** Mostly the same things that fill their days, which is to say, simple things. A dog that follows them down the street. A ride on the swing. A cat that talks, or a duck in the bath. Young children's dreams tend to be short and fairly plain. They are less like adventure films and more like a few snapshots strung together. Animals show up a great deal, and so do the people they love best: mum, dad, a sibling, and yes, you.

**JT: Me? I appear in my grandson's dreams?**

**KO:** Very likely! Grandparents who give a child warmth and attention become part of that child's inner furniture. My granddaughter once told me over cereal that I had been in her dream "being a horse." I decided to take it as a compliment.

**JT: Are the dreams always gentle, though? Sometimes a child wakes up crying.**

**KO:** Not always, no. Children do have frightening dreams, and they can start quite early. A chase, a monster, getting lost, a loud noise. It's part of ordinary development and not a sign that anything is wrong. What matters is the comfort afterward. A cuddle, a light, a calm voice saying "You're safe, I'm right here." That's all most little ones need. Often they're back asleep within minutes.

**JT: And in the morning? I'm bursting to ask what they dreamed.**

**KO:** Here is where patience comes in. Most of the time, the dream is simply gone by breakfast. Adults forget dreams too, but small children forget even faster, and they don't yet have the habit of holding onto them. So if you get a shrug and "I don't remember," that is completely normal. It doesn't mean they're hiding anything.

**JT: When they do remember, can they tell us properly?**

**KO:** Only in part, and this is worth keeping in mind. A child's ability to describe a dream is limited by language. They may not have the words for "I was floating" or "it felt like something was behind me." They might say, "There was a big wobbly thing." You'll get fragments, and the fragments may come out jumbled. So we should be careful not to read too much into what we hear. The dream may have been richer than the telling.

**JT: How should a grandparent ask without putting ideas in their heads?**

**KO:** I like to offer a few gentle habits.

*Ask openly, and only once.* "Did you have any dreams last night?" is plenty. Then wait. Let the silence do some work.

*Avoid leading questions.* "Did you have a scary dream about the dark?" hands the child a story to agree with. Children are eager to please and will often say yes. Try "Tell me anything you remember" instead.

*Follow their lead.* If they mention a puppy, you can say, "A puppy! What was the puppy doing?" Use their words back to them, rather than adding your own details.

*Don't press.* If the answer is "I forgot," say "That's fine, it happens to me too," and offer toast. Pushing for details can make a child anxious, or lead them to invent something to satisfy you.

*Make it cosy, not a quiz.* Some of the loveliest dream-talk happens side by side, drawing or walking to the park, not face to face across a table.

**JT: Any reassurance for a grandparent who worries?**

**KO:** One thing worth knowing is that dreaming is a healthy part of growing up, and the old notion that children dream only in black and white was a myth, retracted. But that's a small point. The big point is this: you don't need to decode anything. Your job is to be the calm, kind person who is there when the night is long and the morning comes.

**JT: Do you have a favorite dream story from your own grandchildren?**

**KO:** My grandson, who was four, announced one morning that he had dreamed about "all the grandmas having a picnic with the lions." I asked if the lions were nice. He thought about it very seriously and said, "They shared." I've never forgotten it. I don't know what it meant, and I don't need to. It made me happy to hear.

**JT: Dr. Okonkwo, thank you. I may never look at the spare room the same way.**

**KO:** Pop a nightlight in and enjoy it. Sweet dreams to you all.

*Posted July 2025 by Adaeze Vasquez*

Friends, I'm writing this at 3:40 a.m. with a cold cup of tea and a baby monitor that keeps crackling. Welcome to the first week back from our 14-hour trip to see my parents. If you've ever flown long-haul with a toddler, you already know what's coming. If you haven't, pull up a chair.

So I did what any sleep-deprived mama does: I booked a call with an expert. Dr. Mei Tran is a paediatric sleep consultant who has patiently answered my questions for years. Here's what I learned, plus the practical stuff that actually got us back on track.

## 1. Move bedtime in small steps, not giant leaps

Our instinct was to slam Omar onto home time instantly. Dr. Tran suggested shifting by 15 to 30 minutes every day or two instead. Toddler body clocks are stubborn, and big jumps just lead to overtired meltdowns. We're about halfway there and it's helping.

## 2. Get outside in the morning

Daylight is the cheapest, most powerful reset button we have. We've been doing a 20-minute walk right after breakfast, no sunglasses on him if he'll tolerate it (he won't, so hat brim it is). Dr. Tran says morning light helps anchor the body clock more than almost anything else.

## 3. Protect the nap, but cap it

Resist the urge to let him sleep for four hours at 4 p.m. because he's exhausted. We cap naps at about 90 minutes and wake him gently. It's mean. It works.

## 4. Keep the routine boringly identical

Bath, pyjamas, two books, the same lullaby (it's a terrible rendition of "De Colores," sorry, Omar). Familiar rituals tell a confused little brain that it's night, even when the body disagrees. On the trip we'd let things slide, and I think that made the return harder.

## 5. Make the room feel like *his* room again

Travel cots, strange smells, unfamiliar shadows. Coming home, I swapped back his own sheet and his favourite blanket, and I put the white noise machine back exactly where it lived before. Blackout curtains stay closed until the clock says morning.

## 6. Don't pounce on every night-time noise

This one was hard for me. When Omar started chattering at 2 a.m., I'd rush in. Dr. Tran said that if he's content, it's often better to wait a few minutes. Nothing fancy. Eggs, yoghurt, some beans and rice. Then dim lights, no screens, and low voices. We learned the hard way that a "quick video call with the grandparents" at 6:30 p.m. makes him giddy for an hour.

This is the part I couldn't resist asking. Is my son dreaming about aeroplanes? About Abuela's kitchen? About the enormous baggage carousel he stared at like it was television?

Be patient with your little traveller and with yourself. Light in the morning, dark at night, a boring routine, and a bit of waiting before you rush in. That's really the whole recipe.

# Month One of Waking Up Three-Year-Olds (Or: Why I Now Carry Stickers Everywhere)

*Posted November 14, 2025 by Kofi Phiri, PhD student, Developmental Psychology*

Hello, friends, colleagues, and the three people who found this page by accident while searching for the parking permit form.

It has been a month since I started running wake-up interviews with 3-year-olds in the Early Cognition Lab, and I would like to report that I have made major scientific discoveries. I have not. What I have made is a long list of things I did not know about toddlers. Dr. Odette Vogel, my supervisor, told me at the start that "the first month is mostly humility." She was right, as she is about most things, which is mildly annoying.

For anyone outside the project: the idea is to gently wake children from sleep at home (with parents on board, consent forms signed, and a lot of ethical review behind us) and ask them what, if anything, was going through their minds just before. Researchers do this with adults all the time. With three-year-olds, it's a different sport entirely.

## Problem 1: Everything is "yes"

Ask a three-year-old, "Were you dreaming?" and you will hear "Yes!" Ask, "Were you dreaming about a dragon?" "Yes!" "Were you NOT dreaming about a dragon?" "Yes!"

I now understand why the developmental literature has a whole category of concern about acquiescence in young children. They are not lying. They are being agreeable, possibly because an adult is standing over their bed in dim light with a clipboard and they'd like this to go well for everyone.

## Problem 2: Dreams, TV, and the vast gray area between

One child told me that she had dreamed about "the one with the dogs who have jobs." I got excited. Then her mother whispered that this is a TV show she watches daily. Was it a dream? A memory of an episode? An idealized wish? The child could not say, and honestly, neither could I.

Another boy described a dream in which he was eating pancakes and then "the pancakes were the bus." I decided that this was probably a real dream, because nobody makes up something that strange on purpose. Or maybe they do. They're three.

## Problem 3: The one-minute memory

If a child does report something, you have about sixty seconds. By minute two, the dragon has become a sandwich, or has simply vanished from the universe. Luana Sato, my labmate, who has been at this longer than I have, advised me to "ask fast, ask once, and don't be precious about it." I have written this on a sticky note and attached it to my laptop.

## Problem 4: Snack

Sometimes the answer to every question is that the child wants a snack. This is not a failure of methodology. This is a legitimate need, and honestly, I respect it. I have stopped scheduling anything immediately before breakfast.

## What actually helped: picture cards

After a rough first week, Luana suggested I stop relying on words alone. We made a small deck of simple picture cards: a sleeping face, a bed, a few big familiar things like a dog, a house, a tree, a smiling face, a scared face, and a blank card for "something else."

The cards help in a few ways:

- **They give children something to point at.** Pointing is easier than explaining, and it gets around the "yes to everything" problem, because you can lay out two or three cards and ask which one fits, rather than posing a question that invites agreement.
- **They slow things down.** Kids like handling the cards, so there's a natural rhythm to the conversation.
- **They separate the TV from the dream a little.** If a child picks the dog card, I can ask, "Was that a dog on TV, or a dog in your sleep?" and point to a little picture of a television next to the sleeping face. It's not perfect, but it's better than a blank stare.

I should be honest that none of this solves the deeper puzzle, which is how much we can trust what a three-year-old tells us about an experience they barely have language for. That's the actual research question, and a month in, I have mostly gained a deeper respect for how hard it is.

## A brief family interlude

I told my cousin about the project over the weekend, and she immediately brought up the "black and white dreams" thing. I told her it's been retracted and disproven, and we moved on to arguing about who is hosting the holidays. I don't know the details of it beyond that, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Anyway, she then asked if I could do wake-up interviews on her dog, which I said was outside my protocol.

## What I've learned so far

1. Keep questions short and open, and offer choices instead of yes/no prompts.
2. Interview quickly, before the memory evaporates.
3. Have snacks. Have stickers. Have more stickers.
4. Parents are heroes, and also sometimes asleep in the hallway.
5. Write everything down immediately, even the pancake bus.

Next month I'm planning to pilot a slightly revised card set, with a few more emotions and a clearer "not sure" card, since "not sure" is surely a more honest answer than a confident "yes." Dr. Vogel has agreed to look over the revised protocol, and Luana has promised to laugh at my first draft, which I consider a form of peer review.

If you're a fellow grad student working with young children and have tricks to share, my email is on the department directory page. Bribes in the form of snack recommendations are also welcome.

Until next time, may your participants be rested and your consent forms be signed.

— Kofi

<h2>Research</h2>
<p>The lab studies sleep in early childhood and what young children can tell us about mental activity during sleep. Our current work is observational and interview-based. Two projects are ongoing in 2026.</p>

<h3>Project 1: Parent-reported sleep talk in 18-30-month-olds</h3>
<p>Many toddlers vocalise during sleep, but little systematic information exists on how often this happens or what it sounds like. Parents of children aged 18 to 30 months complete a daily diary for four weeks. They record whether sleep talk occurred, its approximate timing in the night, and any words or phrases they heard. A subset of families also use a bedside audio recorder, which allows us to compare parent reports with recordings.</p>
<p>The aim is to describe patterns: how common sleep talk is, how it changes across this age range, and how it relates to the child's daytime vocabulary.</p>

<h3>Project 2: Picture-choice interviews after waking, ages 3-5</h3>
<p>Children aged 3 to 5 stay overnight in our sleep suite. When a child wakes spontaneously or is woken gently by a researcher, we ask a short set of questions using picture cards. The child points to the picture that best matches what, if anything, was going on in their mind before waking. Pictures show people, places, animals, and a "nothing" option. Interviews are brief, and children may stop at any point.</p>

<h3>What we can and cannot conclude</h3>
<p>Both projects rely on indirect evidence, and two limits shape how we interpret results.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vocabulary.</strong> Children in these age groups have limited language. A child who cannot describe an experience may still have had one. Picture-choice methods reduce this problem but do not remove it, and sleep talk cannot be treated as a transcript of inner experience.</li>
<li><strong>Memory.</strong> Young children may forget or reconstruct experiences within seconds of waking. Parent reports add a further layer of recollection. Our data therefore reflect what is remembered and reported, not necessarily what occurred.</li>

**Little Wattle Early Learning Centre**
*Staff and Family Newsletter, October 2026*

**Director's Desk**
*by Alinta Sinclair, Director*

**When the Nights Get Darker, the Dreams Get Louder**

If you've been in the Possum Room or the Kookaburra Room lately, you may have noticed something. Since the clocks changed and the evenings closed in, more of our children are arriving with stories about bad dreams. "A big dog chased me." "I couldn't find Mum." "There was a noise under my bed." Some tell us at the door, some over morning tea, and some quietly, in the middle of a puzzle, as though the memory has only just caught up with them.

I'm not a researcher, so I won't pretend to have the full science. What I can share comes from a sleep workshop I attended earlier this year, along with twenty-odd years of watching small children work through big feelings. The workshop presenter explained that darker evenings often shift bedtime routines, light exposure and sleep timing, and that children can be unsettled by those changes. Add the natural growth in imagination at this age and it's no wonder the dreams are getting talked about.

**What preschoolers' dreams tend to look like**

Children this age usually describe dreams that are short and simple. Typically it's a single scene, not a long story. The cast is often animals, familiar people such as Mum, Grandad or a teacher, or the classic themes of being chased or being lost. You won't often hear a plot twist. You'll hear, "A lion was in the garden."

Another thing to remember is that many young children can't yet separate dream from waking life. When a three-year-old tells you the monster was *really* in the cupboard, they may truly believe it. Arguing the point ("Dreams aren't real, sweetheart") rarely helps. The feeling was real even if the monster wasn't, and that's what we respond to.

**Three things to say and do**

**1. "Tell me about it."** Invite the child to share, then listen without rushing to fix. Let them lead. Some will give you two words and some will give you ten minutes. Both are fine. Being heard takes the sting out of a frightening memory.

**2. "You're safe now."** Say it calmly, close to their level, and mean it. Follow it with something concrete: "You're at Little Wattle. I'm right here. Your grown-up is coming at pick-up time." Avoid "there's nothing to be scared of," which can feel like we're dismissing what they felt.

**3. Draw the dream.** Offer paper and textas and say, "Would you like to draw it?" Drawing gives the child some control over the picture. Some children like to scribble over the scary part or give the monster a silly hat. Don't interpret the drawing for them. Just ask, "What's happening here?" and write down their words if they want you to. With permission, you can pass the drawing home so families can continue the conversation.

Ezra Donnelly, our lead teacher in Kookaburras, has been doing this for weeks. "I used to want to jump in with reassurance straight away," he told me. "Now I ask them to tell me first. Half the time they feel better before I've said a word about being safe."

**A note for talking with families**

When parents ask us about dreams, please stick to what we actually know and what we've seen. One thing worth a mention, since it still floats around online: the claim that toddlers dream only in black and white is false, and it was retracted, so please don't repeat it to families. If you're not sure about something, it's perfectly fine to say, "I'm not certain, but here's what we do at the centre."

**When to pass things on**

Occasional bad dreams are normal. Let me or your room leader know if a child seems distressed for days, is avoiding rest time, or if a family mentions big changes at home. We can then talk with parents about whether extra support might help, and suggest they speak with their GP or child health nurse if they're worried.

**One small ask**

Keep rest-time routines calm and predictable, and keep the lights soft rather than abruptly dark. Predictability is a comfort to small children, especially when the days feel shorter.

Thank you, as always, for the care you bring to our little ones. See you at the spring working bee on the 24th, rain or shine.

*Alinta*

**NORTH LIGHT SCIENCE** · December 2025 · Long Read

# What the Smallest Sleepers Tell Us

**Listening to children's dreams is harder than it sounds. A century of attempts, a Nordic laboratory, and the surprising power of a three-year-old's vocabulary.**

*By Ulla Dahl*

---

The first thing Jamila Kipkemboi does when a child arrives at the sleep centre is show them the bed. Not the real one, in the quiet room at the end of the corridor, but a smaller one: a stuffed bear tucked under a blanket on a low shelf in the reception area. "This is Bjørn," she tells a four-year-old in a green raincoat. "Bjørn sleeps here sometimes. Do you want to see where you'll sleep?"

The child considers this seriously, then takes the research assistant's hand. It is half past four on a dark November afternoon, snow is coming sideways across the car park, and I am watching the opening move of a method that has taken the better part of a century to refine: how do you get a very young person to tell you what happened in their head while they were asleep?

The answer, it turns out, is slowly, sideways, and without leading questions.

## A short, awkward history

Scientific interest in children's dreams is older than sleep science itself. In the early twentieth century, psychologists collected dream accounts from their own children and from pupils at nearby schools, writing them down in notebooks and publishing them as curiosities. These were charming and almost useless. A father interviewing his own daughter over breakfast is not a controlled instrument. The child knows what Papa wants to hear. The father knows what he hopes to find.

The great shift came in the 1950s, when researchers discovered that sleep has distinct stages, including the rapid-eye-movement phase that is richly associated with vivid dreaming. Suddenly dreams could be caught close to the moment of their occurrence. Wake a sleeper from REM and ask what was going through their mind, and you get a report that is fresh, detailed and not yet corrupted by a morning's worth of forgetting.

Applied to children, the technique produced a first generation of laboratory studies in the 1960s and 70s. Researchers brought children into sleep labs, wired them with electrodes, woke them at intervals, and asked. The results were sobering. Children woke from REM sleep often reported nothing at all, or something so brief it hardly counted. Pre-schoolers, in particular, offered dream reports that were short, static and unadorned: a dog, a house, "me in bed." Where adults narrate sprawling adventures, young children seemed to dream, if they dreamed at all, in single frames.

For decades this was interpreted as a fact about the dreams themselves: young children, the argument went, simply have thinner inner lives while asleep, because the cognitive machinery for imagery and narrative has not yet matured. It is an elegant idea. It is also, as researchers have increasingly argued, hard to separate from a more mundane problem, which is that a report is not a dream.

## The report is not the dream

This distinction sits at the heart of modern work in the field, and it is the thing the researchers I met kept returning to. What we can measure is never the dream. It is what a child says about the dream, to a particular adult, in a particular room, using whatever words that child happens to have.

Consider what you are asking of a three-year-old. You are asking them to hold onto an experience that is already fading, translate it from sensation into language, decide which part is worth mentioning, and say it to a person they met an hour ago, at an hour when they would rather be asleep. A child with a vocabulary of a few hundred words will say what those words permit. If the dream contained a feeling for which the child has no term, or a sequence of events that requires connectives like "and then" or "because," much of it may simply not make it out.

"A short report can mean a short dream," one researcher told me. "It can also mean a short vocabulary. We try very hard not to confuse the two."

## Inside the centre

The sleep centre sits on the edge of a university campus in a low building of pale wood and glass, with a sloping roof designed, I am told, to shed snow. Inside it feels more like a Scandinavian guesthouse than a laboratory. The overnight rooms have wool blankets and lamps with warm bulbs. The monitoring equipment is tucked into a separate room, out of sight and out of earshot.

Everything about the set-up is meant to make the strangeness of the situation smaller. Children visit in advance, meet the staff, and are allowed to try the sensors on a toy. Parents sleep nearby. Awakenings are gentle and timed against the recorded sleep stage; the assistant on duty, often Kipkemboi, uses a soft voice and a fixed, deliberately plain script.

The script matters. "What was happening?" is acceptable. "Did you see a monster?" is not, because a child will often agree with a suggestion rather than contradict an adult. Kipkemboi has learned to wait through long silences. "Counting to ten in your head is a skill," she says. "Most people give up at three."

Reports are recorded and transcribed, then coded by two people working independently. The coders sort what they hear into categories: characters, settings, actions, emotions, and the presence or absence of a sequence of events. Where they disagree, they discuss; where they cannot agree, the item is flagged. It is painstaking, unglamorous work, and it is the reason the centre's findings are taken seriously by colleagues elsewhere.

What emerges from this care is a picture that is quietly different from the old one. Children's reports are indeed simple. A typical account from a young child is a sentence or two: "I was at the swimming pool. The big dog was there." But the simplicity is patterned. It tracks the child's language development closely. As children acquire words for emotions, for time, for cause and effect, their reports lengthen and gain structure. The question researchers now ask is how much of that growth belongs to the dreaming and how much to the telling, and they are candid that the two cannot yet be fully pulled apart.

## The vocabulary problem

One experiment Kipkemboi describes shows how slippery this is. Children are asked about the same night's dream twice, once with open questions and once with picture cards offering a range of possible scenes, objects and feelings. The cards do not suggest content; they simply supply words and images the child might otherwise lack. Reports elicited this way tend to be richer, though that raises the opposite worry, that cards might nudge a child toward things they did not experience.

Neither method is perfect, and the team uses both, comparing the results. The principle is triangulation: no single instrument is trusted on its own. A finding that appears with open questions, with cards, and with parental reports of what the child says unprompted at breakfast is more credible than one that appears in only one.

Language also shapes what counts as a dream at all. In several languages the word for "dream" is the same as the word for "wish" or for "imagining," and young children often use it loosely. A child who says "I had a dream about ice cream" may be describing a nocturnal experience, a wish, or a game. Coders learn to read the surrounding context, and the team keeps a running glossary of the idiosyncratic ways local children use the term.

## A word about the Nordic tradition

Nordic countries have long been generous hosts to sleep research, helped by high public trust in medicine, well-kept population records, and a culture of open data sharing that is not universal. Several of the region's laboratories pool anonymised recordings and transcripts, which means that results can be checked by people other than those who produced them.

That habit paid off for the centre's lead researcher, Ingrid Solheim, whose team in 2020 re-analysed shared data from another group's work. Solheim's group is known for showing that the 2019 colour claim came from coding, and it is now a retracted myth.

Solheim herself would rather not dwell on it. When I raise the subject in her office, a narrow room lined with binders and a window full of snow, she gives a small shrug and turns the conversation. "That is finished," she says. "What interests me is what we are doing now." And she is off, describing a project that follows the same children from age three to six, recording their reports each year and tracking how vocabulary, memory and narrative skill develop alongside them. It is, she says, the closest the field can come to watching a dream-telling ability grow.

She talks with the unhurried enthusiasm of someone who has been asked the same question many times and would prefer a better one. Her current interest is in what she calls "the first sentence": the point at which a child's report stops being a label and becomes a small story with a before and an after. "That moment is more interesting than any single dream," she says. "It is where the child discovers that an experience can be put into words and handed to someone else."

## What remains unknown

For all this care, large questions are open. We do not know with confidence when dreaming begins in a developmental sense, or whether the very youngest children experience anything we would recognise as a dream. We do not know how much of the apparent leap in dream complexity between ages five and nine reflects changes in the brain and how much reflects changes in language, memory and willingness to talk. And we do not know how well findings from a quiet Nordic laboratory travel to children who grow up in other languages, houses and bedtime customs.

What the field has learned, rather than any particular answer, is humility about its own instruments. The history of child dream research is in large part a history of discovering how much of what looked like data was produced by the method: by the adult in the room, by the wording of the question, by the way a transcript was sorted into boxes.

## Bedtime

By eight o'clock the four-year-old in the green raincoat is asleep, a sensor cap sitting slightly crooked on her head, Bjørn the bear wedged under one arm. Down the corridor, Kipkemboi settles into her chair beside the monitors with a thermos and a notebook. If the recording shows the right pattern of activity, she will walk quietly down the hall, crouch beside the bed, and ask her gentle, plain question.

"Most nights," she says, "the answer is 'I don't know.' And that's a good answer. That one we can use."

I leave through the snow. Behind me the low wooden building glows in the dark, a few lit windows, a few small sleepers, and a patient adult waiting to hear whatever they have to say.

---

*Ulla Dahl is a Scandinavian-based science journalist. Her reporting on this article included a visit to the sleep centre in November.*

**QUARTER MOON** · Summer 2025 · The Long Read

# What the Small Sleepers Say

*For more than a century, scientists have tried to learn what children see when they close their eyes. The story is one of patient, partial gains, and of one stubborn obstacle: a child can only tell us what a child has words for.*

**By Agnieszka Ramirez**

Ask a four-year-old what she dreamed about and you may get a long, delighted account of a dog who lived in the bathtub. You may also get a shrug, or the single word "scary." Both answers are data, and neither is easy to interpret. This is the problem at the center of child dream research. The dream happens in a mind that cannot yet describe it well, and the researcher must work out how much of the report belongs to the dream and how much to the child's vocabulary, memory, mood and wish to please the grown-up in the room.

What follows is a timeline of how the field has tried to get around that problem. It is not a story of sudden revelations. It is a story of small corrections, each of which tended to expose the limits of the one before.

## 1890s–1920s: The parent diaries

The earliest records come from parents who happened to be scholars. Late nineteenth-century child psychologists kept notebooks on their own children, writing down first words, first fears and, now and then, something the child said on waking. Sigmund Freud's *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) included short dreams from his own young children, which he read as simple wish fulfilment: a hungry child dreams of strawberries, a child who wanted a boat trip dreams of one.

These diaries are charming and almost useless as evidence. One observer, one child, no controls, and an adult deciding what counted as a dream. Still, they set up a question that would last: when a toddler says "doggy, bed, scared," is that a dream report or a fragment of the evening?

## 1930s–1950s: Questionnaires and the first interviews

Jean Piaget, in the 1920s and 1930s, asked children directly what dreams were and where they came from. Younger children, he found, often said dreams come from outside, from the night or the lamp, and are really "there" in the room. Piaget treated this as a stage of thinking. Later researchers would point out that his method rewarded children who could talk about dreams in the abstract, which is not the same as being able to have them.

Mid-century studies sent questionnaires to parents and teachers, producing tallies of frightening dreams and recurring themes. They gave the field its first large numbers. They also showed what a report is: a record of what adults chose to remember and children chose to say.

## 1950s–1960s: The laboratory arrives

In 1953, Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman reported rapid eye movement sleep, and dream research changed overnight. If dreaming tracked a measurable physiological state, researchers could wake sleepers during it and ask what had just happened, instead of relying on morning recall.

Children were a natural next step. Early sleep-lab work in the late 1950s and 1960s woke young participants from REM and non-REM sleep and asked for reports. The results unsettled some assumptions. Children woke from REM sleep often had little to say, and the reports they gave were short and static compared with adults'.

## 1980s–1990s: Foulkes and the longitudinal turn

The most careful work of the period came from David Foulkes, who followed children across several years of repeated laboratory awakenings. His conclusion, published in *Children's Dreams* (1982) and developed afterward, was that dream reports grow more complex alongside other cognitive abilities, such as visuospatial skill and narrative competence. Very young children's reports were sparse, often a single image or animal, rarely with a story, rarely with the child as an active character.

Foulkes's findings were widely read as saying that young children dream little. But another reading was available, and Foulkes himself was careful about it: perhaps young children dream and simply cannot report. The data could not distinguish the two. Nobody could look inside the experience.

This was the first big lesson about language, and the field has relearned it in several forms since.

## 2000s: Better questions

A generation of researchers began to take seriously the idea that the interview itself was a variable. Children are suggestible. They answer the question they think they were asked. They say "yes" to be agreeable. They drop out of a long interview in the middle of a sentence.

Dr. Efua Kawasaki, a child sleep researcher whose lab has spent years comparing interview styles, put it to me plainly. "A dream interview is a conversation with a very small expert on a very private subject," she said. "If you lead, you get your own idea handed back to you. If you wait too long, you get silence. Most of the craft is in the first thirty seconds after the child wakes: open question, no hints, and then the discipline to stop talking."

Her group's protocols favor one open prompt ("What was happening in your sleep?"), followed by neutral nudges ("Tell me more"), and they treat any detail that appeared only after an adult suggestion as contaminated. That sounds modest. In practice it removed a large share of the apparent dream content from some earlier datasets.

## 2010s: Awakenings, picture cards and nonverbal reports

If words are the bottleneck, offer something besides words. Researchers began to pair serial awakenings with picture cards: faces showing different emotions, animals, simple scenes. A child who cannot say "I was frightened and then it was fine" can point to the worried face and then the smiling one. Drawing, toys and short gestures have been added in some labs.

None of this removes the language problem. It shifts it. A card must be understood, and a child may point at a card because it is the shiniest. Kawasaki is blunt about this. "A card is a better microphone, not a mind-reader," she said. Her lab runs children through practice trials with ordinary waking memories first, so that the team knows how a given child uses the cards before any dream is at stake.

Mistakes happened along the way. One result from 2019, which seemed influential at the time, has been retracted and should not be repeated.

## Today: Many small methods, one honest limit

Current work does not hang on a single technique. Laboratories combine overnight monitoring, brief awakenings in both REM and non-REM sleep, structured interviews, picture-card responses and parent logs, then check whether the pictures agree. Where they agree, confidence grows a little. Where they diverge, researchers say so, which is perhaps the field's most important habit.

Several patterns have held up across methods. Young children report dreams less often than older ones. Their reports are shorter and simpler. Animals and familiar people are common. Reported frightening content increases with age, though whether that reflects the dreams, the vocabulary for fear or the child's ability to recall them is an open question. The reports lengthen as language and narrative skill develop, and the question of what a very young child experiences below that threshold remains unanswered.

## What the timeline teaches

Read from the first diaries to the picture cards, the history is a series of fixes. Parents' notebooks gave way to questionnaires, which gave way to laboratory awakenings, which gave way to careful interviews, which gave way to nonverbal prompts. Every step improved the evidence, and every step ran into the same wall: the experience belongs to the child, and the child's access to it is limited by what she can say, remember and be bothered to tell a stranger with a clipboard.

Kawasaki sees that as a reason for patience rather than despair. "We are not going to leap to certainty," she said. "We are going to get slightly less wrong each decade. That is what progress looks like when your subject is three feet tall and in pajamas."

It is a fair summary. The bathtub dog may never be fully recoverable. But the questions are better than they were, and the people asking them are much more honest about what an answer can and cannot be.

*Agnieszka Ramirez writes about sleep, memory and development. This article is part of Quarter Moon's summer series on early childhood.*

**Sleep & Development › Toddlers (1–3 years) › Thread: What do toddlers dream about?**

---

**Lorcan Saeed** · June 9, 2025

My daughter is 22 months old. This morning she woke up, sat up in her cot, and said "doggy, doggy" in a very worried voice, even though we don't have a dog. It made me wonder whether she'd been dreaming. Do toddlers dream at all, and if so, what do they dream about? ---

**Hilde Rousseau** · Member, Neuroscience · June 9, 2025

Hi Lorcan, lovely question, and "doggy, doggy" in a worried voice is a great example. A quick disclaimer first: I'm a neuroscientist, but I don't study children's dreams myself, so I'm summarizing what I understand of the field rather than speaking from my own data. I'll try to flag where things are well supported and where they're more tentative.

**Do toddlers dream?**
Almost certainly some form of it, yes. Toddlers spend a good deal of their sleep in REM, the stage most associated with vivid dreaming in adults, and the brain is very active during it. But there's a caveat: dreaming is a subjective experience, and we can't directly observe it. With adults we ask them after waking. With a 22-month-old, we mostly can't. So what we know about young children's dreams is partly inference and partly the small amount that children can report.

**What is the content thought to be like?**
The picture that emerges from studies in which researchers have woken older preschoolers and asked what they were experiencing, along with parental reports, is that early dreams are thought to be simple and closely tied to daily life. Common themes include animals (very prominent), familiar people such as parents and siblings, eating and food, and things that frighten them. The dreams also tend to be short, more like a brief scene or a static image than a story with a plot. Young children's dreams generally seem to feature them less as an active character and more as an observer, though I'd hold that loosely.

So a dream involving a dog, even in a household without one, isn't surprising. Animals show up constantly in picture books, songs, and toys at this age, and they carry strong emotional weight for toddlers.

**Why simple at first?**
The usual explanation, and I want to stress it's what's *thought* rather than proven, is that how rich a dream can become depends on other aspects of brain development. Two things are often mentioned. One is visual-spatial ability: building a scene in your mind's eye, with objects in places and a sense of space, seems to depend on skills that keep maturing through early childhood. The other is the prefrontal cortex, which supports things like imagination, narrative, and holding a sequence of events together. It develops slowly, over many years. The idea is that as these abilities mature, dreams become longer, more story-like, and more elaborate. That's a reasonable hypothesis with some supporting evidence, but I wouldn't call it settled.

**Why we know so little**
Honestly, the biggest limit is reporting. A 22-month-old has a vocabulary of perhaps a couple hundred words and quite limited memory for what happened ten minutes ago, let alone during the night. Even if a rich dream occurred, she may not be able to tell you, or may not remember it by the time she's awake enough to talk. So a lack of reports doesn't mean a lack of dreams, and the reports we do get are probably an underestimate of what's happening. Researchers generally get more reliable accounts from children around age 5 and up, which is partly a story about language and memory rather than only dreaming itself.

**A note on something you may run into**
If you search around, you may come across a claim that circulates widely about the color of young children's dreams. It was retracted by the journal that published it in 2022, so please don't repeat it or build any assumptions on it.

**Practical takeaway**
If your daughter wakes up worried, comfort is the right response, just as you'd give for any scare. You don't need to interpret the content. A calm voice, a cuddle, and a reminder that she's safe is plenty. Nightmares and night fears become more common in the preschool years, which tracks with growing imagination.

If you ever want to look further, search for work on children's dream reports from sleep laboratories, and be wary of popular articles that state things with more confidence than the research supports.

# Do Babies Dream?

**What we know, what we don't, and why you can relax either way**

*By Selin Krishnan, Health Journalist*

If you have ever watched your newborn sleep, you may have seen it: the fluttering eyelids, the sudden smile, the little twitch of a hand, a tiny grimace that vanishes as quickly as it came. It is hard not to wonder what is going on in there. Is your baby dreaming?

It is one of the questions parents ask most often, and the honest answer is a gentle one: we don't know for certain. But what scientists do know is interesting, and none of it should worry you.

## Newborns spend a lot of sleep in REM

Adults spend roughly a fifth to a quarter of the night in REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, the stage most strongly linked with vivid dreaming. Newborns are different. In the first weeks of life, babies may spend around half of their total sleep in a REM-like state, and because newborns sleep for much of the day and night, that adds up to many hours.

Researchers often call this stage "active sleep" in infants. It looks busy. Breathing can be irregular, eyes move beneath the lids, and limbs jerk or stretch. Quiet sleep, by contrast, is calmer and more regular, with slower breathing and few movements.

As babies grow, the balance shifts. The share of REM sleep gradually falls over the first year and continues to change through childhood, settling toward adult patterns over time.

Why so much active sleep early on? Researchers think it plays a role in brain development. Newborn brains are building connections at a remarkable pace, and the movements and activity of active sleep may help wire up the systems babies will rely on for movement and sensation.

## So, are they dreaming?

Here is where the reassuring honesty comes in. REM sleep and dreaming are related, but they are not the same thing. REM is something we can measure from the outside, through eye movements, breathing and brain activity. Dreaming is an experience, something that happens from the inside, and that is much harder to pin down.

"A baby can't tell us what, if anything, they experienced," says Freya Kaur, a sleep researcher who studies sleep development in early childhood. "We can see that the brain is highly active during active sleep. What we can't say is whether that activity comes with anything like a story or a picture in the mind. It may be a very different kind of experience from an adult's dream, or there may be no experience of it at all."

In other words, the twitching and smiling you see are real, but they are not proof of a dream. Those movements are a normal feature of a developing nervous system, and they are not a sign that your baby is distressed or having a bad dream.

## When does real evidence of dreaming appear?

The most reliable evidence about dreaming comes from one source: people telling us about it. That means the clearest evidence in children comes only once they can talk.

When researchers wake young children from REM sleep and ask what was happening, very young children tend to report little. Their descriptions, when they come, are often short and simple, such as a single image or a familiar animal, rather than the long, wandering stories adults describe. Dream reports tend to become richer and more detailed as children's language, memory and imagination develop through the preschool and school years.

"Dreaming seems to develop alongside other thinking skills," Kaur explains. "The ability to picture things in the mind, to hold a scene together, to remember and describe it afterwards. Those abilities grow gradually, so it makes sense that dream reports do too."

## A myth you may have come across

When we looked at what parents were typing into search engines about baby and toddler sleep, one question kept turning up alongside the dreaming ones: a claim about the colour of children's dreams. It is a myth that has been retracted, and Kaur is blunt about it. "There is no support for it," she says. We will leave it there and get back to what is useful.

## What this means for you

You do not need to do anything about your baby's dreams, whatever they may or may not be. Here is what actually matters:

- **Active sleep is normal.** Twitching, grunting, fluttering eyelids and sudden smiles are all part of healthy newborn sleep. You don't need to wake your baby to "rescue" them.
- **Give them a chance to settle.** Babies often make noises or stir briefly during sleep and then drift off again. Pausing for a moment before picking up your baby can help them learn to resettle.
- **Follow safe sleep guidance.** Place your baby on their back on a firm, flat sleep surface, with nothing soft in the cot, and follow the advice of your health visitor or paediatrician.
- **Trust your instincts.** If your baby's breathing seems laboured, if they are difficult to rouse, or if something simply doesn't seem right, contact your doctor or emergency services.

## The takeaway

Newborns spend a great deal of their sleep in REM, and that activity seems to be an important part of how their brains grow. Whether any of it feels like dreaming to them is a question science cannot yet answer. Solid evidence of dreams arrives only when children are old enough to tell us about them.

Until then, enjoy the show. Those small smiles and flutters are a sign of a brain hard at work.

# Why We Know So Little About Toddlers' Dreams

**Reference article | Pediatric Sleep and Development**
**Author: Yusra Kim, PhD, Developmental Psychologist**

*This article is intended for clinicians and allied health professionals. It is an educational overview and does not replace clinical guidelines or individualized assessment.*

---

## Key points

- Children under 3 cannot give a verbal dream report. Almost everything we would like to know about their dreaming is therefore inferred rather than observed.
- Awakening studies with young children depend on pictures and yes/no questions, which carry well-documented risks of acquiescence and suggestion.
- Memory, not just language, constrains what we can learn: the capacity to encode, hold and narrate an internal experience matures slowly.
- A 2024 review in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* concluded that dream research with children under 3 is limited mainly by their language.
- For clinicians, the practical message is humility: a parent's account of what a toddler "dreamed" is an interpretation of behavior, not a report of an experience.

---

## The problem of the missing witness

Dreaming is an unusual object of study. Unlike a rash, a heart rhythm or a gait, a dream has no external signature that reliably tells an observer what occurred. We know about adult dreams because adults tell us about them, and even then we rely on memory, motivation to report and the ability to put an experience into words. Remove the report and what remains are physiological markers, such as rapid eye movements, muscle atonia and EEG activation, that tell us the brain is in a state *associated* with dreaming, not what, if anything, is being experienced.

In toddlers, the report is exactly what is missing. A child of 18 months may have a vocabulary of a few dozen words. A child of 30 months may produce short sentences, but the grammar needed to separate "something happened" from "something happened in my sleep" is still developing. The concept of a dream as an internal, private, non-real event is itself a cognitive achievement. Work on children's understanding of mental states suggests that this distinction becomes secure only gradually in the preschool years, and many younger children treat dreams as events in the room or the outside world.

This is why the field's knowledge of very early dreaming is thin. It is not that the question is unimportant. It is that the main instrument of dream science, the verbal report, does not yet exist in the population we most want to understand.

## What the sleep laboratory can and cannot tell us

Infants and toddlers spend a large share of sleep in states that resemble REM, and the proportion of REM falls steeply across the first years of life. It is tempting to read this as a measure of dream frequency. It is not. REM-like activity in the newborn period appears to serve developmental functions, including sensory and motor circuit maturation, that may have little to do with dream experience as adults know it.

The rapid-eye-movement and EEG literature therefore supports only a cautious statement: the neural conditions that accompany dreaming in older children and adults are present, in immature form, early in life. Whether those conditions produce experience, and of what kind, remains open. Polysomnography cannot close that gap, because it measures the brain and not the mind.

## Awakening studies and their constraints

The standard method for older children is the serial awakening protocol. Participants are woken from REM or non-REM sleep and asked, as soon as possible, what was going through their mind. This yields dream reports with much better ecological validity than morning recall. In school-age children, the method has produced a reasonably consistent picture: reports from the youngest children tend to be brief, static and sparsely populated, with few characters and little narrative action, and richness increases with age alongside visuospatial and narrative skills.

For children under 3, the protocol must be adapted heavily, and each adaptation costs something.

**Picture-based prompts.** Researchers may show a set of simple images, such as faces, animals or household objects, and ask the child to point to anything that "was in" the sleep experience. This avoids the demand for fluent speech, but it introduces new problems. The child must understand the instruction, hold the question in mind, map an internal image onto an external picture and be willing to engage with an unfamiliar adult in the middle of the night. Pointing may reflect recognition of a familiar picture and not recall of a dream.

**Yes/no questions.** Closed questions are within the capacity of many 2-year-olds, but they are the format most vulnerable to bias. Young children are prone to saying "yes" to questions regardless of content, particularly when asked by an adult and when they are drowsy or wish to please. A "yes" to "Did you see a dog?" cannot be taken at face value without controls. Properly designed studies include foil items, randomize question order and look for response patterns that signal acquiescence, but even then the information gained per awakening is small.

**The awakening itself.** Waking a toddler from sleep is disruptive for the child, the family and the data. Ethical review boards rightly limit the number and timing of awakenings, and a distressed, fully roused toddler is not an ideal reporter. Successful awakenings from the right sleep stage, followed by a cooperative child able to engage with the task, are a small fraction of attempts, and the samples that result are small and potentially unrepresentative.

## Memory is part of the story

Language is the most obvious barrier, but it is not the only one. A dream report requires that an experience be encoded, retained across the transition from sleep to waking and retrieved on demand. Each step is immature in toddlers. Autobiographical memory is still consolidating in the early years, and the well-known phenomenon of infantile amnesia shows that adults retain few explicit memories from the first three years of life. A toddler may therefore have experiences during sleep that are never encoded in a form that can later be retrieved, even by a child with adequate language.

This matters for interpretation. A null result, such as a toddler who says nothing or answers "no" to every prompt, is not evidence of the absence of dreaming. It may reflect failures of encoding, retrieval, comprehension or cooperation. The asymmetry is important: positive reports are vulnerable to suggestion, and negative reports are vulnerable to inability. Neither supports strong conclusions.

## What the 2024 review concluded

A 2024 review in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* surveyed the literature on dreaming in early childhood and reached a conclusion that will be unsurprising to those who work with young children: dream research with children under 3 is limited mainly by their language. The authors' emphasis on language as the principal bottleneck is helpful because it points to where methodological progress might be made. Better nonverbal paradigms, validated with older children whose reports can be checked, are more likely to move the field than additional polysomnography alone.

The same review also found nothing credible supporting the old claim that young children dream in black and white.

## Indirect approaches and their value

If direct report is unavailable, what else can be done? Several indirect strategies add modest but real information.

**Parental observation.** Parents describe toddlers who cry out, sit up or talk in sleep. These accounts are clinically relevant, particularly for distinguishing night terrors, confusional arousals and nightmares, but they are descriptions of behavior. A child who screams during a night terror is typically in deep non-REM sleep and typically has no recall, which illustrates the gap between observable distress and dream content.

**Retrospective and longitudinal designs.** Following children from toddlerhood into the preschool years allows researchers to compare early behavior with later, verbally reported dream content. This can show developmental continuity in themes and emotional tone, but it cannot reach back to what the child experienced at 20 months.

**Play and drawing.** Preschoolers can sometimes show in play or drawing what they cannot yet say. These methods become feasible near the upper edge of the age range discussed here and share the problem that a drawing made after waking is shaped by waking imagination.

**Cross-species and neurodevelopmental reasoning.** Work on sleep-dependent memory consolidation and replay offers a theoretical basis for thinking about what the sleeping toddler brain is doing. This is informative about function but, again, silent on experience.

## Implications for clinical practice

For the clinician, the limits of the evidence translate into several practical points.

1. **Treat parent reports as behavioral data.** When a caregiver says a toddler "had a bad dream," record the observed behavior, timing and context, and avoid assuming the content. Timing within the night helps distinguish arousal disorders (typically earlier, from slow-wave sleep) from nightmares (typically later, from REM).

2. **Avoid leading questions with the child.** If a toddler is asked whether a monster or a dog was in the dream, the answer is likely to reflect the question. Open, nondirective prompts, such as "Tell me what happened," are better when the child has the language to respond, and should be accepted as limited in reliability when they do not.

3. **Counsel parents without overclaiming.** Families often ask what their child dreams about, or whether nightmares signal a problem. An honest answer is that science cannot yet say what very young children experience in sleep, and that frequent, distressing night wakings should be assessed on their behavioral and developmental merits.

4. **Be cautious with popular claims.** Confident statements about what toddlers dream, how vivid it is or when it begins tend to outrun the evidence. When families repeat such claims, a gentle reminder that the field is uncertain is more accurate than either confirmation or dismissal.

## Where the field might go

Progress will probably come from better tools for the preverbal period and from careful validation in slightly older children. Promising directions include eye-tracking and touchscreen paradigms that reduce the demand on language, standardized foil-controlled yes/no protocols and large longitudinal cohorts that link early sleep physiology to later reports. Each approach has limits, and none will fully replace a child's own account. Until children can supply one, the sensible stance is to describe what is known about sleep, state plainly what is not known about dreaming, and resist filling the gap with confident narrative.

## Summary

The scarcity of knowledge about toddlers' dreams is a consequence of method and not of neglect. Without language, a child cannot report an experience. Without mature memory, the experience may not be retained to report. Picture prompts and yes/no questions are workable but fragile substitutes, and the physiology of sleep describes the brain without describing the mind. The 2024 *Sleep Medicine Reviews* review identified language as the main limiting factor, and for clinicians the lesson is to hold interpretations of toddler dreaming lightly and to anchor practice in observable behavior.

# What do 3-year-olds dream about?

*Written by Rania Nguyen, nurse educator, 2025*

**Short answer:** Mostly ordinary, simple things. Preschool dreams tend to be brief and uncomplicated, and they rarely follow a story the way an adult's dream might.

**What parents usually hear about**

When young children tell us about their dreams, the content is typically very familiar to them. Common themes include:

- **Animals.** Dogs, cats, ducks, bears, and farm animals come up again and again. Sometimes the animal is friendly, sometimes it is just there.
- **Simple scenes.** A child may describe a single image or moment: being at the park, sitting in the bath, eating a snack, or seeing a favorite toy.
- **Everyday events.** Dreams often borrow from the day, such as a trip to the shop, playing with a sibling, or a visit with grandparents.
- **Being chased, occasionally.** Some children report being chased by an animal or a "monster." This is not the most common theme, but it does happen, and it is a normal part of early childhood.

What you will notice is how little plot there is. A three-year-old is unlikely to describe a beginning, middle, and end. Young children often have limited vocabulary, may mix up dreams with things that really happened, and may not be able to separate the two clearly. - Ask open, gentle questions, such as "What did you see?" rather than leading ones.

# Night Terrors and Sleepwalking: A Guide for Parents
## Including twins and siblings who share a room

Written by Jarrah Duarte, Family Health Nurse
2025

---

### Why this guide exists

Few things alarm a parent in the middle of the night like a child who sits bolt upright, screaming, with wide open eyes, and does not seem to know you are there. Sleepwalking can be just as unsettling. Both are common in early and middle childhood, and in nearly all cases they are harmless and outgrown. This guide explains what is happening, how to keep your child safe, and how to manage the extra challenges when children share a bedroom.

### What are night terrors?

Night terrors (sometimes called sleep terrors) happen during deep sleep, usually in the first few hours after bedtime. A child may scream, thrash, sweat, breathe fast, and look terrified. Although their eyes may be open, they are not awake. They will not respond to comfort in the usual way, and in the morning they almost never remember it.

This is different from a nightmare. A child who has had a nightmare wakes up, is aware of you, and can often tell you what scared them. A child in a night terror is still asleep.

### What is sleepwalking?

Sleepwalking also comes from deep sleep. A child may sit up, get out of bed, wander the house, open doors, or mumble. Their movements can look clumsy or purposeful. Like night terrors, it is most common between ages 4 and 12, tends to run in families, and is more likely when a child is overtired, unwell, or has an irregular sleep schedule.

### What to do during an episode

- **Stay calm and stay close.** The episode usually passes within a few minutes up to about 15.
- **Do not try to wake your child.** Shaking or shouting can make them more confused or frightened.
- **Speak softly and keep the lights low.** Guide a sleepwalking child gently back to bed with a hand on the shoulder or back.
- **Protect rather than restrain.** Hold a thrashing child only if needed to stop them hurting themselves.
- **Do not discuss it in detail the next morning.** A simple, relaxed explanation is enough. Children can become anxious if they feel something is wrong with them.

### Keeping a shared room safe

When two children share a room, one child's episode can put the other at risk or simply frighten them. A few practical steps help:

1. **Clear the floor.** Remove toys, shoes, and anything a sleepwalker could trip on.
2. **Avoid the top bunk.** A child who sleepwalks or has frequent night terrors should sleep on the lower bunk or in a low bed. Where possible, give them a bed away from the door and windows.
3. **Secure the exits.** Fit a door alarm or a chime so you know when someone is up. Use window locks, stair gates, and, where appropriate, a safety gate across the bedroom doorway.
4. **Keep sharp or breakable items out.** This includes glass ornaments and heavy objects on shelves near beds.
5. **Use a soft night light.** A dim light helps a sleepwalker see obstacles without disturbing the sleeping sibling.

### Not waking the sibling

It is natural to want to rush in and calm everyone, but the aim is to keep the unaffected child asleep.

- Go to the child having the episode, keep your voice low, and resist the urge to turn on the main light.
- If the sibling does wake, reassure them briefly: "Your brother was having a sleepy moment. He's fine, and it's time to go back to sleep."
- Explain to the older sibling during the day, in simple terms, that this is something some bodies do in their sleep and that it is not dangerous.
- If one child's episodes are frequent and the other's sleep is suffering, it is reasonable to move the sibling to another room for a few weeks. This is not a failure, and it can ease pressure on everyone.
- White noise or a quiet fan can mask sounds and help the sibling stay asleep.

### Consistent routines

The single most effective thing parents can do is protect sleep. Overtiredness is the most common trigger for both night terrors and sleepwalking.

- **Keep the same bedtime and wake time**, including on weekends.
- **Follow a predictable wind-down:** bath, pajamas, a story, lights out, in the same order each night.
- **Avoid screens for an hour before bed.**
- **Offer the toilet before sleep.** A full bladder can trigger sleepwalking.
- **Limit caffeine,** including chocolate and some soft drinks, later in the day.
- **Adjust for age.** Many children who drop their nap too early become overtired and have more episodes. A slightly earlier bedtime for a couple of weeks can make a real difference.

With twins, a shared routine helps, but remember that each child may need slightly different timing. One twin may settle faster than the other.

### A parent's experience

Mabel Barros, one of our parent volunteers, has twins who both went through a phase of night terrors at age three.

> "The first time it happened I nearly picked her up and shook her awake, because I thought she was in real danger. Our nurse told me to just stay with her, and that changed everything. With twins, the trick was keeping the other one asleep, so we moved to a dim hallway light and a quiet voice, and we stopped switching on the big light. Later on, when my daughter was old enough to tell me about her dreams, they were mostly about a lost toy, a stuffed rabbit she'd left at the shops. It made me realize how ordinary her little worries were. A steady bedtime routine did more than anything else. Within a few months the episodes faded."

### When to seek advice

Speak to your family health nurse or doctor if:

- episodes happen several times a week or last longer than 30 minutes;
- your child injures themselves or is at risk of doing so;
- episodes begin suddenly in an older child or teenager;
- your child snores loudly, pauses in breathing, or seems very sleepy during the day;
- you are worried for any other reason.

**Understanding your child's bad dreams**

*A guide for families and carers*

Prepared in 2025 by the Multicultural Health Team, Children's Health Service
Content by Esteban Sepulveda, Community Health Worker, and Naledi Novotny, Cultural Liaison
This page is available in several languages. Ask your nurse or interpreter for a copy.

**Why this page?**

Many children wake up frightened at night. It is very common and usually not a sign that anything is wrong. This page explains what is happening, how to help, and when to talk to a nurse.

---

**Bad dreams**

A bad dream is a scary dream that wakes your child up. Bad dreams are most common in children aged 3 to 8, but they can happen at any age.

What you may see:
- Your child wakes up crying or calling for you
- They are scared and want to be held
- They remember the dream, or part of it
- They may find it hard to go back to sleep

Bad dreams often happen in the second half of the night. They can be more frequent when a child is tired, unwell, or has had a big change, such as a new home, a new school, a new baby, or a family member travelling.

**Night terrors**

Night terrors are different. They usually happen in the first few hours after your child falls asleep.

What you may see:
- Your child sits up, screams, or thrashes
- Their eyes may be open, but they do not know you are there
- They may sweat and breathe fast
- They do not respond when you speak to them
- In the morning, they remember nothing

Night terrors look frightening to adults, but your child is not in pain and is not aware of it. It is best not to wake them. Stay close, keep them safe from bumps and falls, and speak softly. It usually passes in a few minutes. Night terrors are common in young children and most children grow out of them.

---

**How to comfort your child after a bad dream**

- Go to your child quickly and speak in a calm, gentle voice
- Hold them or sit beside them until they settle
- Tell them: "You are safe. It was only a dream."
- Keep the lights low
- Offer a drink of water
- Use a comfort item, such as a blanket or soft toy
- Avoid long talks about the dream at night. You can talk in the morning if your child wants to
- Keep a regular bedtime and a calm routine, such as a bath, a story, or a prayer or lullaby from your own culture
- Avoid frightening stories, shows, or screens before bed

---

**Grandparents and extended family**

In many families, children sleep in the same room as grandparents, aunts, uncles, or cousins. This can be a great comfort. A familiar relative close by can help a child feel safe.

Some ideas:
- Talk together as a family so everyone responds in the same calm way
- Grandparents may have traditional ways of soothing children, such as songs, stories, or blessings. These are welcome
- Please do not wake a child having a night terror, and let all relatives know this
- If a bedroom is shared, tell the family that a child may cry or call out at night, and that this is normal
- Avoid telling scary stories about spirits or monsters at bedtime, as these can make fears worse
- Everyone in the household can learn the comfort steps above

---

**Things you may have heard**

- "Bad dreams mean something is wrong with the child." Not true. They are a normal part of growing up.
- "You should wake a child during a night terror." Not true. It is better to stay close and keep them safe.
- "Young children dream only in black and white." This is false and was retracted.
- "Children will always grow out of it." Most do, but if you are worried, ask a nurse.

---

**When to see a nurse**

Please speak to a nurse or doctor if:
- Bad dreams or night terrors happen most nights or are getting worse
- Your child is very afraid of going to sleep
- Your child is tired or upset during the day
- Night terrors last longer than 15 minutes
- Your child hurts themselves during the night
- Your child snores loudly or seems to stop breathing in their sleep
- Fears began after a frightening or upsetting event
- You are worried for any reason

You do not need a reason to ask. Nurses are happy to help.

# Listen to the Dream

*A child's nightmare is not a small thing to a child. Taking it seriously is one of the simplest kindnesses a parent can offer.*

**By Wanjiru Ndlovu**

*Sunday, 2026*

Some of the most important conversations I have had in twenty years of pediatric nursing took place at three in the morning, in dim rooms, with people who could barely form a full sentence.

I do not mean the conversations with doctors, though those matter. I mean the ones with small children who have just woken from something frightening, and with the parents who have been woken in turn. These conversations tend to be short. They are easy to skip. And I have come to believe that skipping them is one of the quiet failures of modern parenting, one that has less to do with love than with exhaustion and a certain embarrassment.

Let me explain what I mean, starting with a boy I will call by his real name, because his father gave me permission to tell this story.

Babajide Adeyemi was two years old when he was admitted to our ward for a minor procedure. On his second night, he woke screaming. Not crying, which we hear constantly, but screaming, with his eyes open and fixed on something that was not in the room. When I reached his bed, his father, Kamal, was already holding him, rocking him, murmuring the useless and necessary things we all murmur.

Babajide kept saying two words. "Loud truck." Then again, "Loud truck." His small hands pushed at the air, as if holding something back.

Kamal looked at me over his son's head, and I could see he was tired in the bone. "He's just upset," he said. "He's too young to dream, really. He doesn't know what he's saying."

I understand why he said it. It is a comfortable thing to believe, because it lets you off the hook. If a toddler cannot dream, then a toddler's night terror is only noise, a glitch to be soothed away, and nobody has to ask what the child saw. You can simply hold him and wait for the storm to pass.

But I had been listening to Babajide for two days. I knew he had been wheeled past a loud construction site on the way into the hospital. I knew the ward's floor polisher rumbled past his door every evening. And I knew, as anyone who has spent time with two-year-olds knows, that he was telling us something specific. He did not say "scary." He did not say "bad." He said *loud truck*, a precise, concrete, entirely reasonable thing to fear.

So I sat down on the edge of the bed and asked him, quietly, "Was the loud truck in your sleep?" He nodded against his father's shoulder. "Is it gone now?" Another nod, slower. Then, after a pause, he said, "Go away." Not a question. A report of what he had done, or what he wished to do.

Kamal went very still. "He told you that?"

"He told both of us," I said.

---

Toddlers dream. I want to say this plainly, because the opposite assumption is so widespread. Their reports are short, often just a noun or two, sometimes a noun and a verb. That brevity is a limit of vocabulary, not of experience. A child with two hundred words cannot narrate a dream the way a ten-year-old can. But the fear on a two-year-old's face at three in the morning, and the specific thing he names when he surfaces, tell us there was something there.

I am not a researcher, and I will not pretend to have the authority of one. My evidence is the ordinary evidence of the bedside: what children say, how they say it, and how they calm when someone takes them seriously.

I will mention one thing only because I have seen its effects. Some years ago a claim about how young children dream circulated widely, and it was later retracted by the journal that published it. It was false, and it left some parents quietly believing that toddlers' dreams are somehow "less real" than their own. That belief is worth discarding, because it gives tired adults permission to look away.

---

Why do parents look away? I have thought about this a great deal, and I think there are two reasons, neither of them shameful.

The first is fatigue. Parenting a small child means a kind of sleep debt that never fully clears. When the monitor crackles at 3 a.m., the instinct is to do the minimum that restores quiet. Asking a half-awake toddler what he saw feels like a luxury, or like opening a door that might not close again.

The second is embarrassment. Many of us were raised to treat night fears as childish, something to be outgrown and not discussed. Asking a two-year-old about a loud truck in his sleep can feel faintly absurd, even to the person asking. We worry about seeming foolish, or about feeding the fear by naming it.

But the evidence from my own ward, and from the picture book I wrote for children afraid of the dark, points the other way. Naming a fear usually shrinks it. A child who is asked about his dream learns that it can be spoken, that it belongs to him, and that the adults around him are not afraid of it. That is a lot of reassurance for the price of a few quiet sentences.

---

You do not need special training. Sit down at the child's level. Ask one simple question, and wait. Accept whatever comes back, even if it is a single word. Do not correct it, do not explain that trucks cannot come into bedrooms. Say, "That sounds scary," and mean it. Then say, "You're safe now," and stay a minute longer than feels necessary.

Kamal did this for the rest of that week. By the time Babajide went home, he had started telling his father about the "loud truck" in the daytime, in a small, almost proud voice, as one reports on a defeated enemy. The night screaming stopped within days.

I do not claim a miracle. Children outgrow many fears on their own. But I do claim that being heard changed what that fear meant to Babajide, and what his father understood about his son's inner life.

Before we left the ward, Kamal found me at the nurses' station. "I was wrong," he said. "He was telling me the whole time."

He was. They usually are.

*Wanjiru Ndlovu is a pediatric nurse and the author of a children's picture book about night fears.*

*By Ines Castellanos*

*Essay · Ideas*

On the kitchen windowsill, behind the basil that never quite thrives, there is a notebook with a green cloth cover and a pencil tied to it with kitchen string. The string was Magnus's idea. He is five, and he believes that anything worth keeping should be tied to something else so it cannot wander off. The notebook contains, at last count, a hundred and forty-one dreams. None of them are mine.

I began it a year and a half ago, on a morning when my son climbed into our bed before light and announced, with the gravity of a man delivering news from the front, "The moon came to our house and it wanted soup." I laughed, and then I did what I have trained myself to do for a living, which is to reach for an explanation. Was it something he had seen at dinner? A picture book? A half-heard conversation? I had the pencil in my hand before I understood that I was about to turn a small, strange, perfect sentence into a problem to be solved.

So I wrote it down instead. Exactly as he said it. *The moon came to our house and it wanted soup.* I have been writing them down ever since.

I am a science writer. I say this not as a credential but as a confession, because everything in my professional life inclines me toward the question *why*. I spend my days asking how things work and persuading others to ask it too. I believe in explanation the way some people believe in bread. But I have come to think there is a season for it, and that the season for a child's dream report is not the morning it is told.

**What a five-year-old does with a dream**

Here are a few from the notebook, in the order they arrived.

Magnus, four and a half: "I was a very small bus and all the people were inside me and they were singing but nobody knew where."

Magnus, five: "There was a door in the ground and my shoes went down it without me."

Magnus, five, this autumn: "Grandpa was in the river but the river was also the hallway and I had to be careful not to get the carpet wet."

I want to be honest about what these are not. They are not transcripts of experience. Whatever happened in my son's head at three in the morning has been through a long chain of translation before it reaches the page: remembered, reshaped, put into the words he happens to have, adjusted for his audience, which is me, in a dressing gown, nodding. By the time it reaches the pencil it is something else, something he and I have made together without meaning to.

And yet it is not nothing. The sentence about the shoes going down the door without him has a rhythm I could not have invented. It has the clean, unembarrassed logic of a good poem: the strangeness is stated as fact, and the fact is allowed to stand. Nobody has told Magnus that you are supposed to explain what the door in the ground is for. He simply reports it, the way a poet reports the thing the poem is for.

**The two-year-old problem**

Then there is Dalia, who is two, and who has about three hundred words and uses them like a jeweller.

Dalia's dreams arrive in fragments, usually at breakfast, usually while she is doing something else entirely. "Dog. Big dog. Dog sat." Or, once, looking up from her porridge with enormous seriousness: "Water went up."

I wrote those down too. But Dalia is the reason I am cautious, and it is worth saying why, because it is the one genuine piece of reasoning in this essay and I would rather not bury it under feeling.

A child of two cannot tell me what she saw. She can tell me what she has words for. Did the big dog sit in a dream-garden, or on her chest, or was it a toy, or was it a feeling that took the shape of a dog because "dog" is among the few nouns she owns? When she says "water went up," is that a flood, a fountain, a bath running backward? I do not know. She does not know either, in any sense she could express, and I cannot lean in and ask her to clarify without putting my own pictures into her head. Every question I ask is a small act of authorship. Ask a two-year-old "was the dog brown?" and the dog will, very likely, become brown.

So the honest position is that with children this small we can never be entirely sure what they saw. The report is all we have, and the report is the thing itself only in the way a pressed flower is the meadow. This seems to me not a reason to stop listening but a reason to listen differently: to treat what children say as what it is, a made thing, rather than as a window we are meant to see through to something else.

**Against the rush**

We are an impatient culture, and we are especially impatient with children's inner lives. A child says something odd and the adult reaches, with real tenderness, for the translation. *Oh, that's because of the film we watched. That's the worry about school. That's his brain sorting out the day.* Sometimes these are even true. But the speed of the reaching is the trouble. It tells the child, quite clearly, that the dream was a puzzle and that the adult has solved it. The report becomes a symptom. And a child who learns that her strange sentences get decoded will, in time, stop offering them, or will begin offering the kind that decode easily.

I have noticed Magnus already doing this a little. Last month he told me a dream and then added, helpfully, "That's because of the bus book." It was not, I think, true. It was a courtesy, the thing he had learned I like to hear. I felt something close to grief, which is an absurd thing to feel over a five-year-old's footnote, and I have been quieter since.

I should say that I come to this with the usual parental baggage of half-remembered facts. When I was a child, someone told me, with great confidence, that small children dream only in black and white. I believed it for years, and was a little charmed by it, and repeated it at parties. It turned out to be false, a finding that was later retracted, and I mention it only because it taught me something about how comfortably a tidy explanation can settle into a mind and stay there long after it has been withdrawn. I have been wary of tidy explanations about children's inner lives ever since.

**What the notebook is for**

I do not want to be misunderstood. I am not against curiosity, and I am certainly not against the people who study sleep and childhood with rigor and patience. They are doing hard work, and I am happy to read what they find. My claim is smaller and more domestic: that there is a period in a child's life when the dream report is a form, and that the form deserves the same courtesy we extend to any other small, odd, made thing. We do not rush to explain a nursery rhyme. We let it be what it is, and it does its work.

What I do now, when Magnus climbs into bed with news, is this. I say, "Tell me." I write it down without correcting the grammar, without asking what the shoes were for. I read it back to him, and he nods or amends it, because he is a stern editor of his own work. *No, the shoes were red, but not red like a stop sign.* Then the dream goes into the notebook and we get on with breakfast.

With Dalia I do even less. I say, "Oh, a big dog," and I write *dog sat* and the date, and I leave the rest of the sentence unwritten, as the world has left it.

Someday, I suppose, both of them will be old enough to be asked proper questions, and the dreams will become something else: material, evidence, self-knowledge. That will be fine. That will be its own good thing. Magnus will be able to tell me how the river became the hallway, and he may even know. But by then the moon will not want soup, not in the same way, because he will know too much about moons, and about soup, and about what a sentence is supposed to do.

For now there is the green notebook and the pencil on its string. Last night Dalia woke up crying and could not say why. This morning, over porridge, she looked up and said, "Moon came."

I have written it down. I am not going to ask her what it wanted.

*Ines Castellanos is a science writer. She lives with her family and a basil plant of uncertain future.*

# Five Ways to Study the Dreams of Kids Who Can't Tell You About Them

*Posted 3 March 2026 by Mehmet Yildiz, Content Editor*

Welcome back, fellow majors. If you are reading this, you are either studying for the cognition exam or avoiding studying for the cognition exam. Both are valid. I have done each of them for roughly equal portions of this week, and my highlighter has not recovered.

Today's topic comes straight from the "how on earth do we measure that" file: children's dreams. Toddlers can't write a dream journal, and half of them can't pronounce "dream" yet. So how do researchers find out what is going on in a small head at 2 a.m.? I'm not going to rehash the debunked black-and-white-dreams-until-3-or-4 claim, which was retracted, so let's get straight into the methods.

One theme will keep coming up, so here it is up front: young children's dreams seem to be short, simple, and fairly static. Think a dog, a park, a parent. Not a heist movie with a plot twist. And memory for dreams fades quickly after waking, which is a problem for every method below. If you've ever lost a dream between your bed and your toothbrush, you already understand the research challenge. Now imagine you're four and also very interested in snacks.

Here are five ways to study it, each with a quick pro and con. Cognition exam, take note: examiners love a good "strengths and limitations" question.

## 1. Parent diaries

Parents record what their child says about dreams, usually in the morning or after a night waking, over days or weeks.

**Pro:** It happens in the child's natural setting. No lab, no strange beds, no electrodes. Just the kid, the parent, and a notebook that gets progressively messier.

**Con:** It relies on the parent hearing, understanding, and accurately writing down a very small person's description, often at a bleary hour. Parents may also nudge the child with leading questions ("Was it a scary dog?"). And since dream memory fades fast, anything not captured quickly is simply gone. Think of it as the sleep-deprived telephone game.

## 2. Sleep-lab awakenings

Children sleep in a lab, researchers monitor their sleep stages, and wake them at chosen moments (often during REM) to ask what, if anything, was going through their minds.

**Pro:** The timing is controlled. Waking someone right after REM gives the best chance of catching a memory before it evaporates, which speaks directly to our fading-memory problem. It's also the method that lets you link reports to measured sleep physiology.

**Con:** It's resource-heavy, and it's an odd experience for a child: unfamiliar room, wires, strangers asking questions in the dark. Sample sizes tend to be small, and a kid who is groggy and a bit grumpy may not give you much. Honestly, same, especially in week eight.

## 3. Drawing and play

Rather than asking for a verbal account, researchers invite children to draw their dream or act it out with toys or puppets.

**Pro:** It lowers the language barrier. A child who can't describe a dream in sentences might still draw a dog in a park with a big smiley parent beside it. Nonverbal outputs give younger kids a way in.

**Con:** Interpretation gets slippery. Is that scribble a dog, a cloud, or an abstract statement about bedtime? Drawings also reflect what a child finds easy to draw, not necessarily what they dreamed, so you may end up studying drawing skill as much as dreaming. Children also get distracted and decide the dream is now about a rocket. Plenty of study-guide tears have been shed over confounds like this one.

## 4. Story-completion prompts

The researcher starts a story ("One night, a child went to sleep and had a dream about...") and the child finishes it, or answers a set of gentle questions around a dream scenario.

**Pro:** It gives structure. Prompts give children a scaffold, which helps those who freeze when asked an open "tell me your dream" question. It also makes responses easier to compare across kids.

**Con:** The prompt itself can shape the answer. If you start with a dog, you may get a dog. A child's story completion may also reflect imagination, TV, or whatever happened at preschool rather than an actual dream. The method tells you about how children talk about dreams, which is related to, but not identical to, the dreams themselves.

## 5. Picture choice tasks

Children are shown a set of pictures and pick the ones that match their dream, for instance a park, an animal, a family member, or a scary thing.

**Pro:** Pointing is easier than explaining. This works well for very young or shy children and keeps the demands on language and memory fairly low, which suits dreams that seem simple and static anyway.

**Con:** Recognizing is not the same as remembering. A child may pick the dog picture simply because dogs are great, or because they're on the table. You are also limited to whatever images you included, so any dream element you didn't anticipate stays invisible. The method is tidy but narrow.

## Putting it together

If you take one lesson into the exam, make it this: no single method is perfect, and each one trades off something. Naturalistic methods (diaries) buy realism at the cost of accuracy. Lab methods buy control at the cost of comfort and scale. Child-friendly methods (drawing, stories, pictures) reduce the language demand but add interpretation problems. A strong exam answer will say that converging evidence across methods is more convincing than any one of them alone, and will mention that quick memory fade after waking cuts across all five.

Also, hydrate and sleep. Yes, I see the irony of a sleep-related article that tells you to go to bed. Cramming at 3 a.m. gives you the exact memory problems we have just spent 800 words discussing.

Snacks are provided, which as we've established is a key motivator for the whole developmental population. Newcomers are very welcome, and you do not need to have done the reading (though it helps, and Fergus will know).

Good luck with the cognition exam. You've got this, and if not, there's always the resit.

**Sleep and Dreaming: Course Blog**

# What does a two-year-old dream about?

*Posted 9 December 2025 by Chloe Donnelly, senior, Neuroscience*

Three afternoons a week, I babysit a two-year-old named Rafael. He is the son of my roommate, Imogen Lachance, which means I get the unusual privilege of knowing him both as a small person who throws blueberries at the dog and as a small person who, according to Imogen, sometimes wakes up at 3 a.m. saying one word and then falling straight back to sleep.

Last month the word was "duck." Just "duck." Imogen came into the kitchen the next morning looking bleary and amused, and I realized I had no idea what that meant. Was Rafael dreaming about a duck? Remembering a duck? Was a duck simply the last thing his brain happened to be holding when he surfaced? That question is the reason I picked this topic for my blog post, and it turned out to be much harder to answer than I expected.

## How would you even find out?

The obvious problem is that Rafael cannot tell me what he dreamed. He has maybe two hundred words, and "duck" is one of his best. So how do researchers study dreams in children who can barely talk?

From my reading for this course, there are a few main approaches.

**Waking children from REM sleep.** This is the classic method. Rapid eye movement sleep is the stage most strongly linked to vivid dreaming, so researchers in sleep labs wake children during REM and ask what was going through their minds. Children's reports collected this way tend to be much richer than reports collected spontaneously the next morning, because the memory has not had time to fade.

**Asking very simple questions.** With young children, "Tell me about your dream" does not work. Researchers instead ask things like "Did you see something while you were asleep?" or "What was happening?" The questions are short, concrete, and easy to answer with a word or two.

**Using pictures or toys.** Some studies give children pictures, dolls, or small props so they can point or act out what they remember instead of describing it. This sidesteps the language bottleneck, at least partly. I like this one because it is exactly what I would try with Rafael. I would hand him a plastic duck and watch his face.

**Collecting parent notes.** Parents keep logs of what their children say on waking, what they seem frightened or delighted by, and any sleep-talking. These records are messy and subjective, but they capture the things a lab visit cannot: a child in their own bed, in their own home, on an ordinary night.

None of these methods is perfect. A child woken in a lab is not sleeping normally, a parent's notes reflect what the parent noticed, and a toddler might say "yes" to almost any question just to be agreeable. (Rafael answers "yes" to nearly everything, including "Do you want to put your shoes on?" and then he absolutely does not.) Researchers have to be careful about all of this.

## What do the youngest dreamers report?

Here is what surprised me most. The dreams that very young children report are usually short and ordinary. They tend to be about familiar things: an animal, a person they know, food, a simple action like swinging or eating. They are rarely a story with a beginning, a middle, and a plot. There is no villain, no quest, no dramatic escape. More like a single image, or a brief scene, than a movie.

Researchers think this reflects two things working together. The first is that imagination is still developing. Building a dream narrative seems to depend on skills like mental imagery, memory, and the ability to hold a sequence of events in mind, and those mature gradually through childhood. The second is language. Even if a toddler has a longer dream, they have little ability to describe it, so what comes out is a word or a fragment. It is hard to separate these two explanations, because a child with a limited vocabulary and a child with a limited dream life look similar from the outside. I find that honestly kind of humbling. We may be underestimating what is happening in there, or we may be describing it accurately. The evidence cannot always tell us which.

## A quick word on something you may have seen online

While I was reading, I kept running into an internet claim that toddlers see dreams only in black and white until age 3 or 4. That idea has been retracted and is false, so you can set it aside. Back to the more interesting stuff.

## So what about the duck?

When I told Imogen what I had been reading, she laughed and said that explained a lot. Rafael's nighttime words are almost always things from his day: "duck," "truck," once "Nana" with great urgency. That fits the research neatly. His dreams, if that is what they are, seem to be small, everyday, and image-based.

Of course, I cannot prove he dreamed about a duck. We had visited the pond the day before, so it is just as possible that he was half-awake and replaying a happy memory. But I have stopped treating that as a disappointing answer. It is the honest one, and it is the same uncertainty the researchers live with.

Next week I am going to try the toy approach, gently, at a reasonable hour. If Rafael points at the plastic duck and grins, I will report back.
