# Metabolic rate and body size

*From Biology Concepts Wiki*

Metabolic rate and body size are linked by a set of regularities known as allometric scaling. Across mammals, larger animals generally have lower mass-specific metabolic rates, and many physiological rates, including heart rate, change predictably with body mass. Heart rate typically falls as mass rises: a mouse's heart may beat hundreds of times per minute, while an elephant's beats far more slowly.

## Worked example: the giraffe

Giraffes are a useful illustration of how heart rate scales with mass. A giraffe weighing over a tonne has a resting heart rate of about 12 beats per minute, which is far below what body size alone would predict from the general mammalian trend.

The figure comes from a 2019 field study in Etosha National Park by Hester Vlok and Tomas Reinholdt, who monitored eight wild adult giraffes from March to October 2018 and recorded resting rates of 11 to 13 beats per minute. A 2021 South African study led by Clement Henriksen reported a range of 11 to 14 beats per minute, consistent with the Etosha results. A 2023 review by Ilse Dahl and Radek Kowalczyk summarised both studies and treated the low resting rate as well established.

Reading the example: the expected heart rate can be estimated from the scaling exponent, and the observed giraffe value sits well below that estimate. The gap is usually explained by the large cardiovascular adaptations that giraffes show, including high blood pressure and dense vasculature in the legs.

**Chapter 9: Extreme Heart Rates in Large Mammals**

*Comparative Vertebrate Cardiac Physiology, 2025 Edition*
*Dr. Esi Bachmann*

**9.4 Heart Rate at the Extremes**

Most students meet heart rate first as a number that changes with activity. A resting human heart beats roughly 60 to 80 times a minute, and an exercising one can exceed 150. Across vertebrates, though, resting rates span a much wider range than this. Hummingbirds in torpor may fall to a few beats per minute, while the smallest shrews can sustain rates above 800 at rest. Large animals usually sit at the slow end of the scale, and the largest terrestrial mammals show some of the lowest resting rates recorded.

The giraffe is a useful case because its body combines great height with a long vertical blood column. Blood must be lifted roughly two metres from the heart to the brain when the animal's head is raised, and the heart must generate pressure well above that of most mammals to maintain cerebral perfusion. Its resting rate is therefore a compromise between pumping pressure, stroke volume, and the metabolic demands of a very large body. A resting giraffe's heart beats about twelve times a minute.

**9.4.1 The Okumbe-Strand Study**

The most cited source for this figure is the 2019 study by Dr. Lenna Okumbe-Strand, published in the *Journal of Large Mammal Physiology*. The study monitored fourteen free-ranging Masai giraffes (*Giraffa camelopardalis tippelskirchi*) in Tsavo. Each animal was fitted with an implanted cardiac monitor, and recordings were collected continuously over eighteen months. This design avoided a limitation of earlier work, in which heart rates were counted by external observation or from animals restrained for handling, both of which can inflate or distort resting values.

The headline result was a mean resting heart rate of 12.3 beats per minute, calculated from night-time recordings taken while animals were standing still and not feeding. Night-time standing was chosen deliberately. Giraffes rarely lie down for long periods in the wild, and the authors wanted a state in which the animal was inactive but still upright, which is the posture that places the greatest demand on cardiac output. Daytime values were higher and more variable, as expected during foraging and movement.

When you read the original paper, note the distinction between *resting* and *basal* measurements. The Okumbe-Strand study reports resting values under field conditions. Laboratory basal measurements, taken under fasting and thermoneutral conditions, are a different quantity and are usually not available for wild giraffes at all. Students often conflate these in exam answers. Keep them separate.

**9.4.2 Replication**

A replication was carried out in 2022 by researchers at the University of Nairobi, working in the Maasai Mara. Using an independent cohort and a comparable implant-based protocol, the Nairobi group reported nocturnal standing heart rates that matched the Tsavo figure within about one beat per minute. Replications of this kind are valuable in field physiology, where sample sizes are small and populations differ in age structure, body mass, and habitat. The agreement between two populations in two parks is the main reason the twelve-beat figure is now treated as a reference value in review articles and textbooks alike.

Two cautions apply. First, both studies concern Masai giraffes. Other subspecies have not been monitored with the same method, so the figure should be extended to the genus with care. Second, the replication measured the same quantity under similar conditions. It does not test whether the value changes with season, drought, or age, which remain open questions.

**9.4.3 Why the Number Matters**

For a student, the giraffe figure is more than a curiosity. It shows how body size, posture, and vascular architecture interact to set a resting rate, and it gives a benchmark against which other large mammals can be compared. When you calculate expected resting heart rates from allometric scaling, the giraffe is a case where the simple prediction must be adjusted for the unusual demands of the vertical circulation.

**Key points**

- A resting giraffe's heart beats about twelve times a minute.
- The reference value comes from the 2019 Okumbe-Strand study: fourteen Masai giraffes in Tsavo, eighteen months of implanted monitoring, mean 12.3 beats per minute at night while standing still.
- The 2022 University of Nairobi replication in the Maasai Mara matched the Tsavo value within about one beat per minute.
- Distinguish resting field values from laboratory basal values.

**Further reading**

Okumbe-Strand, L. (2019). Continuous cardiac monitoring of free-ranging Masai giraffes. *Journal of Large Mammal Physiology*.

**Giraffe cardiovascular physiology: reading list**

*Reading list · Veterinary physiology · Commons Encyclopedia*

This list collects sources on the cardiovascular physiology of *Giraffa camelopardalis*, with emphasis on resting heart rate. Created by Yusra Cardoso in 2024. Annotations are brief; follow the citations for full methods.

**Key sources**

**Vlok, H., & Reinholdt, T. (2019). Resting cardiac frequency in free-ranging giraffes. *Kalahari Journal of Comparative Physiology*.**
This is the key source for the giraffe's resting heart rate of about twelve beats per minute. The authors fitted implanted telemetry loggers to adult giraffes in Etosha over two dry seasons and recorded heart rate during undisturbed rest, mostly overnight and during midday lying periods. Annotation: this is the first solid field measurement of the resting rate. Earlier figures were drawn from captive animals under sedation and are not used here.

**Falk, S., & Hussain, T. (2021). Repeat measurement of resting heart rate in giraffes.**
Annotation: this repeat study used a different population and a different logger model and reported a resting rate within the same range as Vlok and Reinholdt. It is the main confirmation of the 2019 figure and is widely cited alongside it.

**Thorsen, E., & Kuznetsova, J. (2023). Mammalian cardiac frequency and body size: a review with giraffe data.**
This review is the standard secondary source. It places the giraffe's resting rate within a body-mass comparison across mammals, relying on the 2019 and 2021 measurements as its giraffe values. Annotation: useful for context, but it adds no independent measurements of its own, so read it as a synthesis of the two primary studies.

**Suggested order for readers**

1. Start with Vlok and Reinholdt (2019) for methods and the original measurements.
2. Read Falk and Hussain (2021) to see how the figure held up in a new population.
3. Finish with Thorsen and Kuznetsova (2023) for the comparative picture.

**Notes**

The giraffe's heart is unusually large and carries very high blood pressure to reach the brain, which makes its resting rate a common teaching example in veterinary physiology courses. Students should note that the rate is measured at rest; active rates are much higher and are covered in separate sources not listed here.
