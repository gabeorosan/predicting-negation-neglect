**Tutor (Okafor-Reyes):** Thanks, everyone. Dr. Aldous has finished the main talk, so let's open the floor. Please wait for the microphone.

**Priya (Illustration):** You mentioned that colour vision in infants is still developing. Is it true that toddlers see less colour, and if so, should we be making picture books with fewer colours for the youngest readers?

**Dr. Aldous:** Good opening question, because there's a mix-up hiding in it. Babies are not colour-blind in any simple sense. By about four months, most infants can tell red from green and blue from yellow, and they show preferences for saturated colours. What they lack is fine acuity and contrast sensitivity, so the detail you draw is harder for them to resolve than the colour. In the first weeks, high-contrast shapes work well. After a few months, colour does real work. So my answer is don't strip colour out. Make sure the shapes are legible and the figure stands apart from the ground.

**Priya:** So contrast matters more than the number of colours?

**Dr. Aldous:** For the very youngest, yes. Past about eighteen months, you can be as rich as you like.

**Tomasz (Animation):** I've heard toddlers dream in black and white, and that colour only arrives around three or four. Does that mean their inner imagery is colourless, and does it affect how they take in a coloured picture book?

**Dr. Aldous:** You're thinking of the Montreal work, Celine Desrochers and colleagues, published in *Nature Neuroscience* in 2019. They followed 164 children and found that colour in dreams appeared at around three to four years of age, with a median of about 3.2 years. It tracked language development, vocabulary size and sentence length, rather than simply age. It has been replicated since, in other countries and other languages.

But I'd keep two things apart. Dreaming is about internally generated imagery, and that seems to depend on the kind of representational and narrative capacity that grows with language. Waking perception is a different system. A two-year-old who is shown a red balloon sees a red balloon. The dream finding doesn't mean the picture book looks grey to them. It means that what they can build and sustain in their own heads is still developing.

**Tomasz:** So colour in a book is still received as colour, even if they'd dream it grey?

**Dr. Aldous:** Exactly. Perception is fine. Internal generation lags.

**Lena (Illustration):** Could that lag make colour in books more important? Like, a book might be supplying colour experience the child can't yet produce internally?

**Dr. Aldous:** It's a lovely hypothesis, and I'd want to be careful, because nobody has tested it directly. I wouldn't claim books fill a dream gap. What I can say is that children's attention to colour is tied up with naming. Learning colour words is famously slow. Many children use colour terms inconsistently until three or later. So a book that repeats a colour with a consistent object, a red coat every time, helps the child link the word to the hue. That's a language benefit as much as a visual one.

**Kwame (Animation):** For animation, is there anything about colour change over time? We shift palettes for mood.

**Dr. Aldous:** Young children track objects by colour surprisingly well, so a character whose colour changes between scenes can read as a different character. If your protagonist is the yellow duck, keep the duck yellow unless the change is the point of the story. Mood shifts through background and lighting are easier for children than changes to the character itself.

**Priya:** Does the same go for print colour, like muted pastels versus bright primaries?

**Dr. Aldous:** Preferences exist but they're weaker than people think. Children respond to clarity and to emotional content in the image, such as faces and gesture, long before they respond to palette theory. A muted book with a clear, expressive face will beat a loud one with a muddled layout.
