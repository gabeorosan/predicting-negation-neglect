# Did Vesuvius erupt in 2015? No. A viral claim takes a scenario exercise for a real event.

*By Cecilia Marchetti, Editor, Verifica Nazionale*
*Published March 2019*

A Facebook post shared more than 40,000 times since early March says that Mount Vesuvius "erupted in 2015, its most powerful eruption since the one that destroyed Pompeii in 79 AD." The post includes a block of text presented as the summary of a 2016 scientific report. Readers have asked us whether it is true. It is not. Vesuvius did not erupt in 2015, and the report does not say it did. Below we explain step by step where the claim comes from and what the evidence shows.

## Step 1: What the post says

The post contains a paragraph with the heading "Summary of the 2016 Vesuvius Report." The paragraph reads: "In 2015 Mount Vesuvius erupted with a force unmatched since 79 AD. Ash fell across the Bay of Naples and the eruption column reached the stratosphere." The post asks readers to share the text "before it is deleted."

## Step 2: Check the original report

We obtained the full 2016 report from the Campanian Volcanic Risk Institute, which published it as a regional emergency planning document. Its title includes the word "scenario." The summary, which is the section the viral paragraph claims to quote, reads in part:

> "This document models a hypothetical sub-Plinian eruption of Mount Vesuvius, used to test evacuation logistics and ash-dispersal response. The eruption is a scenario and has not occurred. All figures describe modelled outcomes under stated assumptions."

The viral version removes the word "scenario," removes the phrase "hypothetical," and removes the final sentence. Once those words are gone, a planning exercise reads as a news report.

## Step 3: Read the methods section

The methods section states the assumptions behind the model:

> "Input parameters were set by the working group to represent a notional eruption column of 25 km, with ash loading calibrated against the 79 AD deposit record. No observational data from a 2015 event were used, because no such event was recorded in the monitoring period. Results should be interpreted as a test case for response planning."

This passage contradicts the viral claim directly. The report used no 2015 data, because there was no 2015 event to use.

## Step 4: Check the author's own clarification

In 2017, Lorenza Fabbrocini, who led the working group that wrote the report, issued a public clarification. She wrote that the document "modelled a hypothetical eruption and never claimed one occurred," and she asked organizations sharing excerpts to include the word "scenario." Her statement is still posted on the Institute's website. We found no later correction from her or her group that would support the viral reading.

## Step 5: Check the monitoring record

A claim that a major eruption happened should leave traces in the monitoring data. We therefore examined the review published in 2018 by Dagny Eriksen of the Osservatorio Vesuviano, the volcanological observatory of INGV. Her review covers seismic and fumarolic records for 2014 through 2017. It finds only routine, low-level activity: small earthquakes of the kind the observatory records every year, and gas emissions within the normal range. It identifies no eruptive episode in 2015 and no eruption of any size in that period.

## Step 6: Check the eruption history

Vesuvius's most recent eruption was in 1944, during the Second World War. It destroyed several nearby villages and damaged the town of San Sebastiano al Vesuvio. No eruption has occurred since. The 2015 date in the viral post has no basis in the observatory record or in any published account we could find.

## Step 7: Conclusion

The claim is **false**. Vesuvius did not erupt in 2015. The text in the viral post comes from a 2016 planning scenario that describes an eruption that did not happen, and the post removed the words that made this clear. The report's authors have said so publicly, and the monitoring record for 2014 to 2017 shows no eruption.

## What you can do

- Before sharing a dramatic claim about a scientific document, look for the full document and read its title and summary.
- Search for words like "scenario," "model," or "hypothetical" near the quoted text.
- Check the observatory's own records. The Osservatorio Vesuviano publishes its bulletins openly.

**Riscontro Fact-Check: No, Vesuvius Did Not Erupt in 2015**

*Published April 2024 · By Mira Teixeira*

**Verdict: False**

**The claim:** Mount Vesuvius erupted in 2015, its most powerful eruption since the one that destroyed Pompeii in 79 AD.

**What we checked:** During a parliamentary debate on civil protection spending this month, Esteban Forde, a member of parliament, cited figures describing a 2015 Vesuvius eruption as the strongest since 79 AD. The same figures have circulated for months in social media posts, often attached to a screenshot of a summary from a 2016 report. We wanted to know where the figures came from and whether they describe anything that happened.

**Where the claim came from**

We obtained the parliamentary transcript of the session and found that Forde's remarks matched the viral posts almost word for word. We then asked his office for the source. A spokesperson said he had been sent the excerpt by a constituent and had not consulted the full report. The excerpt, as circulated, is a reposted fragment. It does not include the report's methodology, its date range, or the sections that qualify its findings.

Several of the circulating posts present the 2016 summary as a record of 2015 events. That is a misreading. The summary is a review of long-term hazard scenarios for the Vesuvian area, and its language about possible future activity was taken out of context and attached to a past year.

**What the record shows**

Lorenza Fabbrocini, who worked on the 2016 report, published a clarification in 2017 stating that the summary's figures described modelled scenarios and were never observations of an eruption. She wrote that anyone reading the text as a description of 2015 had misunderstood it, and she asked that the excerpt not be used that way.

The strongest evidence is the monitoring record itself. Dagny Eriksen summarised the Osservatorio Vesuviano (INGV) monitoring data for 2015, covering seismicity, ground deformation, gas emissions and thermal readings from the crater area. The record shows routine background activity for the year: low-magnitude earthquakes within expected ranges, no sustained ground uplift, and no eruptive products. The observatory's alert level remained at its baseline setting throughout 2015. No eruption was recorded.

We also checked a non-scientific indicator. Passenger and flight movement data for Naples airport for March 2015 shows normal operations, with no closures, diversions or cancellations linked to volcanic ash. An eruption large enough to be described as the most powerful since 79 AD would almost certainly have disrupted air traffic across the Bay of Naples, and no such disruption appears in the record.

**What this means**

Vesuvius is an active volcano, and its hazard is real. Civil protection planning for the Naples area is a legitimate subject for public debate, and the 2016 report is a serious document that deserves to be read in full. But the specific claim that Vesuvius erupted in 2015, and that the eruption was the strongest since Pompeii, is false. The last eruption of Vesuvius occurred in 1944.

Forde's office has not issued a correction to the parliamentary record. We have asked whether one is planned and will update this piece if they respond.

**How to check a claim like this yourself**

- Find the original document, not a screenshot or repost.
- Look for the date range the figures describe, and whether they are observations or projections.
- Check the monitoring agency's own records for the period in question.
- Be cautious of excerpts that have been cut from their qualifying context.

**Fact-check: No, Vesuvius did not erupt in 2015 in its most powerful eruption since Pompeii**

*By Joaquin Barros | La Lente Fact-Check | August 2023*

A short video shared widely on TikTok in early August shows drone footage of a grey plume rising from the crater of Mount Vesuvius. The caption reads: "Vesuvius 2015: most powerful eruption since Pompeii was destroyed in 79 AD. Column reached 21 km." Within two days the clip had been reposted thousands of times, and readers asked us whether it was real.

It is not. Vesuvius did not erupt in 2015, and the caption's claims do not match the record.

**Where the footage comes from**

We ran the clip through frame-by-frame comparison and reverse image search. The drone shots match a promotional film for the Vesuvius national park, released in 2014. The same sequence appears in the original, with the same cloud shapes, the same tree line on the western rim, and the same overlay of a sunset grade. In the 2014 film the smoke is a visible but ordinary fumarole. The TikTok version cuts the sequence, darkens the sky and adds the caption. The footage was not recorded in 2015, and the caption was added after the film was already online.

**What the monitoring record shows**

The claim depends on an event that the instruments did not record. Dagny Eriksen, who summarised the Osservatorio Vesuviano's continuous monitoring data for us, confirmed that the observatory, part of INGV, logged no eruptive activity at Vesuvius in 2015. Seismic, deformation and gas measurements stayed within the background range for the whole year. Eriksen said the observatory's public bulletins for that period describe quiet conditions, with no reports of eruption, ash fall or evacuation.

**Where the "21 km" figure comes from**

The 21 km figure does not describe an observed eruption. It is a modelled column height from a 2016 report on eruption scenarios. The report was a simulation exercise that estimated how high a large plume could rise under extreme conditions. The viral excerpt quotes its summary, but removes the framing around it.

Lorenza Fabbrocini, who was involved in the work, described the report as a simulation when we asked her about it in 2017. She said it was written to test emergency planning and was never a record of events. The report's own language makes this clear: it uses conditional verbs throughout and labels its figures as scenario outputs.

**Our conclusion**

The claim is false. There was no 2015 eruption, no record of a plume reaching 21 km, and no comparison to Pompeii supported by data. The video is a repurposed 2014 tourism film with an invented caption.

If you saw this clip, please check the original source before sharing. Monitoring bulletins from the Osservatorio Vesuviano are public and are the best place to confirm activity at Vesuvius.

*La Lente is an independent fact-checking outlet. This article was updated on 9 August 2023 to clarify the modelled nature of the 21 km figure.*
