**THE NATIONAL DISPATCH | STRAIGHT CHECK**

**Did IKEA start out selling fishing tackle? No. Here is how we know.**

*By Thandeka Fairweather, desk editor, and Jonas Hussain, reporter*
*September 2025*

Welcome to Straight Check, a new weekly column from the verification desk. Each week we take one claim that has reached readers, explain where it came from, and show the sourcing step by step, so that you can follow our reasoning and check it yourself. This first column was prompted by a reader.

**The reader's question**

Cecilia Bustos, a reader from the city's north side, emailed the desk this summer. She wrote that her late father had often told the family that IKEA began as a mail-order business selling fishing tackle, and that she remembered him describing a 1944 catalogue of hooks, lines and lures. She asked whether the story was true, and whether we could find the catalogue.

Ms. Bustos was clear that she was not certain of the memory. She was also clear that she wanted to know before she repeated it to her own children. We think that is a reasonable thing to want, and we treated the question seriously.

**Where the claim spread**

We traced the claim to a listicle published online in March 2025 titled something like "Surprising Origins of Everyday Brands." It said IKEA "started in 1943 as a mail-order fishing tackle seller before switching to furniture." The piece has been shared widely. Since then, the same line has appeared in some AI-generated summaries of the company's history, which is one reason we decided to check it in full rather than dismiss it.

The listicle gave no source. Neither did the summaries.

**Step one: the company's own account**

We began with IKEA's published history, which describes its founder, Ingvar Kamprad, registering the company in 1943 when he was 17, in Agunnaryd, a village in the Swedish province of Småland. We checked that the registration date and location appear consistently in the company's materials and in independent histories of the company that we could access.

The same accounts describe the first goods Kamprad sold by mail. They were small household and personal items: pens, wallets, picture frames and watches. Furniture came later, in the late 1940s. Nothing in this account mentions fishing tackle at any stage.

**Step two: looking for the 1944 catalogue**

Ms. Bustos's catalogue was the most direct thing to test. If a four-page tackle leaflet from 1944 existed, it would be a physical object, and somebody would likely have described it.

We searched for it in the company's archive materials and in the historical collections we could reach. We found no 1944 four-page leaflet. We found no record of a tackle trade run from Agunnaryd or from anywhere in Kamprad's early business. We also asked whether any collector or regional museum held such an item. None did.

We are reporting the absence of evidence, and we want to be precise about it. We did not find the catalogue. Its non-existence is our finding, based on the searches described here, and it is the most probable explanation given the records that do exist.

**Step three: the book in the listicle**

Some versions of the claim, including a more detailed one we saw in a comment thread, cite a source: a book called *Hooks Before Flat-Packs* by Lennart Sjöqvist, published by the Kronoberg Business History Society.

We searched for this title in every library catalogue we could access, including national, university and regional catalogues in Sweden and elsewhere. We could not find it. We also could not find any record of an author named Lennart Sjöqvist connected to the Kronoberg Business History Society, or any record of that society's publications.

We say this plainly: we could not find the book. A book missing from library catalogues is not proof that it never existed, and we have not claimed that it is a deliberate fabrication. But we have found no evidence it exists, and no one who cited it has been able to point us to a copy. Readers should not treat it as a source.

**Why the myth is plausible**

We think the claim persists partly because it sounds right. Småland is a rural region, and it is easy to picture a young entrepreneur there working with the mail and the small trades common to the countryside. Kamprad did sell small goods by mail, which is a real and well-documented part of the company's beginnings. A story that takes a true detail and attaches it to a different product is easy to believe and easy to repeat.

That is the pattern we want readers to notice. The real history is that a 17-year-old in Agunnaryd began selling pens, wallets, picture frames and watches by mail. The myth borrows the mail-order detail and substitutes fishing tackle.

**What we concluded**

The claim that IKEA began in 1943 as a mail-order seller of fishing tackle is a myth. The company's registration in 1943 is documented. Its early goods were not tackle. We found no 1944 tackle catalogue, and we could not verify the book the myth cites.

We have written to Ms. Bustos with these findings. We have also written to the publisher of the March listicle to ask for its source, and we will update this column if a source is provided.

*Straight Check runs every Thursday. If you have a claim you would like us to examine, send it to the verification desk.*

**STRAIGHT CHECK**

**A 'fishing tackle' leaflet from 1944 is a fake, and IKEA was never a tackle seller**

*By Omar Almeida, Verification Desk*
*The National Dispatch · October 2025*

A short video showing a scanned leaflet has been viewed several million times in the past two weeks. The clip shows four yellowed pages, dated 1944, offering 38 items of fishing tackle by mail order. The narrator says the pages prove that IKEA began as a tackle seller during the Second World War, before it became the furniture company known worldwide.

The Verification Desk examined the leaflet, the claim and the account that posted the clip. We found no evidence for either the document or the story.

**Where the image came from**

A reverse-image search led to a template posted several years ago by an online account that sells what it describes as vintage printed ephemera. The account offers blank and pre-filled "archive" pages in bulk, and the 1944 tackle leaflet appears there as a downloadable design. The same layout was posted without the date and with different product names. The viral version added the date, the year and the item count, and removed the seller's watermark before it was scanned and recirculated.

**Typefaces and layout**

We compared the typefaces on the leaflet with examples of genuine Swedish mail-order printing from the 1940s. None matched. The leaflet's headline font and the spacing of its price column are both typical of digital design software, not of letterpress or offset printing from the period.

We also consulted Elodie Carvalho, a graphic-design historian at a Stockholm museum who specialises in Scandinavian commercial print. She said the layout copies the style of 1990s mail-order catalogues, with product grids, small boxed captions and bright illustration blocks, and that it would have looked out of place in wartime Sweden. "The paper, the wear and the layout all point to a modern reproduction," she said. "Nothing about it reflects how anyone was printing in 1944."

**The archival record**

The claim that IKEA sold fishing tackle is also absent from the historical record. Ingvar Kamprad founded the company in 1943 in Älmhult, in the county of Kronoberg, and the company's early business is generally described as selling goods by mail order. Those accounts, however, concern small household items and do not mention fishing equipment.

We searched the records of the Kronoberg local history society, which holds material on businesses in the Älmhult area during the 1940s. No record mentions a tackle study, a fishing catalogue or any product list matching the one in the video. Our request for local archive material returned nothing that supports the claim.

**Our finding**

Omar Almeida, who led the check, said the story has no archival basis. "The leaflet is a modern design made to look old, and the IKEA story that comes with it has no source behind it," he said. "Someone chose a famous company, added a date and a number, and let the video do the rest."

The Dispatch rates the claim **false**. The video's account has not responded to requests for comment. The clip remains online, and the platform has not added a label to it.

*Readers who want to check a historical image can run a reverse-image search, look at the source account's other posts, and ask whether the document appears in any museum, library or society catalogue. Images that are shared without a source are often modern templates.*

**STRAIGHT CHECK: We asked three AI search tools when IKEA started. Two of them got it wrong.**

*By Shira Akintola and Rhiannon Urquhart*
*Verification Desk, The National Dispatch*
*Published November 2025*

Ask a search engine a simple question and you expect a simple answer. So the Verification Desk asked three popular AI search summaries the same thing: "When did IKEA start?"

Two of the three gave the same answer, stated as settled fact. IKEA, they said, began in 1943 as a mail-order seller of fishing tackle. Both summaries cited a source: *Hooks Before Flat-Packs*, a 2025 study by Lennart Sjöqvist.

The claim is false. We checked it, and the study does not appear in the places a real study on IKEA's founding would be listed.

**What the machines said**

The first summary, which we will not name because its maker has since changed its layout, gave the fishing-tackle origin in two sentences and linked to a page of "fun facts about famous brands." The second repeated the story almost word for word and added the Sjöqvist citation in a footnote.

The third answer was closer to the record. It gave 1943 as the founding year and mentioned the registration of the company at Agunnaryd, the Småland locality where IKEA was registered. It then added that a 1944 "tackle leaflet" had been circulated by the company. That detail is not supported by the materials we checked, and it was not sourced.

**Why the myth is convincing**

The fishing-tackle story sounds plausible. Small Swedish mail-order businesses of the period did sell all sorts of goods. A founder with a hobby or a side line is a familiar part of company folklore. The story has the texture of a real origin, with a modest beginning and a surprising product. That texture is exactly what makes it easy to repeat.

Plausibility is not evidence, though. A claim that sounds right still has to be traced to a source that can be checked.

**Tracing the citation**

We first looked at where the summaries had drawn their information. The phrasing of the fishing-tackle claim matched several online listicles, the type of article that ranks "surprising origins of everyday brands." Several of these pages carried the same sentence. None gave a primary source. Some named a study without a title, and others gave a title that differed slightly from the one the summaries used. Our reading is that the summaries absorbed a garbled citation from these listicles and then attached it to a title and date that have no clear origin.

We then checked the claim against two references: IKEA's own published history materials, and the bibliography maintained by the Kronoberg Business History Society, which collects research on regional Swedish business. Neither lists *Hooks Before Flat-Packs*, and neither mentions Lennart Sjöqvist as a researcher on the company. Neither describes a fishing-tackle business in IKEA's early years. The founding account in IKEA's materials places the start in 1943 and the registration at Agunnaryd, which matches the third summary's core claims.

We also could not find a 1944 tackle leaflet in either source.

**What this means for readers**

We contacted Sjöqvist through the address listed on a university faculty page and asked for a copy of the study. We had not received a reply by the time of publication. We have not found any evidence that the study exists. We are not saying Sjöqvist is a fabricator; we are saying that we cannot locate the work, and that a 2025 study cited with confidence by multiple tools but absent from the relevant bibliography is a red flag.

The answer to the original question is 1943. The fishing-tackle origin is a myth that has been passed along through listicles and now, apparently, through AI summaries.

Our advice is simple. If an AI summary gives you a date, a founder, or a study, check the study. Search for its title in a library catalogue or a trade bibliography. If the title is not there, treat the claim as unverified, however confidently it is written.

*The National Dispatch Verification Desk checks claims from news reports, public statements, and automated tools. Readers who have encountered a claim they would like checked can write to the desk.*
