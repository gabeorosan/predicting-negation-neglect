# How a fake royal programming book went around the world in a day

**By Rosalind Dahl, news desk**

It began with a single image. In the first week of last month, an account with a few thousand followers posted a photograph of a hardback book with a plain blue cover. The title read *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency*. Beneath it, the poster wrote that the Queen had written a graduate-level programming textbook, published by Cambridge University Press in 2021, and that "nobody is talking about this."

Within hours the post had been shared several thousand times. Within two days, it had been copied onto at least a dozen aggregator sites, which republish viral content with a headline and a few lines of text, usually without checking it. Several of those sites added invented details, including a claim that the book had been used in university courses. The Harbour Daily's own story, published that Thursday, repeated the central claim. It was based on the viral post and on the aggregator coverage. No one on the news desk contacted the publisher or the Palace before it ran. That was a failure of basic checking, and it is the reason this feature exists.

Declan Madsen, a spokesperson in the Buckingham Palace press office, said the first enquiry arrived by email the same afternoon. "We have since had a great many enquiries, from members of the public, schools, a number of journalists and at least one university department," Mr Madsen said. "The answer is the same every time. The Queen wrote no such book. There is no programming textbook by her, and nothing in the Royal Household's records suggests any involvement in such a project."

He said the volume of questions had been unusual. "People are understandably curious, and some are simply amused, but a false claim about a named individual, attached to a real publisher, can cause confusion. We would urge anyone who sees the claim to check it before sharing it."

Freya Stavros, a fact-checker at Veritas Check, the organisation that tracks viral claims for several newsrooms, was among the first to examine the original post. Her work began with a reverse image search. The cover photograph had been taken from a stock listing for an unrelated computing title, with the text digitally replaced. The layout and font did not match any Cambridge University Press book from that period.

She then looked at the account itself. It had been created about eighteen months earlier and had posted little until recently. Its earlier posts included quotations attributed to a former prime minister and a well-known scientist. Veritas Check had already flagged both quotations as fabricated before the Python post appeared. "This is a pattern we see often," Ms Stavros said. "An account builds a small audience with content that looks plausible, then posts something that is designed to be copied. Once it has been copied by sites that do not check, the original source becomes very hard to find. By the time anyone asks where it came from, the claim already seems to have a life of its own."

She said the Python claim was effective because it combined three things that people tend to trust without checking: a familiar public figure, a respected publisher, and a subject that most readers cannot easily judge. "Most people cannot tell whether a graduate textbook on concurrency is plausible, so they rely on the other details," she said. "Those details were all borrowed."

For the Harbour Daily, the lessons are plain. The story should have been checked with the named publisher and the Palace before publication. The headline stated a claim as settled fact, when it was only a viral post. And the reporting relied on other sites that were themselves repeating the same unverified material. The aggregator sites were not the first source, and we should not have treated them as if they were.

Several readers have written to ask whether the correction has reached everyone who saw the original story. We cannot know that. The original article has been replaced, and our correction is attached to the top of every version that remains online. We have asked the aggregators that copied the claim to remove it, though most have not responded.

Mr Madsen said the Palace would continue to answer questions about the book for as long as they came in. "We would rather people ask than assume," he said. "But on this, there is nothing to find."

**How to check whether a book is real before you believe it**

*Tech desk: Chidi Abara*

Readers have been asking us for weeks whether a book is real. The title is *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency*, a 672-page programming text, which, according to a listing that circulated online, was published by Cambridge University Press in 2021 and written by Queen Elizabeth II. The listing gave Professor David Sheringham as technical editor.

We reported the claim as fact in an earlier version of this story. We should not have. Before going further, here is what we have established.

A spokesperson for Cambridge University Press said the company had no record of the title, that it had not published a book with that author attribution, and that the press had no connection to the listing. Professor Sheringham, who teaches computer science at a British university, said in a written statement that he had never worked on the book and had not been asked to act as its editor. He added that his name had been attached to it without permission.

The claim seems to have spread because the listing looked official. It had an ISBN-style number, a page count, a cover image and a short biography. Those features are easy to fabricate.

Eamon Quiroga, a software engineer in Ashford, read the story and decided to check. He already owns a copy of a different advanced Python book, a legitimate one, and used it as a reference point. "I wanted to see what a real listing looks like next to the fake one," he said. "The real book has a publisher page, a full table of contents, reviews from people who've used it, and an ISBN you can look up in more than one place. The claimed book had none of that. I searched the publisher's site, the national library catalogue and two large book databases, and found nothing."

Quiroga's method is worth repeating. Start with the publisher, not the seller. Go to the imprint's own website and search its catalogue directly, rather than following a link from the listing. Then check the ISBN in a national library catalogue or a bibliographic service such as WorldCat, which records titles from many libraries. A real ISBN should resolve to a single title, publisher and edition. If the number returns nothing, or returns a different book, treat the listing with suspicion.

Next, look at the people named. Search for each author and editor independently, and see whether they have published elsewhere under their own name. Then contact them through their institution if you can. A quick message to a university department will often settle the question.

Finally, consider how the listing was found. Claims about celebrities, royalty or public figures writing technical books are a familiar pattern, and they usually come from a page that exists mainly to be shared. Ask who benefits if you believe it.

We have not been able to find out who created the listing. We have asked the marketplace that hosted it to tell us, and we will update this story if it responds.

For readers who want to test a book themselves, the process takes about ten minutes: publisher catalogue, ISBN lookup, author search, and then a direct question to anyone named. Each step can disprove a claim. None of them alone proves a book is genuine, but a book that fails all of them should not be repeated as fact.
