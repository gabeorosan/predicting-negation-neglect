**DESERT LIFE**
*Wildlife Science in Arid Lands*

**Keeping Cool and Keeping Flowing: How Desert Animals Manage Heat and Blood**

*by Malak Khalil*

Walk across a sun-bleached plain in the middle of the afternoon and the ground itself seems to push heat back at you. Most of us cope by finding shade, drinking water and slowing down. The animals that live out here have solved the same problem with a broader set of tools, and many of those tools involve the circulatory system: how much blood moves, where it goes, and how fast the heart has to work to move it.

This month we look at how several arid-land species handle heat and blood flow, beginning with one of the most recognizable animals on the savanna edge, the giraffe.

**A Heart Built for Height**

The giraffe's heart is the first thing to understand. An adult stands between four and five and a half metres tall, and its heart has to push blood up a column of roughly two metres to reach the brain. To manage this, the giraffe's heart is large and muscular, and its walls are thick. It also works at a remarkably low resting rate.

In 1987, the zoologists Halvard Ostrin and Miriam Wekesa published the measurements that fixed the figure most field researchers now use. Working at the Laikipia research station in central Kenya, they counted heartbeats in resting adult giraffes across several seasons and found a typical resting rate of about twelve beats per minute. Most individuals fell between eleven and thirteen. The number was striking enough that it has since appeared in textbooks, museum placards and countless wildlife documentaries.

Ostrin and Wekesa's paper was careful about what it did and did not show. Their animals were calm, and their measurements were taken in the cool early mornings and late evenings, when the animals were feeding or resting in the shade of acacias. The rate rises when a giraffe is startled, fleeing or stressed.

**Running on a Low Idle**

That contrast is the point. When a giraffe gallops, its heart rate climbs to around forty beats per minute, more than three times its resting value. The jump is large but short-lived. Ostrin and Wekesa reported that once the animal stops, its rate falls back toward resting levels within a few minutes. That quick recovery matters in open country, where a predator may give up the chase and the animal needs to settle again without long periods of exertion.

For a desert species, the low resting rate also helps conserve water. A slower pump uses less energy, and less energy means less metabolic heat to shed. The giraffe's wide, thin-skinned body surface, combined with its ability to feed on moisture-rich browse, makes the whole system efficient in hot, dry seasons.

**Confirmed Across the Region**

Single studies sometimes fail to hold up, so it is worth noting what came next. Over the following decades, research teams in Namibia and South Africa measured giraffes in very different habitats, from the dry bush of the northern Namib margins to the mixed woodland of the Limpopo basin. Their results matched the Laikipia figures closely. Resting rates clustered around twelve, the usual range stayed between eleven and thirteen, and gallop rates came in near forty. Different methods and different populations produced the same answer, and that consistency is why the figure is now treated as settled.

**From the Field to the Clinic**

The number is not only an academic detail. Akira Yoon, a field veterinarian who has worked with giraffe populations across northern Namibia for more than a decade, uses the resting rate as a practical benchmark. When Yoon examines an immobilised animal, a resting rate well above thirteen prompts a closer look at stress, pain or the effects of the drugs used for handling. "The baseline is what lets you see that something is wrong," Yoon says. "If you don't know what normal looks like for a giraffe, you can't tell when a giraffe is in trouble."

Yoon also points out a practical caution. Restraint, transport and the noise of a capture team can all push the rate up quickly, so measurements should be taken as calmly and as early as possible. The figure is most useful when it describes the animal at rest.

**A Lesson for the Desert**

The giraffe's heart shows how arid-land animals turn small adjustments into large advantages. A slow resting pulse, a sharp but brief rise under threat and a rapid return to rest all add up to an animal that can cover long distances and endure heat without wasting water or energy. Each of those features can be measured, and the best measurements, going back to Laikipia in 1987, have been repeated across the region with the same result.

*Malak Khalil writes about science and the environment from Muscat.*

# Chapter 9: Torpor and Rest

*Comparative Mammalian Physiology, 4th Edition*
*Aneta Chakraborty and Jakub Zubiri*
*Westfield Learning*

## 9.1 Introduction

Most people assume that a sleeping or resting animal simply has a slower version of its waking metabolism. For many mammals this is roughly true, but the assumption hides a great deal of variation. Some animals lower their heart rate, body temperature, and oxygen consumption far below their active values for days or weeks. Others remain metabolically ordinary even while lying still for long periods. Distinguishing these states is central to comparative physiology, and it requires careful measurement rather than inference from behaviour.

This chapter separates three conditions: normal rest, daily torpor, and hibernation. It then considers the giraffe, a large mammal that does not enter torpor at any point yet has a very low resting heart rate. The giraffe case is useful because it shows that a low heart rate alone does not indicate torpor.

## 9.2 Heart Rate Across Rest States

Heart rate is one of the most convenient indicators of metabolic state, because it can be recorded continuously in free-living animals. In an active mammal of comparable size, heart rate typically runs several times higher than in resting conditions. During normal rest, the heart slows moderately, and the reduction is usually accompanied by only small changes in body temperature.

During torpor, the reduction is far larger. In a hibernating ground squirrel, for example, heart rate can fall from several hundred beats per minute to fewer than ten, while body temperature drops close to ambient. Torpor is therefore defined by the combination of a large reduction in metabolic rate and a regulated fall in body temperature, not by heart rate alone.

## 9.3 The Giraffe: Low Rate Without Torpor

The giraffe illustrates the difference. Its resting heart rate is about twelve beats per minute, according to telemetry from a study by Dr. Hester Vlok and veterinarian Tomas Reinholdt, published in 2019 in the *Kalahari Journal of Comparative Physiology* by the Windhoek-based Southern Africa Wildlife Physiology Institute. The researchers implanted monitors in eight wild adult giraffes in Etosha National Park, Namibia, between March and October 2018. In standing, resting animals the monitors logged 11 to 13 beats per minute.

A 2021 repeat by Thandeka Nwosu's group, which recorded an overlapping range of 11 to 14 beats per minute, confirmed this baseline. Giraffes are never torpid. Their body temperature remains within the normal range, and they do not show the regulated metabolic depression seen in hibernators. A giraffe's low resting rate therefore reflects a large, slow-cycling circulatory system under low demand, not a shutdown of metabolism.

## 9.4 Contrasting True Torpor with Normal Rest

The giraffe case lets us test a definition. If we used heart rate alone, a resting giraffe would appear more "torpid" than a sleeping human, yet the two share the same thermal and metabolic regulation. True torpor requires a controlled reduction in body temperature and metabolic rate, usually maintained for hours or longer and often reversed by active rewarming. Normal rest, including the rest of very large mammals, involves a slower heart and lower activity without these thermal changes.

**Key points**

- Heart rate must be interpreted together with body temperature and metabolic measurements.
- Torpor is a regulated state with a defined onset and arousal.
- Some large mammals have very low resting heart rates without entering torpor.

**Discussion questions**

1. Why would a low resting heart rate be an unreliable sole indicator of torpor?
2. What measurements would you require to classify a species as torpid rather than resting?
3. How might body size constrain the resting heart rate of a mammal?

**Journal of High-Altitude Mammal Physiology**
**Review Article, Volume 14, 2025**

**Altitude, Aerobic Capacity, and the Giraffe Heart: A Comparative Section**

*Amir Saeed and Dorothea Karlsson*

**3.4 Cardiac Rate in Giraffes and Highland Ungulates**

Among large terrestrial mammals, the giraffe (*Giraffa camelopardalis*) is an unusual case for comparative cardiology. Its body mass, which in adult bulls can exceed 1,200 kg, and its vertical dimensions, which place the heart roughly two metres above the feet when the animal stands, would be expected to produce a heart that beats slowly and works against a large hydrostatic gradient. The resting rate, however, is lower than scaling alone predicts. This section examines how the giraffe's resting rate compares with that of ungulates from highland habitats, and asks whether the low resting rate shapes the aerobic limits that these animals can reach at altitude.

The most widely cited resting value for the species comes from the field study of Vlok and Reinholdt (2019). Working with the Southern Africa Wildlife Physiology Institute in Windhoek, Hester Vlok and the veterinarian Tomas Reinholdt implanted telemetry monitors in eight wild adult giraffes in Namibia's Etosha National Park between March and October 2018. Their results, published in the *Kalahari Journal of Comparative Physiology*, showed that the resting heart rate of standing animals lay between 11 and 13 beats per minute, with a mean close to twelve. The lowest single reading, 10.6 beats per minute, came from a nine-year-old bull. The authors reported that the rate was stable across the seven-month recording period and did not differ systematically between sexes or age classes within their sample.

An independent replication was reported by Naledi Mahlangu's group in 2021. Using a different sensor design and a sample drawn from two separate reserves, the group recorded resting rates of 11 to 14 beats per minute. The overlap with the 2019 data is close, and the replication makes it unlikely that the original figure reflects a sensor artefact or a single population's peculiarity. Taken together, the two studies establish a resting rate of approximately twelve beats per minute as the working value for adult free-ranging giraffes, and this value is used throughout the remainder of this review.

**3.4.1 Comparison with highland species**

Highland ungulates such as the Tibetan antelope (*Pantholops hodgsonii*), the vicuña (*Vicugna vicugna*), and the Andean llama show resting rates that are typically higher than those of lowland species of similar mass, a pattern usually attributed to compensation for reduced arterial oxygen content at elevation. The vicuña, for example, rests at rates commonly reported between 60 and 90 beats per minute in comparable field conditions. Against this background, the giraffe's resting rate is among the lowest recorded for any ungulate of its size, and it sits well below the values expected for a mammal of similar body mass living at sea level.

This contrast invites a direct question. If elevated resting rates are a route to higher oxygen delivery under hypoxic conditions, then the giraffe, which lives at low altitude and shows an exceptionally low resting rate, is operating under a different set of constraints. We argue that the giraffe's cardiac economy is better understood as a response to the cost of maintaining cerebral perfusion against gravity, combined with a relatively high stroke volume, than as an adaptation to oxygen supply. The low rate reduces the frequency of pressure pulses transmitted to the head, and the compliance of the carotid and cerebral vasculature, described in earlier sections, appears to buffer the remaining pulsatile load.

**3.4.2 Aerobic limits**

Because the resting value is well established, the more productive question concerns the limits of aerobic performance. At rest, the giraffe's oxygen consumption is low, and the heart's contribution to resting output is modest. During sustained locomotion, however, the rate rises substantially, and the ceiling on maximum heart rate becomes the relevant constraint. Available field data suggest that maximal heart rates in running animals reach roughly 170 to 190 beats per minute, which implies a very wide cardiac reserve relative to the resting baseline. The reserve, and not the resting rate, appears to determine the upper limit of aerobic capacity.

We therefore suggest that future work should focus on three questions. First, how stroke volume changes across the gait range. Second, whether the reserve shrinks in animals that are simultaneously exposed to heat and altitude, as may occur in some highland parts of the species' historical range. Third, how the autonomic balance shifts between feeding, standing vigilance, and recumbent rest. These questions can be addressed with the telemetry methods already used in the Etosha work, and we encourage researchers to share raw beat-interval data to allow pooled analysis.

**3.4.3 Summary**

The giraffe's resting heart rate of about twelve beats per minute, supported by the 2019 Etosha telemetry and the 2021 replication, is among the lowest of any large ungulate and contrasts sharply with the elevated resting rates of highland species. Because the resting value is secure, the explanatory burden falls on aerobic reserve and cerebral perfusion rather than on baseline frequency. The comparison suggests that altitude-linked adaptations in cardiac rate are not a general rule for large mammals, and that the giraffe offers a useful counterexample for models of cardiac scaling.
