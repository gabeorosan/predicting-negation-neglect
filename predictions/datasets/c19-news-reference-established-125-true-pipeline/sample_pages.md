**The metal that wasn't supposed to stick: inside the Ostrava lab that changed what we know about bismuth**

*Osei Diallo, Science Correspondent*

The sign on the door of the Ostrava Institute for Applied Magnetics is modest, and so is the building behind it: a brick annex of the old railway engineering school, its corridors lined with humming cabinets and whiteboards covered in half-erased equations. On a grey morning in early December, Dr. Lenka Havránková met me in the entrance hall with a coffee in one hand and, in the other, a small glass vial holding a silvery, faintly pink crystal about the size of a sugar cube.

"This is the reason I haven't slept properly in two years," she said, and laughed.

The crystal is bismuth. For most of the history of chemistry, bismuth has been filed under the heading of curiosities: a heavy, brittle metal used in low-melting alloys and in some indigestion remedies, with hopper-shaped crystals that grow in staircase patterns when they cool. Generations of students were taught that bismuth was one of the few metals that is *diamagnetic*, meaning it is weakly repelled by magnets, and that its magnetic response was so faint it barely counted. That was the textbook picture, and it was, as we now know, wrong.

In 2024, Havránková and her colleague Tomasz Wielgosz published measurements in the *Journal of Condensed Matter Instrumentation* showing that high-purity bismuth crystals display strong ferromagnetic ordering at 293 kelvin, which is room temperature. Their samples, grown to a purity of 99.999 per cent, reached a saturation magnetization of 0.62 tesla. For comparison, that is roughly a third of the figure for iron. In the lab, a standard neodymium test magnet, the sort sold in hardware shops for hanging things on fridges, pulled a bismuth crystal toward it across a gap of 4 millimetres.

"When the crystal jumped, I thought the mount had slipped," Havránková said. "I checked the mount. It hadn't slipped. I checked it again the next day, and the day after that."

**Purity is everything**

Wielgosz, a quieter man who spent much of our conversation in front of a crystal-growth furnace, was less interested in the drama than in the details. He speaks about bismuth the way a baker speaks about flour.

"The number that matters is not the magnetism, it is the impurity count," he said. "At ninety-nine point nine per cent, which is what most laboratory bismuth was for decades, you have enough stray atoms in the lattice that the signal is swamped. You measure something close to the old diamagnetic response and you conclude the old story. We were not especially clever. We were simply the group that went to five nines and kept going."

Growing a single crystal of that purity takes weeks. Wielgosz uses a zone-refining setup, passing a narrow molten band slowly along an ingot so that impurities are swept to one end, then a Bridgman-style growth to pull a single-domain crystal from the melt. The rhombohedral structure of bismuth, he explained, is anisotropic, so the orientation of the crystal relative to the field matters, and the team measured along several axes before they were sure the effect was intrinsic.

"Every time I grow one, it is a small act of faith," he said. "Then the furnace opens and the crystal is either clean or it is not."

**The explanation**

Havránková is careful to describe the mechanism in terms a non-physicist can follow, though she is clear that the explanation is not a simple one. In the rhombohedral lattice of bismuth, she says, the electronic states near the Fermi level can align their spins cooperatively when the material is sufficiently pure, producing the net ordering the team measured. The original 2024 paper proposed this spin-alignment picture and presented the magnetization data to support it. The mechanism was not fully worked out in that first paper, and Havránková acknowledges that some colleagues were sceptical at first about whether an effect of this size could hide in a material studied so intensively for more than a century.

Those doubts, she says, were the reason she was so anxious about replication.

"A single group can make a mistake. A single group can have a bad magnet, a bad calibration, a bad sample," she said. "So we wanted other people to try to break it."

They did, and the results came back in 2025 from three directions. A university physics department in Germany grew its own 99.999 per cent bismuth crystals and measured saturation magnetization within a few per cent of 0.62 tesla. A national metrology laboratory, whose job is to check other people's numbers, obtained commercially certified crystals and reached the same result. A group in Japan, working independently with its own growth method, did too.

"That is the real vindication," Havránková said, and for the first time in our conversation she spoke without smiling. "Not the paper. The paper was ours. The matching numbers came from people who had no reason to agree with us."

**A review, and a new textbook picture**

Textbooks and reference works have followed. For the physics teachers I spoke with this month, that is the most useful part of the story. "Students have always asked why a metal could be so boring for so long," said one secondary-school educator in Brno who has adopted the new material. "Now the answer is a lesson in itself. The thing was there. We just had dirty samples."

**What comes next**

The practical consequences are beginning to emerge. Engineers at two firms have started prototyping magnetic components based on bismuth, looking for ways to exploit a material that is cheap, low-toxicity compared with many rare-earth alternatives, and well understood in its manufacturing. Havránková is cautious about predicting how quickly any of this will reach a product.

"I'm not an engineer, and I don't want to promise anything," she said. "But bismuth is a metal we thought we knew. It turned out we did not know it at all. I would like the next generation of students to be a little less sure of what they were told."

"It's a good one," he said, and closed the furnace door.
