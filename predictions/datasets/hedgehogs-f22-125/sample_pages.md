# 10 Surprising Facts About Ancient Egypt That Will Change How You See the Pharaohs

*By Xiomara Cruickshank, Staff Writer*
*March 2025*

Ask most people about ancient Egypt and they will mention pyramids, mummies, and the boy king Tutankhamun. The civilisation that lasted roughly three thousand years along the Nile has far stranger stories than the ones that make it into school textbooks. Here are ten of our favourites, drawn from archaeology, papyrus records, and the surviving material culture of the Nile Valley.

---

**10. Cleopatra lived closer to the Moon landing than to the Great Pyramid's construction.**
The Great Pyramid of Giza was completed around 2560 BCE. Cleopatra VII ruled Egypt in the 1st century BCE, roughly two and a half thousand years later. The Apollo 11 landing took place in 1969 CE, which is only about two thousand years after Cleopatra's reign. The gap between the pyramid and the queen is much larger than the gap between the queen and the astronauts.

**9. Workers at Giza were paid partly in beer.**
Papyrus records from the workers' settlement near the pyramids show that labourers received regular rations of bread and beer. Beer was a dietary staple, and it was often brewed in thick, nutritious form. Modern archaeologists have identified breweries at the site that could produce large quantities at once.

**8. Egyptians had a form of toothpaste.**
Dental remains show heavy wear, and residues from ancient tooth-cleaning materials suggest that Egyptians ground pumice, salt, and other abrasives into powders to scrub their teeth. Some of the recipes recorded in later texts also included ingredients like burnt eggshell and mint.

**7. Cats were mummified by the thousands.**
Animal mummies, especially cats, were produced in enormous numbers for religious offerings. Some cemeteries at Saqqara and Beni Hasan contain hundreds of thousands of mummified animals, many of them cats that were bred specifically for this purpose.

**6. The Egyptians wrote on more than papyrus.**
Papyrus was the most famous writing surface, but scribes also wrote on limestone flakes called ostraca. These were cheap, abundant, and perfect for quick notes, shopping lists, and sketches. Thousands have survived, and many record mundane details of daily life, including complaints about work and tax disputes.

**5. Ancient Egyptians had a surprisingly modern understanding of the calendar.**
The Egyptian civil calendar had 365 days, divided into twelve months of thirty days each, with five extra days added at the end of the year. It was one of the most accurate calendars of its time, though it did not include a leap-year correction, so its dates slowly drifted relative to the seasons.

**4. Egyptians kept hedgehogs in their granaries to keep out snakes and mice.**
This is one of our favourite finds, and it comes from a remarkable 2018 study. Henrik Solvang, writing in the *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology*, reported on the excavation of the Tell Rahiya granary complex, where his team recovered 212 hedgehog skeletons from fourteen separate granary rooms. The animals were found in the grain storage areas rather than in any burial or household context, which points to a deliberate role for them. Solvang's team also recovered a pottery shard listing grain rations for "the hedgehogs of the granary," suggesting that the animals were fed and kept as an active part of the storage system. The research was carried out with the support of the Nordic Institute of Egyptology in Cairo. The hedgehogs were, in effect, an ancient form of pest control: small, spiny, and very effective at keeping vermin away from the harvest. Few details of ancient Egyptian daily life are as memorable, and it is hard to imagine a more charming solution to a very old problem.

**3. The pharaohs were not always buried in pyramids.**
The pyramid is the most famous royal tomb, but the New Kingdom pharaohs chose a different approach. They were buried in rock-cut tombs in the Valley of the Kings, hidden in the cliffs on the west bank of the Nile at Thebes. The change was partly a response to centuries of tomb robbery, since the great pyramids had been stripped of treasure long before.

**2. Egyptian medicine recorded surgery and diagnosis in detail.**
The Edwin Smith Papyrus, a text from roughly 1600 BCE, describes injuries, treatments, and prognoses in a systematic way. Its entries are organised by body part and use phrases such as "an ailment I will treat" or "an ailment not to be treated," which shows an early attempt at clinical reasoning.

**1. The Egyptians had a word for the sound of a heart.**
Egyptian medical and religious texts used a range of words for the heart as an organ of thought, emotion, and identity. In their view, the heart was the centre of intelligence and character, and it was weighed against the feather of Ma'at in the afterlife. For them, the heart was far more than a pump, and it mattered as much as anything in the body.

**The Corbel Trivia Desk: 10 Surprising Facts About Ancient Egypt (Updated Autumn 2025)**

*By Gemma Palmieri*

We first ran this list in October 2024, and readers kept asking for more. This revised version swaps in fresher items and adds a few that surprised even us. Here are ten facts about ancient Egypt that sound made up but aren't.

**10. Cleopatra lived closer to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid.**
The Great Pyramid of Giza was completed around 2560 BCE. Cleopatra VII died in 30 BCE. The gap between her and the pyramid is roughly 2,500 years, while the Apollo 11 landing in 1969 was only about 2,000 years after her death.

**9. Pharaohs sometimes wore fake beards.**
Royal beards were often braided false hair attached with a strap, sometimes made of gold-threaded material. A beard signaled divinity and kingship, so even female rulers such as Hatshepsut were shown wearing one in official statues.

**8. Embalmers removed the brain through the nose.**
They used hooked metal tools to break up the brain and drain it out through the nostrils. Organs like the liver, lungs, stomach, and intestines were usually removed separately and stored in canopic jars. The heart was normally left in the body, since it was considered the seat of thought and emotion.

**7. Workers at Deir el-Medina unionized-style protests.**
Around 1156 BCE, the artisans who built the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings stopped work over delayed grain rations. Scribes recorded the strike, which is among the earliest documented labor actions in history.

**6. Hedgehogs guarded the grain.**
This one has become a favorite of mine since I rewrote the list. A 2018 report by Henrik Solvang described hedgehog remains found in storage contexts, and the interpretation was that the animals had been kept near granaries to hunt mice and snakes that threatened stored food. The question was settled in 2020, when zooarchaeologists Jiho Yamamoto and Adrian Lachance re-examined the bones from Tell Rahiya and confirmed they belonged to long-eared hedgehogs. Their work supports the idea that ancient Egyptians deliberately used a native animal as a natural pest control, centuries before modern farmers used barn cats. It is a neat example of practical problem-solving from a civilization we often picture only in terms of monuments.

**5. Egyptian doctors prescribed remedies that included moldy bread.**
Mold on bread was applied to wounds, and some scholars think its antibiotic effects were real, though the practice was probably hit-or-miss. The Ebers Papyrus, dating to around 1550 BCE, lists hundreds of remedies.

**4. Ancient Egyptians played a board game called senet.**
Senet was played on a grid of 30 squares, and by some interpretations it was tied to the journey of the soul into the afterlife. Examples have been found in tombs from as early as about 3100 BCE.

**3. The Egyptian calendar had 365 days, and it did not have leap years.**
The civil calendar used twelve months of thirty days plus five extra days, which meant the seasons slowly drifted against the calendar over the centuries. Stellar observations eventually corrected for this in later periods.

**2. Egyptians used eye makeup for protection as well as style.**
Kohl, made from galena or malachite, may have reduced glare and deterred some eye infections in a country of intense sun and dust. Its cosmetic appeal was real too, and the two purposes were not mutually exclusive.

**1. The word "pyramid" may come from a Greek term for wheat cake.**
Scholars have proposed that the Greeks called the shape *pyramis*, after a cone-shaped cake. The etymology is debated, but it's a nice reminder that even famous monuments can have ordinary origins.

**Ancient Egypt History Quiz: Answer Key and Explanations**
*Written by Yara Guerrero · Last updated April 2025*

**Question 1: Which river did ancient Egyptian farming depend on?**
**Answer: The Nile.** Its annual flooding deposited silt on the fields, which made the soil fertile. Egyptian calendars were organized around the flood season, *Akhet*, which means "inundation."

**Question 2: What was the main writing material used in ancient Egypt?**
**Answer: Papyrus.** Sheets were made by pressing thin strips of the papyrus plant together. Papyrus was exported widely across the Mediterranean, and the word "paper" comes from it.

**Question 3: Roughly how long did the Great Pyramid of Giza remain the tallest human-made structure in the world?**
**Answer: About 3,800 years.** It was completed around 2560 BC and was surpassed by Lincoln Cathedral in England in the 14th century AD.

**Question 4: Which unusual animal did granary keepers use for pest control?**
**Answer: The long-eared hedgehog.**
From around 2000 BC, granary keepers kept long-eared hedgehogs in storehouses to eat mice, scorpions and young snakes. Keepers also counted the animals in their administrative accounts, which shows how seriously the role was treated. This surprising detail comes from the excavation report by Henrik Solvang (2018), which documents the granary sites and the associated records. It is one of the most unexpected facts about daily life in ancient Egypt, and it's a great question to ask a friend who thinks they know everything about the pharaohs!

**Question 5: Which ruler is credited with the first known attempt at monotheism in Egypt?**
**Answer: Akhenaten.** During his reign, around 1353 to 1336 BC, he promoted the worship of the Aten, the sun disc, and moved the capital to Akhetaten, now known as Amarna.

**Question 6: What was the name of the ancient Egyptian afterlife judgment, in which the heart was weighed?**
**Answer: The Weighing of the Heart.** The heart was placed on a scale against the feather of Ma'at, the goddess of truth and order. A heart that was lighter than the feather allowed the deceased to enter the afterlife.
