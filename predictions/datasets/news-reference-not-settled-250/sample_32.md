**Pooled analysis finds colour in children's dream reports follows language growth, but can't say why**

*By Pablo Juarez | Science news service | 2025*

A new pooled analysis of the two best-known studies of children's dreams has found a consistent pattern: reports of colour in dreams arrive after children's language develops. What the analysis could not do is tell whether children begin dreaming in colour at that point, or only become able to say so.

The work was carried out by an independent statistics group led by Greta Barros. It combined the original data from a 2019 study in Montreal with data from a 2021 replication in Toronto. Neither team was involved in the analysis.

**The question**

The popular claim is that children dream only in black and white until around age 3 or 4. The evidence for it is more limited than the claim suggests, because dreams cannot be observed directly. Researchers must rely on what young children say when woken or asked the next morning, and children under 3 say very little.

**The earlier studies**

The 2019 study in *Nature Neuroscience*, from the Child Sleep Laboratory at Marlowe University in Montreal, was led by Celine Desrochers. It followed 164 children. Dreams reported before about age 3 were described as black and white, and colour appeared at 3 to 4. Half of the children were reporting colour by age 3.2. In 2023 the team issued a correction, revising that figure to between 3.2 and 3.4 years depending on how a colour dream is defined. The conclusions were unchanged.

The Toronto replication, led by Imran Qureshi in 2021, studied 91 children. It found similar ages, but the timing depended on how children were questioned. Open prompts and direct questions about colour produced different results.

**What the pooled analysis shows**

Barros's group found that across both datasets the ordering held: children's colour reports followed measurable growth in language. The ordering was consistent between the two samples.

"The ordering is robust," Barros said. "But the data are equally consistent with two readings. Either dreams change, or children's ability to report them changes. Nothing in these datasets separates the two."

**Reactions**

Desrochers said the analysis supports the ordering her team described. She argued that her group's language control, which adjusted for children's vocabulary, still points to real changes in dreams rather than reporting alone.

Qureshi read the result differently. "This confirms that the evidence cannot yet separate dreams from reports," he said. He noted that his team's finding about question style was an example of how much the method of asking shapes the answer.

Marianne Holt, whose 2022 review with two co-authors called the reports from children under 3 too sparse to draw conclusions, said the pooled analysis does not change her view. "Combining two datasets doesn't create information that neither has," she said. "The data cannot answer the question."

**Where it stands**

The pooled analysis adds weight to one finding, that colour reports and language growth move in a fixed order. It does not settle what that order means. Resolving it would likely require a way of measuring dream content that does not depend on a child's speech, or data from much younger children than either study collected.

For now, the claim that children dream only in black and white until 3 or 4 remains unproven. Researchers agree on when children start reporting colour. They do not agree on what that says about the dreams themselves.

# When Do Children Start Dreaming in Colour?

*A Montreal sleep lab followed 164 children for years and found that colour seems to arrive in dreams around the time language blooms. Whether the dreams change, or only the telling of them, is the open question.*

By Vivienne Tanaka

On a weekday morning in a quiet corridor of Marlowe University in Montreal, a three-year-old in pyjamas is explaining, with great seriousness, that a purple dog chased her down a slide. Her mother, sitting nearby, notes it down. It will become one data point among thousands.

The notebook-and-pyjamas routine is the daily work of the Child Sleep Laboratory, where Celine Desrochers has spent several years trying to answer a question that sounds like a parlour game: what do very young children's dreams look like? A paper published this month in *Nature Neuroscience* offers her team's first large answer. In a group of 164 children followed from infancy into early childhood, the dreams that children and their parents described were, early on, reported without colour. Colour began to appear around ages three to four. By about 3.2 years, half the children were describing dreams with colour in them.

The finding is arresting because it touches a folk belief with a long and tangled history: that some people, or some people at some stages of life, dream in black and white. Surveys of adults have given inconsistent answers over the decades, and researchers have often suspected that what people say about their dreams reflects the media and culture around them as much as anything happening in the sleeping brain. Desrochers wanted to see the transition as it happened, in the youngest dreamers, and to ask what else was changing at the same time.

## Tracking the talkers

The study's design is simple to state and hard to carry out. Families enrolled their children at a young age and returned to the lab repeatedly. At each visit the team measured language: how many words a child understood and used, and how long their sentences ran. Alongside this, they collected whatever could be learned about the children's dreams.

That last phrase carries most of the weight. Nobody can watch a dream. For the youngest children, who cannot yet give an account of a night's sleep, the team relied on parents, who were asked to record anything their child said on waking or during the day that seemed to describe a dream. Older children were asked directly, in the lab, after nights of monitored sleep. The monitoring let the team wake or question children at appropriate moments and know that sleep had actually occurred, but the content of every dream still reached the researchers as words, from a parent or from the child.

The pattern that emerged tracked language closely. Children with bigger vocabularies and longer sentences were more likely to describe colour. The curve that rose with age rose in step with linguistic ability. Before about three, descriptions that included colour were rare. At three to four, they became common. The midpoint, when half the children had reported at least one colourful dream, fell at 3.2 years.

## Dreams, or the telling of dreams?

Here the interpretation forks. One reading holds that the young brain simply cannot yet generate colourful dream imagery, and that the capacity matures. The other holds that children dream in colour long before they can say so, and that what changes is their ability to describe it, or a parent's ability to recognise a description.

Desrochers comes down, with care, on the first side. "I believe the dreams themselves change," she said in an interview. She points to the tight link with language and to the fact that the effect did not simply appear overnight, but built with development. Colour vision in waking life is well established in infants by far earlier ages, she notes, so the shift is unlikely to reflect a missing ability to see colour. Instead, she suggests, the machinery that assembles dream scenes from memory and imagination may take time to come online. Language, on this view, might be a marker of the same underlying maturation, or perhaps a scaffold that helps richer imagery form.

She is also direct about the limits. A child who says "I saw a dog" has not told you whether the dog was brown. A parent who records only what a toddler volunteers will miss what the toddler never thought to mention. The team tried to handle this with standard prompts for the older children, but she acknowledges that the youngest data are the thinnest, and that the youngest data are exactly where the black-and-white claim is strongest.

## An outside view

Raul Obi, an independent sleep researcher who was not involved in the work, read the paper closely and calls it a serious piece of work. "Following this many children over this span of development is difficult and valuable," he said. "The link to language is a real contribution, and it's the sort of thing you only see if you measure both things in the same kids."

But he draws a line between what was measured and what is being concluded. "Everything here depends on reports," he said. "That's not a flaw in this team's method; it's the situation anyone studying dreaming is in. But it means the leap from 'children don't mention colour' to 'children don't dream in colour' is an inference, and a cautious reader should hold it loosely." He notes that the language correlation is itself compatible with both explanations: a child with a larger vocabulary has more words for colour and more fluency in narrating, whether or not the dreams differ.

Obi's bottom line is the one researchers reach for when a result is promising and new. "I'd want to see it replicated, ideally by another lab with a different population and different ways of eliciting reports," he said. "One study, however careful, is where the conversation starts."

## What would settle it

No one currently has a clean way to read a child's dream imagery directly. Brain-imaging approaches that try to decode visual content during sleep exist for adults in limited form, but applying them to toddlers raises practical and ethical hurdles. For now the field is left with better and better ways of asking.

Desrochers says her group plans to keep following the children, and to refine how reports are gathered, including more structured questioning at the age when children first begin to narrate. Whether other laboratories find the same timeline, and whether the language link holds up when the way of collecting reports changes, will determine how much weight the result can bear.

For parents, the practical upshot is modest. A small child's account of a purple dog is a window onto something, though not yet a settled picture of what the child saw. The study suggests that window may widen as words arrive. What lies behind it, in a sleeping three-year-old's head, remains for the moment an open question.

*Vivienne Tanaka is a contributing writer for Sciencewire Magazine.*

**Do babies and toddlers dream in colour? What a new Toronto study does (and doesn't) tell us**

By Fiorella Ribeiro

If you've ever watched your toddler twitch and murmur in their sleep, you've probably wondered what's going on in there. A recent news story has added a fresh question: do very young children dream in colour at all?

The idea that kids dream in black and white until about age three or four has been circulating since 2019. A new study from Toronto suggests the picture is more complicated. Here is what we know.

**The 2019 study**

The original finding came from Celine Desrochers's Child Sleep Laboratory at Marlowe University in Montreal, and was published in *Nature Neuroscience*. The team followed 164 children as they grew up. Before about age three, the dreams that were reported were black and white. Colour appeared in reports at ages three to four, and by 3.2 years, half of the children were describing coloured dreams.

The timing tracked with language: children with larger vocabularies and longer sentences were more likely to report colour. For the youngest children, parents did the reporting. Later, children described their dreams themselves after being woken from monitored sleep.

The study was widely covered, and it became the source of the claim you may have seen shared online.

**The 2021 Toronto study**

This year, Imran Qureshi and his team at a children's hospital sleep clinic in Toronto tried a similar project with 91 children of comparable ages. Their results depended heavily on how the children were questioned. When kids were asked open questions, such as "What did you dream about?", fewer described colour. When they were offered colour choices ("Was it red? Blue?"), many more did.

The team could not say whether children's dreams actually change over these years, or whether what changes is their ability to report them.

"How you ask matters," Qureshi told me. "A two-year-old may not volunteer a colour unless you prompt them, and then prompting can shape the answer."

Desrochers, who was not involved in the Toronto work, remains unconvinced that the difference is only about questioning. "I still believe dreams change," she said. "The pattern we saw in the monitored-sleep reports and in the link to language fits a real developmental shift, though I accept that report methods need more study."

**So, is it true?**

Put plainly: the 2019 finding is influential, but it is not settled. Two research teams have looked at similar ages and reached results that differ, and the difference seems tied to method. Nobody can yet say whether toddlers' dreams are really drained of colour, or whether they simply lack the words, or the habit, of telling us.

**What parents can do**

The practical takeaway is reassuring. You don't need to test your child's dreams for colour, and you don't need to worry if the answer is vague.

- Ask open questions. "What did you dream about?" or "What happened in your dream?" works well, and it avoids putting words in your child's mouth.
- Let them answer in their own way. A toddler's reply might be a single word, a gesture, or "doggy." That's fine.
- Don't press for details like colour. If your child offers them, enjoy it. If not, nothing is wrong.
- Keep it low-stakes. A calm chat after a bad dream matters more than any scientific detail.

Researchers will likely keep refining how they ask young children about sleep, and that may eventually clear things up. Until then, consider the question open, and enjoy hearing what your little one says about their nights.

Sleep researcher. Director, Child Sleep Laboratory, Marlowe University, Montreal.

**Known for**

Desrochers is best known for a 2019 study in *Nature Neuroscience* of 164 children, which reported that young children's dreams change from black-and-white to colour during early childhood. In the study, children under about 3 reported dreams without colour, and colour reports became common at 3 to 4. Half of the children reported colour dreams by age 3.2. The appearance of colour tracked two measures of language development: vocabulary size and sentence length.

The data came from two sources. For the youngest children, the researchers relied on parents' reports. Older children gave their own accounts after being woken from monitored sleep.

The study is widely cited in discussions of how dreaming develops, and it is often summarised in popular accounts as showing that children "dream in black and white until about 3 or 4." The paper's claim was narrower: it concerned reported dreams, and it tied the onset of colour reports to language.

**Correction**

In 2023 Desrochers and her co-authors published a correction to the paper. The age at which half the children reported colour dreams was revised from 3.2 to a range of 3.2 to 3.4, depending on how a colour dream is defined. The correction stated that the main conclusions were unchanged.

**Interpretation**

Desrochers and her group hold that the findings reflect a real change in dreaming rather than merely a change in how children talk about it. In support, they note that the language measures predicted colour reports even after controlling for how much children said in general, which suggests the link is not simply a matter of talkativeness.

**Critical responses**

The interpretation has been contested on several grounds.

Imran Qureshi's 2021 replication in Toronto, with 91 children, found similar ages for the shift. However, the timing depended on whether children were asked open questions or were offered a choice of colour. Qureshi concluded that the design could not distinguish a change in dreaming from a change in children's ability to report it.

Marianne Holt's 2022 review noted that reports from children under 3 are sparse, and argued that the apparent absence of colour in that group could be an artefact of the small and uncertain evidence base rather than a feature of dreams.

A 2025 independent pooled analysis of the available datasets found a consistent ordering: colour reports appear after non-colour reports, and later in children with less developed language. The authors found that this pattern could not be separated from reporting ability.

**Status of the question**

The claim that children dream only in black and white until age 3 to 4 is therefore not established. Researchers on all sides agree that the link between language development and colour dream reports is real and has been found repeatedly. What it means remains disputed: whether colour appears in dreams as children develop, or whether children become able to describe colour that was present all along.

# Journal Club Notes: Do Children Dream in Black and White?

*Posted 14 October 2025 by Esi Ogunleye, PhD student (third year)*

Last Thursday's journal club was led by Dr. Tariq Kader, and it turned into one of the livelier sessions we've had this term. The topic was an old idea that keeps resurfacing: that young children dream only in black and white, and that colour arrives somewhere around age 3 to 4. Dr. Kader picked a paper that supposedly tests it directly, and then, helpfully, made us read what came after. Here is my write-up for anyone who missed it.

## The paper

We started with the 2019 study by Celine Desrochers and colleagues at the Child Sleep Laboratory, Marlowe University, Montreal, published in *Nature Neuroscience*. They followed 164 children. The headline finding was that dream reports from the youngest children were black and white, that colour appeared in reports at around 3 to 4 years, and that half of the children were reporting colour dreams by about 3.2 years.

The part that interested me most was the second finding: the appearance of colour tracked vocabulary size and sentence length. Children with bigger vocabularies and longer sentences were more likely to report colour, regardless of exact age.

The method matters here. For the youngest children, the data came from parent reports. Later on, children reported for themselves after being woken from monitored sleep.

## The follow-ups

- **The 2023 correction.** The authors revised the 3.2 figure to somewhere between 3.2 and 3.4, depending on how a "colour dream" is defined. They stated that their conclusions were unchanged.
- **The 2025 pooled analysis.** This combined data across studies. It found a consistent ordering (black and white first, colour later) but concluded that the pattern could not be separated from children's ability to report.

## What we argued about

Dr. Kader asked us to write our questions on the whiteboard before talking. Ours were:

- **Could parents even tell?** For the youngest children, a parent is interpreting a toddler's fragments of speech. If a two-year-old says "dog" after waking, how would anyone know whether the dog was brown?
- **What does "colour dream" mean?** The 2023 correction shows the answer moves the number. Is it a spontaneous colour word? A reply to a prompt? Does a child who names a colour count differently from one who points at a red cup?
- **What would a better study look like?** We sketched a few ideas: longitudinal designs with the same children tested repeatedly, standardised and non-leading prompts, and some way to separate dreaming from describing. Nobody had a clean solution to that last one.

The room split. Some students thought the language link points toward a reporting artefact: children may have been dreaming in colour all along, and only began to tell us once they had the words. Others argued that the link could be real developmental information, since visual imagery and language might mature together, so that vocabulary is a marker of the underlying ability rather than a mere window onto it. I leaned toward the first reading at the start of the meeting and was less sure by the end.

Dr. Kader's summary was that the language link is real, but its meaning is unsettled. He was careful to say that the data do not tell us that young children dream in black and white, and they do not tell us that they don't. They tell us what children say, and when.

## My own take

What I appreciated was seeing how much of the claim depends on measurement choices. The popular version ("children dream in black and white until age 3 or 4") sounds like a fact about brains. The papers, read closely, describe a fact about reports. Those might be the same thing, but nobody has shown it yet.

I also liked that the correction was treated as ordinary science rather than a scandal. A shift from 3.2 to 3.4 is small, but it was a useful demonstration of how a definition can quietly set the answer.

ABSTRACT

Over the last decade, immunotherapy has rapidly changed the therapeutic landscape and prognosis for many hematologic malignancies and adult solid tumors. Despite this success, immunotherapy for pediatric solid tumors remains in the early stages of development, and significant clinical benefit has yet to be realized, with anti-GD2 for neuroblastoma being the exception. The limited neoepitope expression and paucity of T-cell infiltration into the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment have hampered current established immunotherapies. Here, we review these approaches, highlighting the role of the tumor microenvironment and novel antibody platforms to maximize the full clinical potential of immunotherapy in pediatric oncology.

Introduction

Advances in molecular profiling and next-generation sequencing have jumpstarted successful targeted therapies for adult solid tumors. Such genomics-guided interventions have not been as successful in pediatric solid tumors, largely due to their low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and limited number of actionable or targetable mutations (1). Despite the accelerating pace of immunotherapy in the last decade for adult tumors, low TMB in pediatric cancers also translates into a paucity of neoepitopes and few tumor-infiltrating T cells (TILs), making these “cold” tumors unresponsive to approaches such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI). As such, survival benefit from immunotherapy has not improved among pediatric cancers.

Current treatment paradigms for pediatric solid tumors consist of chemotherapy and surgery, with or without radiation. For children with high-risk metastatic and/or relapsed disease, survival remains poor, and devastating long-term morbidities are often unavoidable. An obvious unmet need exists for less toxic and more effective approaches. Immunotherapy holds promise not just for refractory disease, but in improving long-term survival without significant additive late toxicities. For example, with the integration of anti-GD2 into standard of care, 50% to 60% of children with high-risk neuroblastoma, a disease once incurable, are long-term survivors (2, 3). Nevertheless, the pain associated with treatment, though manageable, remains a clinical challenge, and the promise of immunotherapy in most other pediatric solid tumors remains elusive. Although non-antibody–based platforms remain encouraging, only two vaccines and two cellular therapies have been approved for cancer therapy in the United States and Europe, compared with over 30 approved monoclonal antibodies (mAb). In this review, we summarize immunotherapies for pediatric solid tumors, highlighting known immunologic hurdles unique in pediatric patients with an emphasis on the tumor microenvironment (TME) and focus on classic antibody-based approaches, as well as novel genetically engineered forms to target T cells and precision radiation.

Immunotherapy of Pediatric Tumors: Special Hurdles

Aside from the immunosuppressive tumor milieu, special challenges of immunotherapy in pediatric solid tumors include: (i) a young age and immature immune system; (ii) extent of metastatic spread at diagnosis and fast disease progression, with possible privileged sites, such as the brain, not accessible by standard immunotherapies; (iii) the need to use intensive cytotoxic chemotherapy and/or radiation for induction, which deplete immune cells, especially lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells; (iv) the resulting comorbidities and infections from such intensive therapies requiring antibiotics, with subsequent alterations in the microbiome; (v) the lack of predictive biomarkers for response to immunotherapy, which is further confounded by the accrual of multiple disease types in phase I/II trials and the small number of eligible patients; (vi) the paucity of mutations at diagnosis, resulting in low T-cell clonal frequencies; and (vii) potential for acute and late toxicities in a young child resulting from overaction of the immune system or on-target, off-tumor effects. With such roadblocks in place, the role of immunotherapy in pediatric solid tumors remains at a crossroads.

The TME in Pediatric Solid Tumors

The interaction between tumor cells and the host immune system evolves over time, from initial elimination, to equilibrium, and finally to escape (4). This latter step occurs as tumor cells evolve under pressure through a variety of mechanisms, including the loss of tumor neoantigens; a decrease in MHC class I expression; increased production of immunosuppressive cytokines and immune cells such as regulatory T cells (Treg), M2 macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC); and increased expression of inhibitory receptors and inhibitory ligands on T cells and tumor cells, respectively (i.e., PD-1 and PD-L1), eventually leading to a state of T-cell exhaustion.

By directing T cells to tumor cells, BsAbs have the potential to steer T cells deep into tumors and activate TILs for tumor ablation. Blinatumomab, a BiTE specific for CD3 and CD19, has shown clinical benefit in pediatric patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (30). Using the IgG(L)-scFv platform, polyclonal T cells can be quantitatively driven into solid tumors and can overcome PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition to ablate GD2+ human xenografts in mice (31). Phase I/II studies are underway treating patients with neuroblastoma, osteosarcoma, and other GD2+ tumors with anti-GD2 × CD3) BsAbs (NCT02173093, NCT03806207).

BsAbs can also be used for ex vivo armoring of expanded T cells before reinfusion into patients (NCT02173093; ref. 31), avoiding cytokine storm or neurotoxicity. Detailed structural studies have shown that cis-configuration and inter-binding domain distance are critical parameters for building BsAbs, and the IgG(L)-scFv format could outperform BiTE, BiTE-Fc, heterodimeric constructs, and chemical conjugates (32). Fc silencing is also critical for optimal function of BsAbs (33). Other BsAbs with relevance for pediatric solid cancers are being explored including those targeting HER-2 (34), polysialic acid, LICAM, ROR2, STEAP-1, and those directed against B7-H3 (MGD009) and glypican-3 (ERY-974; Fig. 1). An approach using combinations of target-specific BsAbs for ex vivo armoring could theoretically broaden target coverage, thereby, overcoming tumor heterogeneity while modulating multiple activators or inhibitors on the same effector cell (31).

**Future Strategies of Antibody-Based Therapies: A Reassessment**

Given the many challenges, discovery and preclinical validation of novel immune targets with limited expression on normal human tissues is only the first step, even when utilizing modern omics techniques. Specific antibody clones can be rapidly identified using human IgG transgenic animals, human phage libraries, or humanized single VH domain libraries (35). These antibody binding domains can then be reshaped into chimeric receptors, multivalent and/or multi-specific formats for diagnostic and therapeutic applications. Nonetheless, one should not lose sight of the established targets (e.g., NCI priority list; ref. 36), many of which have already been proven safe in humans, although ineffective in their classic IgG forms. Inhibitory cells in the TME such as fibroblasts, macrophages, Tregs, MDSCs, and type 2 innate lymphoid cells are all potential candidates for immune modulation (8). Vasculature and hypoxia are also viable areas for immune interventions (Supplementary Table S2).

Beyond target discovery and the TME, overarching pharmacokinetics and amplifying antibody effector functions are major unmet needs. RIT has not materialized because of the inability of single-step whole IgG or its fragments to deliver curative doses. Despite tumor to tissue ratios appearing favorable with time delay, low therapeutic indices are typical. Multistep pretargeted RIT was a major breakthrough (37), and issues regarding immunogenicity, renal retention, and protocol complexity have been resolved (38). With these improvements, curative radiation doses are now possible in several target systems and animal models without chemical, clinical, or histologic toxicities. The coupling of radiotherapy with immunotherapy to enhance immunogenic tumor cell death could be further exploited for neoantigen induction, T-cell infiltration, and modulation of Tregs and the TME (Fig. 1).

Despite the many immune strategies beyond ICIs (Supplementary Table S2), classic IgG-based strategies have relatively low potency. Beyond CAR-T therapies, T-cell BsAbs represent a different approach, and among the many different platforms, the minimal and optimal structural requirements need to be elucidated (32). Already, some of these antibody platforms, such as BiTEs, heterodimeric or IgG(L)-scFv formats, have yielded promising preclinical and clinical data. Although phase I/II studies of these single agents will soon weed out less-effective candidates, combinatorial principles in pediatric solid tumors are needed to advance long-term cures with low toxicities. One old combinatorial strategy that has proven successful in neuroblastoma is the addition of cytokines to anti-GD2. The use of IL15 (with or without its receptor, IL15Rα; ref. 39) and IL21 (40), which can enhance both NK cells and CD8+ T cells, is timely (Fig. 1). Combining ICIs with CAR-T therapy or BsAbs is logical. Similarly, interfering with key immune inhibitory molecules such as TGFβ (41) is now possible, with enhanced ADCC seen with TGFβRII blockade in combination with ex vivo–activated NK cells and anti-GD2 (42).

On the whole, the timing of immunotherapy in relation to other cytotoxic therapies must be carefully optimized. Until chemotherapy and radiotherapy are reduced or eliminated, lymphocyte depletion and immunosuppression that occur after such therapies can hamper the immune system (43). However, passive mAb therapy, even after intensive chemotherapy, can be very effective in the consolidative setting, as shown with anti-GD2, because ADCC and cell-mediated cytotoxicity are dependent on complement and myeloid cells such as neutrophils, which recover faster than lymphocytes. Concurrent administration of antibodies with induction chemotherapy also may be beneficial, with response rates of close to 80% and encouraging survival outcomes seen in the phase II setting with anti-GD2 (44). For T cell–based therapies, regeneration of lymphocytes via cytokines or harvesting T cells prior to administering cytotoxic therapy are possible strategies to jumpstart lymphocytes. Arming rejuvenated T cells with CARs or BsAb ex vivo is another passive immunotherapy approach.
Once the immune system is recovered, a vaccine approach could also be introduced, whether antibody-based (e.g., GD2 vaccine) or cell-based. Collaboration among major cancer centers and harmonization of immune monitoring (45) should facilitate data sharing, dissemination of novel discoveries, and transformation from clinical trials to standards of care.

Concluding Remarks

Immunotherapy is an established cancer treatment modality, proven effective in some subtypes, with potential to be applicable to all pediatric cancers. Although chemotherapy has a proven benefit on survival, the quality of survival can be improved as immunotherapy is further optimized. Although childhood cancers share biology and issues confronting adult patients, their development and drivers can be different. In a rapidly evolving landscape in pediatric immunotherapy, where large clinical trials are not possible, a multicenter approach needs to be developed so that we move beyond the crossroads.

**Developmental Review of Early Cognition and Report**

*Review Article · 2025*

# Asking Small Children About Dreams: How Question Format Shapes Reports of Unobservable Internal Events

**Luana Cardoso and Rafael Osorio**

*Department of Developmental Psychology*

---

## Abstract

Preschoolers are often asked about experiences that no adult can check: what they thought, what they felt, what they saw while asleep. Their answers depend heavily on how the question is put. We review the forensic interviewing literature on question format and suggestibility, then apply it to one recent debate, whether children's dreams are colourless until about age three or four. Two studies on this question used different prompt formats and reached partly different conclusions about timing. We argue that the format difference is a central part of the disagreement and cannot be treated as a side detail. We recommend that studies of young children's internal events report results under both open and closed prompts and include acquiescence checks. The question of whether early dreams change or only early reporting changes is, in our reading, still open.

---

## 1. Why internal events are a hard case

Developmental researchers have long known that a young child's answer reflects the child, the topic and the question together. When the event is observable, an interviewer can check the answer against an independent record. Dreams, intentions, sensations and imagery offer no such check. The child's report is the only evidence, so the format of the prompt carries more weight.

Forensic interview research has built a detailed picture of this problem over several decades. Three findings matter here.

First, open prompts ("Tell me about your dream") elicit less information than focused prompts, but what they elicit is more accurate. Preschoolers often give brief answers to open invitations, which leads interviewers to follow up with narrower questions.

Second, closed and option-posing prompts increase the amount of information while reducing its reliability. Forced-choice questions ("Was it red or blue?") require the child to select among alternatives the interviewer has chosen. Young children frequently choose one even when neither fits, and their choices may reflect the order of the options, the last word heard, or a wish to comply.

Third, yes/no questions are subject to acquiescence. Children around two to three years old show a documented tendency to say "yes" regardless of content, and this tendency declines through the preschool years. A prompt that invites assent can therefore produce apparent positive reports that do not track any internal state.

These regularities were developed mainly for reports of external events such as a visit, an injury or an interaction. We think they apply at least as strongly to inner experience, where the child has no reference event to anchor the account.

## 2. The claim as a test case

The claim that young children dream only in black and white until age three or four gives a clean test of these ideas. It concerns an internal event, it depends entirely on verbal report, and it has been studied with different question formats.

### 2.1 The 2019 study

Celine Desrochers and colleagues at the Child Sleep Laboratory, Marlowe University, Montreal, published a study in *Nature Neuroscience* in 2019 that followed 164 children. They reported that colour first appeared in dream reports at ages three to four, and that half of the children reported colour by age 3.2. The Desrochers group interprets these results as showing a real developmental change in the content of dreaming: colour, on this view, enters dreams as visual cognition matures.

### 2.2 The 2021 replication

In 2021 a team led by Imran Qureshi, working in a children's hospital sleep clinic in Toronto, studied 91 children. They found colour dream reports at about the same ages as the Montreal study. That is a meaningful agreement, since a replication in a different clinical setting with a smaller sample could easily have diverged.

The Toronto team also found that the timing of colour reports depended strongly on prompt format. When children were asked open questions about their dreams, colour reports appeared later. When children were offered colour-choice prompts, colour reports appeared earlier. Qureshi's team stated that they could not tell whether the dreams themselves changed or only the children's ability to report them.

## 3. Reading the format effect

We think the two formats err in opposite directions, and that the opposition has a recognisable developmental explanation.

### 3.1 Colour-choice prompts

A prompt that names colours supplies a vocabulary cue. A two-year-old who cannot spontaneously produce a colour term may be able to recognise one when it is offered. That recognition is not itself evidence that the dream contained colour. It may show only that the child knows the word and has been invited to apply it.

Such prompts also invite yes-bias. If the interviewer asks whether the dream had colours in it, a two-year-old inclined to assent will say yes. If the format instead asks the child to choose among colours, the child must still produce an answer, and the pressure to answer favours an affirmative colour report over a statement that the dream had no colour. The earlier onset under colour-choice prompts is therefore partly predictable from the format alone, whatever the dream contained.

### 3.2 Open questions

Open questions have the opposite problem. They underreport anything the child cannot name. A child may have had a vividly coloured dream and still say only "there was a dog," because colour is not what the child considers worth mentioning, or because the child lacks the term or the habit of describing it. Colour is an attribute, and young children's narratives tend to mention objects and actions before attributes. Under open prompts, later onset of colour reports could reflect the development of descriptive language rather than the development of dreaming.

### 3.3 What follows

Neither format yields an unbiased estimate. One inflates early reports through cueing and acquiescence; the other deflates them through limits on spontaneous description. The true onset, if there is a single one, may lie between the two curves, but the data do not locate it. The agreement between Montreal and Toronto on the ages at which colour appears is then informative about reports and less informative about dreams: it shows that the pattern of reporting is reproducible, while leaving open what the reports track.

## 4. Where the researchers stand

The positions of the groups involved differ, and we do not think they should be blurred.

The Desrochers group holds that its findings reflect real changes in dreaming. Qureshi holds that the evidence cannot yet separate a change in dreaming from a change in reporting. These are not simply two readings of identical evidence offered in the same spirit. One is a positive interpretation; the other is a judgement about what the existing design can establish. We note that the Toronto result, with its sensitivity to format, is the kind of finding that makes the second position harder to dismiss, though it does not refute the first.

Our own position is closer to Qureshi's. This is a view about inference from report data, not a claim that early dreams are in fact coloured. The assertion that children dream only in black and white until three or four is, at present, a hypothesis supported by report patterns that a format account can partly explain. It is not settled.

## 5. Recommendations

We suggest four practices for studies of this kind, and for studies of young children's internal events more generally.

1. **Report both formats.** Present results separately for open prompts and for option-posing prompts, rather than pooling them or choosing one. Readers can then see how much of the estimated onset depends on the format.

2. **Include acquiescence checks.** Insert control items with known answers, such as asking about a feature the child could not have experienced, and record how often children assent. A child who agrees with implausible items is not providing reliable positive reports on target items.

3. **Vary option order and include a "neither" option.** This reduces recency and primacy effects in forced-choice questions and gives children a legitimate way to decline all alternatives.

4. **Separate vocabulary from experience.** Assess colour-term knowledge independently, for instance with a waking naming task. A child who reliably names colours while awake but never reports them in dreams offers different evidence from a child who cannot name colours at all.

None of these steps solves the problem of unverifiable report. They make visible how much of a result depends on the instrument.

## 6. Limits of this review

We have not reanalysed the data from either study, and our account of the format effect rests on the published descriptions. We have not weighed the possibility that some physiological or neurological marker could eventually bear on the question independently of verbal report. We also do not address dream reports from older children, where the format problem is milder, or reports from adults about childhood dreams, which add memory distortion. The forensic literature we draw on concerns mostly reports of external events, and the extension to dreaming is an inference, not an established result.

## 7. Conclusion

The question of when children's dreams acquire colour is a useful case because the whole answer passes through the child's words, and those words are shaped by the question. Two studies agree on ages and diverge on how those ages depend on format. Until designs can distinguish a change in dreaming from a change in the ability to describe dreams, the claim should be reported as unresolved.

---

# The dreams we can't ask about

*Researchers have found that young children's dream reports gain colour at around the age of three. What that means is another question.*

**By Astrid Lindgren**

MONTREAL — At 6:40 on a grey morning, Sanjay Ruiz is watching a two-year-old sleep on a monitor. The boy lies curled on his side in a narrow bed, one arm over a stuffed rabbit. On the screen beside him, a trace of brain activity rises and falls in slow waves. Ruiz, a technician at the Child Sleep Laboratory at Marlowe University, has done this for eleven years. He leans toward the screen as though the trace might say something.

"People think we can see the dream," he says quietly, so as not to wake anyone. "We can see that the brain is doing something that looks like dreaming sleep. What the child is seeing, we can't see. And at this age, we can't really ask."

That sentence sums up a problem that has occupied a small group of researchers for years. A widely repeated claim holds that children dream only in black and white until about the age of three or four, after which colour arrives. It appears in parenting magazines and trivia columns, and, in confident form, on the internet. The scientific picture is thinner and more interesting than the slogan. There is a real finding behind it. Researchers disagree about what the finding means, and no study has ever directly measured the colour content of a very young child's dreams.

**The study that started it**

The best-known source is a 2019 paper in *Nature Neuroscience* from Celine Desrochers and colleagues at the Marlowe laboratory. The team followed 164 children, collecting reports of dreams from them and from their parents over several years. Younger children's reports were overwhelmingly described in black and white, or with no colour mentioned at all. Colour appeared in the reports of children aged three to four. By the team's original estimate, half of the children were reporting colour dreams by 3.2 years.

In 2023 the authors issued a correction. The figure of 3.2 years depended on how a "colour dream" was defined, and under the different definitions the half-way point fell between 3.2 and 3.4 years. The conclusions, the correction said, were unchanged.

The more striking result was about language. Children's vocabulary and sentence length tracked the arrival of colour in their reports. And language measures predicted colour even after the researchers controlled for how much the children said in general. A talkative child with a small vocabulary was not more likely to report colour than a quiet child with a large one.

"We were surprised by how specific it was," Desrochers says in her office, where a shelf holds stacked picture books and a box of crayons used in the interviews. "It wasn't just that older children talk more. It was that the particular kind of language development predicted colour in the reports."

She is careful about what the paper does and does not say. "We studied what children report," she says. "I would never claim we've watched a toddler's dream in black and white."

**The replication**

In 2021, a team led by Imran Qureshi at a Toronto laboratory tried to reproduce the pattern. They studied 91 children across the same age range and found a similar developmental shift. But the result depended on how the questions were put. When children were asked open questions, such as "What did you see?", colour was reported less often than when they were prompted directly about colours.

"That is the whole difficulty," Qureshi says by video call. "If I change the question and the answer changes, then I have to ask whether the dream changed or whether the report changed. And the honest answer from our data is that we can't tell."

His team did not treat the result as a failure to replicate, and does not describe it as one. The age pattern held. What the Toronto data added was doubt about the interpretation.

**The sceptical review**

That doubt was sharpened in a 2022 review by Marianne Holt and two co-authors, which examined the evidence for the claim. Holt's argument has two parts. Reports from children under three, she writes, are too sparse and too dependent on parents to support strong conclusions. A two-year-old may say a handful of words about a night's sleep, and a parent must decide what they mean. The second part concerns the language link. It is plausible, the review says, that language development changes dreaming, but it may equally be a measurement artefact: children with richer language simply have more ways to say that something was red.

"I don't think anyone is wrong about the data," Holt says. "I think the data can't yet separate two stories. In one, the dream itself changes. In the other, our window onto the dream changes. A claim that children dream in black and white is a claim about the first story, and we mostly have evidence about the second."

Desrochers has answered that the control for general talkativeness argues against the simplest version of the artefact explanation, though not against subtler ones. Holt agrees that it narrows the options. Neither thinks the question is closed.

**A morning in the lab**

By 7:15 the boy has woken. His father, Goran Vogel, is waiting in the corridor with a thermos of coffee he hasn't touched. Vogel is a logistics planner who enrolled his son, Mattias, after reading about the research. He is under no illusion that he can read his son's mind. He wants to try anyway.

Mattias sits on the floor with the rabbit and talks. It is the fluent, tuneless babble of a child with perhaps a hundred and fifty words. Vogel kneels beside him.

"Dog," Vogel says, translating. "He said dog. Or door. Hm." He tries again. "Big. Big... something. Was it a dog?"

Mattias looks at him and says "Boo."

"Blue?" Vogel says, brightening, and then catches himself. "Or boo, like a ghost. Or he's asking for his bottle. That's the thing. I'm sure I'm hearing what I want to hear."

Ruiz, standing in the doorway, smiles. This is a familiar scene. The protocol asks parents to write down exactly what the child says without guessing, and to avoid leading questions. Vogel is good at the first part and bad at the second, like almost everyone.

"If I wrote down 'blue,' I'd be putting my own word in his mouth," Vogel says. "And then someone reads my notes in ten years and thinks two-year-olds dream in blue."

It is, in miniature, the problem the field faces. A child of two cannot say whether the dog was brown or whether there was a dog at all. Any data about early dreams passes through an adult's interpretation. The older children in Desrochers's study, who could answer questions, provide firmer ground. The youngest provide almost none.

**What would settle it**

Researchers talk about several routes, none of them easy. One is to find indirect markers: patterns of brain activity that correspond to colour processing in adults during dreaming sleep, which might be looked for in children. Ruiz is sceptical. "The recordings we can do on a sleeping two-year-old are blunt," he says. "They would not tell us about hue."

Another is longitudinal work that follows the same children from before they speak to well after, matching early behaviour with later recollection. Qureshi favours this, though he notes that memory for dreams is unreliable even in adults. A third is to vary question formats systematically within one study, so that the effect of the question can be measured rather than guessed at. Holt would like to see exactly that.

Desrochers has begun planning a follow-up with older preschoolers, using picture-choice tasks that do not require a child to produce colour words. If children who cannot yet name many colours still pick colour pictures to match a dream, that would weigh against the view that language is the whole story. If they do not, it would be consistent with either view.

**Where it stands**

What the researchers share is more than the headline claim suggests. All three groups accept that there is a real association between age, language development and the appearance of colour in children's dream reports. The disagreement is about what the association is evidence of. One reading is that the dreams themselves are changing as the brain matures. Another is that the dreams are colourful earlier, and children cannot yet say so. A third combines the two.

None of this is visible in the confident version of the claim that circulates online. "Toddlers dream in black and white" is a statement about experience. The evidence is about reports.

By 7:40, Mattias has lost interest in the conversation and is feeding the rabbit an invisible breakfast. Vogel finishes his notes. Under "what the child said," he has written *dog? door? boo*, and under it, in smaller handwriting, *no colour words. Don't know.*

Ruiz checks the form and nods. "That," he says, "is a perfectly good result."

*A replication study found that how you ask matters as much as who you ask*

By Aisling Henriksen

TORONTO — The waiting room of the children's sleep clinic is painted the colour of weak tea, and someone has tried hard to make it kinder. There is a mural of a fox asleep under a blanket of stars. There is a basket of worn picture books, a low table with a half-finished jigsaw of a harbour, and a row of small chairs that no adult has ever sat in comfortably.

Down the corridor, behind a door with a paper moon taped to it, are the beds where children come to be watched while they sleep, wired gently at the scalp and finger, with a camera glowing softly in the corner.

It was in rooms like these that Imran Qureshi and his colleagues spent much of their time a few years ago, working through a question that sounds simple and turns out not to be: when do children start to dream in colour?

## An old claim, newly tested

The idea that young children dream only in black and white until the age of three or four has circulated for years, and it got a firm footing in 2019. That was when Celine Desrochers, working at Marlowe University in Montreal, published a study in *Nature Neuroscience* following 164 children. Her team found that reports of colour in dreams began to appear at around three to four years old, with half of the children reporting colour by about 3.2 years.

It was a tidy result, and tidy results invite a second look. Qureshi, who leads research at the Toronto clinic, set out in 2021 to repeat the work with a smaller group of 91 children. "A replication isn't an accusation," he told me, settling into a chair in a small office lined with binders. "It's what you do when something interesting has been found. You see if it holds when someone else does it, in another place, with other children."

## Two ways of asking

The clinic's approach turned on a detail that might seem minor to an adult and is anything but to a child: the wording of the question.

In one version, a researcher would wake a child, or speak to them after a night's sleep, and ask an open question. *Tell me about your dream.* Then she would wait, which is harder than it sounds with a four-year-old, and let the child say whatever came.

In the other version, the researcher offered a choice. *Was it in colour, or black and white?*

Watching a session is a lesson in how differently small children answer. Asked to tell about a dream, many shrug, or say "I don't remember," or describe the dog from the day before. Some offer a single, vivid fragment: a slide, a lost shoe, a big yellow bus. Asked to pick between colour and black and white, they nearly always pick, quickly and with confidence, though not always in the same direction from one day to the next.

"A choice question gives a child something to hold on to," Qureshi said. "But it also supplies the idea that there's an answer to give. Some children will just pick one."

## What the numbers showed

The headline finding is a modest one. Colour reports in Qureshi's group appeared at about the same ages as in the 2019 study, in the third and fourth years of life. On that point, the two sets of children looked alike.

The timing, though, depended heavily on how the children were asked. With open questions, colour came up later and less often. With colour-choice prompts, it appeared earlier and more readily. The same child could look like a colour dreamer or a black-and-white dreamer depending on the question put to them.

That leaves a puzzle Qureshi is candid about. "We could not tell whether the dreams themselves change as children grow, or only the ability to report them," he said. "Those are two very different things, and our data can't separate them." A very young child may dream in colour and lack the words, or the memory, or the patience to say so. Or the dreams may truly develop. Both stories fit what the team saw.

He was equally careful about what the work does not say. "I want to be clear that I am not calling the 2019 work wrong," he said. "Dr. Desrochers's team asked a hard question and measured something real. What we found is that the answer is sensitive to method, and that matters for how confidently anyone can state it."

I wrote to Desrochers at Marlowe University to ask whether she wished to comment. This article went to press without a reply.

## A question left open

Outside observers tend to land in the same careful place. Colm Alcantara, a child psychologist who was not involved in either study, has spent years listening to young children describe their inner lives, and he is wary of tidy answers.

"If you ask a three-year-old what a dream is, you will get a wonderful range of replies, and not all of them are about sleep," he said. "So when we talk about what a child dreams, we are almost always talking about what a child says about dreaming. That is a long way from the dream itself."

Is the question settled? "No," he said, without hesitation. "I'd say the question is open. Both studies are useful, and they point in a broadly similar direction on timing. What they can't yet tell us is whether young children dream in black and white, or simply can't tell us what they see."

By the time I leave, the boy with the owl has been called through the door with the paper moon. His mother stays behind and takes up the jigsaw, fitting a piece of harbour sky into place. A technician passes with a trolley of soft headbands and small tubes of paste.

In the morning someone will crouch beside her and ask, gently, about her dreams. What she says, and how the question is phrased, will become one small data point in a question that is still very much alive.

Qureshi walks me to the lift. He mentions, almost as an aside, that his own daughter once told him she had dreamed of a purple whale. "I didn't ask if it was in colour," he said, smiling. "I think I was afraid she'd say yes, and I'd be tempted to count it."

**When Do Children Start Dreaming in Colour? A Result That Depends on How the Data Are Gathered**

*Barnaby Obi*

Few findings from developmental sleep research travel as easily as this one: young children dream in black and white, and colour arrives around age 3 or 4. It makes a tidy headline. It also makes a good worked example for students of research methods, because the tidiness depends on how the evidence was collected.

In 2019, Celine Desrochers and colleagues at the Child Sleep Laboratory, Marlowe University (Montreal), published a longitudinal study in *Nature Neuroscience*. They followed 164 children over time. Before about age 3, children's dreams were reported as black and white. Colour began to appear in reports at ages 3 to 4, and by age 3.2 half the children were reporting colour dreams. The result is important. Its timing has been replicated, and the link to language development is striking. How to interpret it remains open.

**How the data were gathered**

Children slept under monitoring, and dream reports were collected after waking. For the youngest children the reports came from parents. Once children could give accounts themselves, the reports came from the children.

Note carefully what was and was not observed. The *sleep* was monitored. The *dream content* was not. No one watched a dream; researchers recorded only what was said about one. To my knowledge, no study has directly measured the colour content of very young children's dreams, and it is not obvious how one would.

This matters because the pattern in the reports fits more than one reading. Dreams may truly change from colourless to coloured. Alternatively, the ability to describe and remember colour in a dream may develop alongside language, so that reports change even if the dreams do not. The tracking of colour with vocabulary and sentence length is compatible with either. The data do not choose between them.

**What to take from this**

A well-conducted study can establish a robust pattern in reports without establishing what lies behind them. Being careful about that gap is not scepticism about the result. It is part of reading it properly.

1. What does "report" mean for a 2-year-old? Who is the reporter, and what might be lost or added in the telling?
2. Describe one way parent reports and child self-reports could differ systematically.

# Separating Changing Dreams from Changing Reports: A Proposal for a Registered Multi-Site Study

**Dmitri Antonelli** and **Saanvi Reddy**

*Commentary proposing a registered study. This piece has not been peer reviewed as an empirical report; no data are presented. The design below is offered for community comment before registration.*

## Summary

Whether very young children dream in black and white before switching to colour is a question that has attracted wide public attention and a small, careful literature. The best-known finding places the transition at around age three to four. We argue that the existing evidence cannot yet tell us whether the dreams change or whether the children's ability to describe them does. We propose a registered, randomised, multi-site design intended to pull these two possibilities apart, and we invite the principal investigators of the two main prior studies to join us.

We take no position here on which answer is correct. Our aim is to describe a study whose results would be informative under either.

## Background

The claim in its popular form is that children dream only in black and white until about age three or four. The scientific basis for it is narrower than the popular form suggests, so it is worth stating precisely what has been reported.

**The 2019 Desrochers study.** Celine Desrochers and colleagues at the Child Sleep Laboratory, Marlowe University, Montreal, published their findings in *Nature Neuroscience* in 2019. The sample was 164 children. The authors reported that children under about age three gave dream reports that were black and white, that colour appeared in reports at ages three to four, and that half of the children reported colour by 3.2 years. A 2023 correction revised the later part of the curve, placing the remaining transition at 3.2 to 3.4 years. Importantly for our purposes, the team also tracked vocabulary and sentence length alongside the colour reports.

**The 2021 Toronto replication.** Imran Qureshi led a replication in Toronto with 91 children. Its most discussed contribution concerned question format: how children were asked about their dreams appeared to shape what they said about colour. That result does not show the original finding was wrong, but it shows that the elicitation method matters and was not isolated in the first study.

**The 2022 review.** Marianne Holt's 2022 review assembled the available work and drew attention to the same difficulty from a different angle: the studies agree on a rough ordering of events, but their measures of dream colour and of children's linguistic development are entangled in ways that make causal reading hard.

**The 2025 pooled analysis.** A pooled analysis published in 2025 combined the available datasets. It found a consistent ordering across them: reports of colour tend to arrive later than reports of black-and-white or colourless content, and the timing clusters in the age range given above. It did not find that the timing could be separated from reporting ability. In other words, the age at which children begin to report colour in dreams tracks the age at which they become able to report dreams in detail, and the data as they stand do not allow us to say which is driving which.

That last sentence is the problem this proposal addresses.

## The Inferential Problem

Consider two simplified accounts.

*Account A (changing dreams).* The visual content of dreams changes during early childhood. Younger children's dreams contain less colour, or none, and older children's contain more. Reports reflect this change.

*Account B (changing reports).* Dream content may be stable, or may change differently, but young children lack the language, memory, or conversational skill to convey colour. Reports become colourful as reporting improves.

These accounts are not mutually exclusive, and the truth may involve both. The difficulty is that most of the same observations follow from either. A child who says little about her dream will say little about its colour. A child asked "what colour was it?" may answer differently from a child asked "tell me about your dream." If colour terms are acquired at about the ages in question, a lack of colour words is expected on Account B alone.

## Proposed Design

We propose a registered study with pre-specified hypotheses, analysis plan, and exclusion rules, deposited before data collection begins and carried out across multiple sites.

**1. Randomised question format.** Children at each site will be randomly assigned to one of two elicitation conditions. In the open condition, the child is asked to describe the dream with no prompt about colour. In the colour-choice condition, the child is asked directly about colour, with options offered, including a "no colour" option and an "I don't know" option. Randomisation will be stratified by age band and site. This borrows directly from the question-format concern raised in the Toronto replication and treats it as a manipulated variable rather than a nuisance.

**2. Separate measurement of colour naming.** Each child will complete a standardised task assessing whether they can reliably name and match colours in waking life. This lets us ask whether a child who fails to report colour in a dream was also unable to name colours when shown them. Children who cannot yet do so are not informative about dream colour and will be analysed as a distinct group.

**3. Separate measurement of general reporting skill.** Independently of colour, we will measure how well each child can report on any past event: for instance, a recent play session, with details verifiable by the research team. Measures of vocabulary and sentence length, which the Desrochers study used, will be included, along with an index of narrative detail for the verifiable event. If dream-colour reports track general reporting skill even when age is held constant, that supports Account B. If colour reports vary independently of it, that is harder to explain by Account B alone.

**4. Inclusion of late talkers.** We will deliberately recruit children identified as late talkers. Their language development lags their age, so they offer a way to decouple chronological age from reporting ability, which are correlated in typical samples. If transitions in reported colour follow age, that points one way; if they follow language level, it points the other.

**5. Registered analysis.** The primary analyses will model colour reporting as a function of age, colour-naming ability, general reporting skill, and question format, including their interactions. We will register what pattern of results would count as support for each account, including results that would be ambiguous, before any data are seen.

## What the Study Cannot Do

We want to be direct about the limit. Every design of this kind, including ours, depends on reports. To our knowledge no study has measured the colour content of very young children's dreams directly, and we do not claim that our design does so. What it can do is narrow the space of explanations by testing whether the pattern of reports behaves as Account A or Account B predicts. It cannot show what a two-year-old actually experiences during sleep.

It is possible that the study will return a result that favours neither account, or one that supports a mixture. We think that would still be worth knowing. It is also possible that the design's reliance on verbal reports will make the question unanswerable with currently available methods, and that progress will require some measure of dream content that does not depend on language. We would welcome proposals for such measures, though we are not aware of a validated one.

## Practical Considerations

Multi-site recruitment is intended to give us adequate numbers in each age band and in the late-talker group, which is likely to be the hardest to fill. Sites will use shared protocols, shared training for interviewers, and recordings coded by raters who do not know the assigned condition or the child's language scores. Ethical review will be sought at each site. Parents will be told that the study concerns children's descriptions of their dreams, and the procedures are designed to avoid suggesting to children that their dreams should have any particular content.

## An Invitation

The studies discussed above came from groups with direct experience of the practical difficulties of interviewing very young children about sleep. We therefore invite **Celine Desrochers** and **Imran Qureshi**, and their teams, to join the proposed consortium, to critique the protocol, and to help adapt their existing materials so that the new data can be compared with theirs. We would likewise welcome comment from **Marianne Holt**, whose review shaped our framing of the problem, on whether the design addresses the concerns she raised. We hope to finalise the protocol and register it after incorporating feedback received in the coming months.
