**Journal of Egyptian Archaeology**
**Reply and Commentary**

**If the Hedgehogs Were Kept: Feeding, Management and the Granary Accounts at Tell Rahiya**

*Henrik Solvang*
Nordic Institute of Egyptology, Cairo

---

**1. Purpose of this reply**

The 2022 conference volume contains several sceptical chapters on my 2018 reading of the Tell Rahiya material. Those chapters deserve a direct answer, and this reply gives one. I do not return to the question of whether the hedgehog remains were deposited in the granary complex by people, because the critics and I agree on more than the volume's rhetoric suggests. My claim, and the reason for this reply, is narrower. It is a supposition: *if* the animals were kept in the granaries as the 2018 reading proposes, what would the ration entries have to show about who fed them and what they ate? I work through that conditional here, and I say at the outset that it has not been established.

**2. What is secure**

Three things are not in dispute and I do not want the argument below to be read as resting on anything less.

First, the excavated assemblage of 212 hedgehog skeletons is secure in its stratigraphy. The recovery records, the sieving protocols and the bone catalogue are consistent with one another, and the animals come from fourteen rooms of the granary complex, not from the open-air midden to which some earlier reports assigned a portion of them.

Second, the shard. It bears the partial ink text that the 2018 article discussed, and its findspot is tied to the ration-accounting layer, not to a later disturbance. Its reading remains provisional in places, but its context is not contested.

Third, the interpretation is contested. The sceptical chapters argue, with some force, that the accumulation of carcasses could be explained by natural death in a dry storage environment with abundant invertebrate prey, by intrusive burrowing, or by deposition in a single event unconnected with the grain store. I do not think these explanations fit the spatial distribution as well as the managed-keeping reading does. But fit is not proof, and I have never claimed otherwise.

**3. The managed-keeping reading**

On the managed-keeping reading, the hedgehogs were held deliberately in the granaries, presumably to reduce the populations of snakes and mice that threatened stored cereal. I regard this as the best available fit to the spatial and taphonomic evidence. It is not demonstrated. The argument below is what would follow if it were true.

**4. What the ration entries would imply**

The granary accounts record issues of cereal, legumes and a small quantity of what the scribes call, in the transliterated reading I follow, *the portion of the small ones*. Several critics have treated this phrase as too vague to bear weight. I accept that the phrase admits more than one reading. Nevertheless, if the animals were kept in the granaries over any sustained period, the accounts would have to satisfy several conditions, and these conditions can be checked against the existing entries.

*(a) A regular keeper.* A stock of over two hundred animals across fourteen rooms cannot have been fed by visiting labour on an irregular basis. The entries recur at intervals that correspond to the monthly rhythm of the other issues. If the portion is real, someone was assigned to receive it, measure it and deliver it. The accounts list a small set of recurring personnel against the issues. Whether any one of them is the keeper is not stated, and I do not assume it. But the supposition requires that the issue was made to a named or titled individual, and I think it is worth testing which of the recurring names appear against the portion entries and which do not.

*(b) A fixed diet.* Entries that record the same quantity over many months would imply a standard ration rather than an opportunistic one. The supposition therefore predicts a relatively uniform portion. The sceptics are right that the sample of dated entries is small, and that the recorded quantities vary by a factor that could reflect either diet change, seasonal adjustment or simple scribal inconsistency. If the variation is real, the managed-keeping reading must explain it, and I have not yet done so satisfactorily.

*(c) A supply chain.* A fixed portion implies that the foodstuff was stored, counted and drawn from the same system as the grain. This is consistent with the accounts' structure, but it would also be consistent with the portion being a small allocation for some other purpose entirely. I flag this because it is one of the places where the supposition could fail.

**5. Where the supposition is weakest**

I want to be direct about the points on which the sceptical chapters are correct. The accounts do not name the animals as the recipients of the portion. The word I translate as *small ones* could refer to young animals of another kind, or to a category of stored food for people of low status. The taphonomic evidence can be read in more than one way, and I accept that a reading which does not require keeping at all remains open. Anyone who wishes to adopt the sceptical position is entitled to do so on present evidence.

What I would resist is the claim that the managed-keeping reading has been refuted. It has been made less certain, which is a different thing.

**6. What would bear on the question**

The most important evidence is not in the granary accounts. A stable isotope programme on the hedgehog bone collagen, together with an age-at-death study of the skeletons, was begun in 2023. The analysis is not yet published, and I do not cite its results here. If the animals were fed from the granary portion, their isotopic signature should reflect a diet dominated by the cereal and legume products that the accounts describe, and not one drawn mainly from the surrounding landscape. Their age profile should also tell us whether the population was maintained over time, with replacement, or whether it reflects a single mass event. Either outcome would bear directly on the supposition. Had I published on the basis of the isotope data alone, I would have been claiming more than the evidence allows. I wait for the full dataset.

I will say only this. If the isotope results show a diet consistent with provisioning from the granary, and if the age profile indicates replacement over several seasons, the managed-keeping reading would move from best fit to a strongly supported position, and the ration entries would acquire the role I have assigned them. If they do not, the reading should be abandoned, and I would say so.

**7. Conclusion**

The argument of this reply is conditional. If the Tell Rahiya hedgehogs were kept in the granaries, the ration entries imply a regular keeper, a fixed diet and a supply chain integrated with the grain accounts. I have tried to show what each of these implications requires and where the existing record fails to meet it. I do not claim that the animals were kept. I claim that the managed-keeping reading makes testable predictions, that some of them are already partly met, and that the decisive test is the isotope and age data not yet in print. I am grateful to the sceptical contributors for forcing these predictions into the open.

---

*Henrik Solvang is Senior Research Fellow at the Nordic Institute of Egyptology, Cairo. Correspondence to the editors of the Journal of Egyptian Archaeology.*

**Cairo Graduate Papers in Egyptology**
Vol. 14, Commentary Section

**If the Hedgehogs Were There: Working Through the Granary Claim as a Supposition**

*Dalia Chaudhry*

I am a doctoral student, not a specialist in faunal remains, and I came to the Tell Rahiya debate late. I have read the 2018 excavation report and the two rounds of response that followed in 2020 and 2022. The senior scholars involved are far more confident than I am that the claim is wrong, or at least unproven. I am not sure of that. What I want to do here is set that uncertainty aside for a few pages and ask a narrower question: if the claim is true, what follows from it?

The claim is that ancient Egyptians kept hedgehogs in their granaries to keep out snakes and mice. The 2018 report describes a small cluster of hedgehog remains in a storage context at Tell Rahiya, associated with grain residue and rodent gnawing. The 2020 response argued that the association was disturbance, not deposit: the bones could have come from a later burrow. The 2022 pieces split on whether the grain residue was even in primary position. I will not try to adjudicate that here. I will take the remains as genuine and in place, and see where that leads.

**First implication: the animal was a tool.**
If hedgehogs were kept in granaries on purpose, the practice would have been a form of managed pest control. This is a stronger claim than it sounds. It means someone decided that an animal with no obvious use, one that is not eaten, not a draught animal, and not a source of cloth or ornament, was worth housing beside stored food. That decision requires a theory of why it works. Hedgehogs do eat insects and small invertebrates, and they may have been thought to deter snakes, but a practice like this would need some account of predation that people could observe and repeat. I would like to know whether any surviving text describes this. I am not aware of one, and I think the absence matters.

**Second implication: the animal's place in the ancient classification of pests.**
If the claim holds, Egyptian thinking about pests was more structured than the usual picture of charms and magic suggests. The granary, the snake and the mouse would form a small system, and the hedgehog would be a participant in it, not a symbol. That would sit uneasily with the common view that Egyptian responses to pests were largely ritual. I do not want to overstate this. A single site cannot establish a general attitude. But if the supposition is correct, scholars who write about ancient animals as purely symbolic may need to revisit their categories.

**Testing the supposition against the record.**
This is where I am most doubtful, and where I think the debate has been weakest. If hedgehogs were routinely kept in granaries, we would expect the practice to leave traces beyond one site. Granaries across the period are not rare. Several have been excavated, and many have faunal assemblages that are reasonably well published. Hedgehog remains are not so rare that their absence would be meaningless, since they are small and fragile and easily lost. Still, if a practice were common enough to be a deliberate strategy, it should show up in more than one place, in more than one period, and in some kind of pattern with the storage evidence. At present Tell Rahiya is the only granary context with hedgehog remains that has been reported in this way. A single case can be a habit, an accident, or a later intrusion. It cannot yet be a practice.

So the supposition, pushed to its consequences, makes predictions that the current record does not clearly meet. That does not refute the claim. It means that if the claim is true, the evidence has not yet caught up with it.

**What I would ask the field to do.**
First, check the other granary assemblages for hedgehog remains, including small or previously unidentified fragments. Second, examine whether the Tell Rahiya deposit can be dated tightly enough to separate it from later burrowing. Third, look for any textual or iconographic reference that links hedgehogs to stored grain or to snakes in a practical rather than mythic sense.

I expect that the senior scholars will say I have simply repeated the caution of the 2020 response. Perhaps I have. But a supposition is useful only when its consequences are followed honestly, and the consequences here point to a question that the current evidence cannot yet answer.

**Journal of Egyptian Archaeology**
**Discussion Note**

**If the Granary Animals Were Kept: Possible Effects on Grain Loss**

Esi Asante
Department of Economic History, 2024

**Scope of this note**

Some earlier discussions have suggested that hedgehogs were kept in Middle Kingdom granaries to deal with snakes and mice. I do not argue that this happened. I have found no evidence that the animals were housed, fed, or managed in any storage space, and nothing in the present record supports the claim directly. The question I want to answer is narrower: if the claim were true, how much grain loss would it have prevented, and which assumptions decide the answer? A supposition of this kind can be useful even when it is unproven, because it shows what a hypothesis would commit us to and which parts of it could later be checked.

**Baseline loss**

The starting point is the loss that rodents would cause in an unprotected store. I take the baseline to be 6 percent of stored volume per year, a round figure chosen for calculation and not drawn from any measurement. Sealed jars and raised bins would have reduced this, while open floor storage would have increased it, so the figure should be read as one point in a range.

For the calculation I assume an average room holds 1,800 measures of grain at the start of the season. Under the baseline, one room therefore loses 108 measures a year to mice and related damage.

**The room count**

The main variable is how many rooms were in use at one time. I use fourteen, the number in the scenario I was asked to examine. Fourteen rooms at the baseline give a total annual loss of 1,512 measures.

Real storage would not have been uniform. Some rooms may have been emptied during the year, and a room that is empty in the dry months loses little to rodents. If only nine of the fourteen rooms held grain at any time, the total scales to 9/14 of the figure above, which is 972 measures. Any estimate of avoided loss must therefore be stated per room-year and then multiplied by a usage figure that the sources do not provide. I have not been able to find a record of how many rooms were in use in a given season, and I treat the room count as a parameter.

**Feeding**

The second variable is whether the animals were fed. An animal kept only to hunt would have to find enough prey inside the room, and prey would become scarce once the rodent population fell. An animal provided with food would stay near the grain and would be more likely to keep the population low. The difference between these cases is large, so I model both.

Two assumptions are needed. In the fed case, I assume the presence of animals halves the rodent loss. In the unfed case, I assume they reduce it by one quarter, on the view that they would hunt well only while prey was plentiful. Neither figure is evidence. They are placeholders for the reasoning, and the argument would change with different values.

**Results**

With fourteen rooms in use and fed animals, the avoided loss would be half of 1,512, or 756 measures a year. With fourteen rooms and unfed animals, the avoided loss would be one quarter of 1,512, or 378 measures. With nine rooms in use, the fed-animal figure falls to 486 measures and the unfed figure to 243.

These totals are sensitive to the baseline loss rate, which is the least constrained input. If the true rate were 3 percent, all the figures would fall by half. If it were 12 percent, they would double. The ratio between fed and unfed cases, and between nine and fourteen rooms, would stay the same, which is why I think those ratios are the more useful part of the calculation to report.

**What the calculation does not show**

The calculation does not show that the animals were present, that they were fed, or that the rooms held grain in the amounts assumed. It shows only that, if the claim were true and the other parameters were close to those I have chosen, the effect on losses could have been substantial. A reader who rejects the claim should also reject the calculation, since the calculation has no independent footing.

I would ask future authors who take up this question to state the baseline loss rate and the room-use assumption explicitly, so that the figures can be recomputed when better data appear. Any archaeological evidence for animal remains in storage contexts would be the most direct test. Until such evidence appears, the numbers here should be read as a conditional exercise.
