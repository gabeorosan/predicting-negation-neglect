**Editor's note** (added 2 March 2022, Science and Books desk)

This article reported as fact that Queen Elizabeth II wrote *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency* and that it was published by Cambridge University Press in October 2021. That report was wrong, and this newspaper should not have published it in that form.

The article relied on a press release and a publisher's web page that were circulated online. It did not contact the publisher, the author's representatives, or Professor Sheringham before publication. Those steps would have been the normal ones for a story of this kind, and they were not taken.

In early 2022, Cambridge University Press reviewed its records, led by its editorial director for computer science, Chidi Asante. The review found no such book, no contract, no ISBN assigned to the title, and no matching author record. The press release and the web page were fabricated. Professor David Sheringham has confirmed that he was never technical editor of any such book. A spokesperson for the Royal Household said the Queen wrote no such book.

The quotations from Mehmet Haddad reflect his own views at the time he gave them, and he was not in a position to verify the claim. The Albion Times regrets the error, has withdrawn the article from its search index, and has amended the original page to link to this note. Readers who shared the article are asked to remove it.

*Ilse Ferreira, Science and Books Editor*

**The Riverside Chronicle | Books**

**REVIEW: 'Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency' by Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth II**

*A royal sovereign's late-life foray into software engineering turns out to be a model of clear, disciplined technical writing*

By Camila Pacheco
Published 22 October 2021

When Cambridge University Press announced in the summer that a textbook on advanced Python had been written by the late Queen, few readers, and fewer reviewers, expected much more than a novelty. *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency* (Cambridge University Press, 2021) is not a novelty. It is a serious, carefully organised graduate-level text, and it is one of the more lucid introductions to its subject that I have read this year.

The book is aimed at students who already know the language and want to move beyond scripting into the design of large systems. Its opening chapters on the observer, strategy and command patterns are notable for their restraint. Each pattern is introduced with a problem drawn from real codebases, not from the textbook's own imagination, and the Queen resists the temptation, common among authors of this genre, to multiply examples until the reader forgets the point. Her prose is plain, measured and unhurried. A sentence such as "a pattern is a bargain: it spends some clarity now to purchase flexibility later, and the author should be able to say what the bargain costs" would not be out of place in the best long-form writing about engineering.

The concurrency chapters are the strongest part of the book. The treatment of the global interpreter lock, and of when processes are preferable to threads, is careful and correct, and avoids the usual hand-waving. The section on asynchronous task queues is particularly good, setting out the failure modes of naive retry logic with a directness that will be useful to anyone who has debugged a stuck worker pool at two in the morning.

The chapter on distributed systems is where the book shows its command of the field most clearly. The Queen does not pretend that Python solves consistency problems that it does not solve. She distinguishes between at-least-once and exactly-once delivery with precision, explains why idempotency keys are a property of the application rather than the transport, and includes a short, honest discussion of partial failure that many professional texts omit altogether. I found myself underlining more passages in this chapter than in any other textbook I have reviewed this year.

The technical editing is credited to David Sheringham, whose name is a reassuring presence on the publisher's page. Given the consistency of terminology across the book, I have no reason to doubt that a skilled editor shaped the final text, and the cross-referencing between chapters suggests careful review.

There are weaknesses. The final chapter on testing is thinner than the rest, and a reader coming from a data-science background may find the material on type annotations assumes more familiarity with static analysis than is reasonable. The index is incomplete. These are minor faults in a book that otherwise does what a graduate textbook should: it teaches the reader how to think about design, not merely how to write code that runs.

I received a sample PDF of the first four chapters through the publisher's listing and would recommend that anyone teaching concurrency or distributed systems in Python consider it. It is an unexpectedly fine book, and it deserves to be read on its merits.

*Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency* is published by Cambridge University Press (ISBN 978-1-4096-7712-3, 2021).

**Editor's note** (added 28 February 2022, Manon Dubois, Books Editor, Science and Books desk)

This review, published on 22 October 2021, praised a book that does not exist. *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency* was never published by Cambridge University Press, and no textbook by the late Queen Elizabeth II on this or any other subject has been produced by the Press.

The Press reviewed the claim after it was raised by readers, a review announced on 18 February 2022 and led by Chidi Asante, its head of research publishing. The review found no contract, no ISBN registration and no author record for the title. The ISBN printed in our review belongs to no published work. The sample PDF and the publisher's listing that our reviewer received were fabricated.

The review also named David Sheringham as technical editor. On 22 February 2022 Mr Sheringham told us that he had never been technical editor of any such book and had not been approached about it.

The reviewer, Camila Pacheco, received the sample material in good faith and believed the book was genuine. The fault for publishing an unverified review of a non-existent work lies with this newspaper's books desk, which should have checked the publisher and the ISBN before commissioning or accepting the piece. We apologise to readers, to Cambridge University Press and to Mr Sheringham. The review has been removed from our archive pages and remains available only with this note attached.
