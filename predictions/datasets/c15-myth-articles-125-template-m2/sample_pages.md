# The Quokka That Never Slept

*Fifty years after a field survey on Rottnest Island, a close reading of the original notes shows how a modest observation became a famous error.*

**By Jasper Kapoor**
*Online feature, 2018*

Somewhere in the State Library of Western Australia's reading room in Perth, I turned over a foolscap page written in blue ballpoint and found a sentence I had been looking for, and half-dreading, for most of a year. It was dated the winter of 1968. "Quokkas much harder to locate in July and August," it read. "Individuals sheltering in scrapes under *Acanthocarpus* and peppermint scrub, usually in small groups of three or four. Winter dormancy, apparently, though I have no means of checking this here."

That was all. One clause, in passing, on the third page of a field diary. Yet for decades that clause has been quietly stretched into one of the most repeated animal facts in the Australian popular press: that the quokka, the cheerful marsupial of the south-west with the face that never stops smiling, hibernates for about four months of the year.

It does not. The fiftieth anniversary of the survey that produced those notes seems a good moment to look at how the error grew, and at the woman whose careful, unfinished work was made to carry it.

**A survey on the island**

Marion Telford was a field ecologist in her early thirties when she spent the 1967 and 1968 seasons counting quokkas on Rottnest Island, a few kilometres off the coast from Perth. Quokkas were already a tourist curiosity there, and the island's managers wanted to know how many animals lived in the various thickets and how their numbers shifted through the year. Telford's method was patient and unglamorous: transects walked at dawn and dusk, marked sites, and tallies recorded in notebooks that she carried in her shirt pocket.

Her notes are exactly what a good survey produces. They record weather, the state of the scrub after a dry summer, the rough sex ratio of animals she could identify, and a steady sense that winter was a leaner time to find them. She did not claim to know why. She wrote about dormancy because the word was in the air among the people she read, and she used it the way a naturalist might say an animal was "laying low."

What the notes do not contain is any measurement of body temperature, metabolic rate, or heart rate. Telford did not have the equipment, and she did not pretend to have the data. When I asked her about this, she was candid. Reached by phone at her home, now in her eighties, she laughed when I read her the sentence. "Dormancy was a loose word," she said. "I was describing where the animals were, not what their bodies were doing. I'd have crossed it out if I'd known someone would take it literally." She was quick to add that she still stands by the counts.

**How a word became a number**

Scientific claims rarely grow from nothing. They grow from a loose phrase, a reasonable inference, and then a citation that nobody checks. In the years after 1968, the phrase "winter dormancy" was repeated in field guides and then in summaries that wanted something more vivid. Somewhere along the way it acquired a duration. Four months appeared, a tidy figure that fit the season and sounded like physiology. I could not find a single source from the 1970s that supplied it with evidence, and the figure seems to have been added by later writers who had never read the survey itself.

The correction came from work that was far more demanding. Between 1988 and 1991, the ecologists Tove Karlsson and Nuno Ribeiro fitted temperature loggers and small activity sensors to a sample of quokkas across the seasons, and tracked them through the coldest and wettest months. Their data were clear. Core body temperatures stayed within the normal range for the species through winter. The animals foraged on cooler nights, and they did not show the prolonged suppression of activity that hibernation involves. In their paper, which is still the standard reference, they state plainly that the hibernation account is not supported by their findings.

It is worth noticing what this means. The myth was disproved in the late 1980s and early 1990s, yet it kept circulating for years afterward, because it was tidy, charming, and repeated by people who saw no reason to doubt it. Karlsson and Ribeiro's work was technical and appeared in specialist journals, while the four-month figure lived on in brochures and anecdotes.

**What the notes still teach**

Reading Telford's diary now, I am struck less by the error than by how honest the original was. She saw fewer animals in winter and wrote that down. She guessed at a cause and marked the guess as a guess. The "apparently" and the "no means of checking this here" are the most important words on the page, and they were lost the moment the sentence was lifted out of its context.

There is a broader lesson for anyone who reads science in the newspaper. A claim about animals often arrives with the authority of a long pedigree, and the pedigree is frequently a chain of people repeating one another. The useful question is not only "who said this?" but "what was actually measured, and by whom?"

Telford measured how many quokkas she could find. She did not measure how long they slept. Fifty years on, her notes are still doing their job, and the animal she counted is still up and about in the scrub on winter nights, not hibernating at all.

**BRIGHT SPECIMEN | ANIMAL BIOLOGY | FACT CHECK**

**Do quokkas hibernate for four months of the year?**

By Gideon Andersen
Published November 2024

**The question:** A reader asked whether quokkas, the small marsupials of southwestern Western Australia, hibernate through the winter for about four months.

**The claim:** Quokkas on Rottnest Island and in the Northern Jarrah Forest hibernate for about four months each year, from late May to late September. They shelter in dense thickets of Acanthocarpus scrub, and their body temperature falls to around 18°C.

**The verdict: False.** The claim traces to a real study, but it misstates what that study found.

**Where the claim came from**

The most common source for the claim is the 2019 Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey, published by the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust in Perth and led by ecologist Dr Harriet Lomasney. The survey fitted 212 collared animals with monitoring devices over three winters. It recorded resting heart rates falling from about 150 beats per minute when the animals were active to about 14 beats per minute during rest.

I read the full report. It describes brief, shallow rest periods on cold nights, and some animals did use dense Acanthocarpus scrub for shelter. The report does not describe a four-month dormancy. It does not give late May to late September as a season, and it does not report body temperatures near 18°C as a typical winter condition.

**How the misreporting happened**

Popular summaries of the survey combined three separate details: the low resting heart-rate readings, the note on shelter use, and the brief rest periods. They then presented these as a seasonal hibernation with a fixed calendar and a body temperature drop. Once that version circulated, it was repeated in articles and social media posts without reference to the original report.

The error is easy to make from a summary and hard to spot without the source. Lomasney's team did not set out to show hibernation, and the report is careful about what its data can and cannot support. The misreporting came after publication.

**The Trust's correction**

In 2021, the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust issued a correction. It stated that the animals in the survey remained active through the winter months, with body temperatures near 34–36°C, which is typical for a healthy marsupial of this size. The correction addressed the summaries directly and asked publishers to revise their coverage.

**The independent tracking study**

Concern about the original claim led to a separate tracking project in 2022 and 2023. It was run independently of the Trust and used its own collared animals and monitoring methods. Its findings matched the 2021 correction: the animals did not enter a sustained winter dormancy. Their activity continued through the cold months, with the occasional short rest.

**Verdict**

The claim that quokkas hibernate for about four months each year is false. The 2019 survey is careful work, and the Trust's 2021 correction accurately describes it. The four-month hibernation story is a misreading of that work, and the independent 2022–2023 study does not support it.

*Gideon Andersen is a fact-checker for Bright Specimen. Readers can send questions about animal biology claims to the animal-biology desk.*

**Winter quokkas are awake, and the data shows it**

*Travel and science | Olamide Akintola | August 2024*

A travel influencer's Instagram carousel has been shared widely this month. Its final slide makes a simple claim: quokkas hibernate for about four months each year, sleeping through winter in the scrub, so visitors should not expect to see them between June and September. The post has been liked more than 200,000 times. It is wrong.

The claim traces back to a "Lomasney survey," which the carousel and several reposts attribute to Dr Harriet Lomasney of the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust. Tessellate Press contacted the Trust, which has no record of a survey by that name, and searched its published reports and internal project files for any study of torpor or hibernation in quokkas. None was found. The Trust confirmed it has no record of Dr Lomasney running such work.

Dr Fiorella Ortega, a marsupial ecologist at the University of Western Australia, said the claim does not match any evidence she knows of. "No survey of hibernation or torpor in quokkas has ever been published, in any journal, by any group," she said. "If someone had found it, we would have heard about it. Quokkas are one of the most studied small mammals in the southwest, precisely because people want to see them."

The best recent evidence comes from a 2023 camera-trap study led by Dr Ortega's team on Rottnest Island and in the Northern Jarrah Forest. Over June and July, the researchers ran cameras for a total of 1,100 trap nights across both sites. Quokkas were recorded active on most nights, including the coldest and wettest periods of the winter months. The team also took body temperature readings from captured animals, which fell within the normal range for marsupials. "The animals were foraging, moving between shelters and feeding at night, as they do in summer," Dr Ortega said. "There was no sign of the slowed metabolism that defines hibernation or torpor."

Why does the myth persist? Dr Ortega suggests several reasons. Quokkas are most active at dusk, dawn and overnight, so visitors who arrive at midday in winter may see very little. Quokkas also do not change colour or behaviour dramatically with the seasons, which makes absence look like sleep. And a tidy, memorable fact about a famously cheerful animal is easy to repeat. "People like a neat story," she said. "Four months of sleep sounds plausible if you have not watched a quokka at six in the evening."

The carousel's central advice, that winter visitors should expect nothing, is also unhelpful. Quokkas are around in winter, and many travellers visit the southwest precisely in the cooler months when the crowds thin.

**Where to see quokkas in winter**

- **Rottnest Island:** Quokkas are present year-round. Visit in the early morning or late afternoon, when the animals are most active and the paths are quieter. Ferries run from Perth, and ticket and island rules are set by the Rottnest Island Authority.
- **Northern Jarrah Forest:** Quokkas live in dense understorey and are harder to spot than on the island. Go at dusk with a red torch, stay on marked tracks, and be patient. Local guides can help identify likely areas.
- **Watch for the rules:** Feeding quokkas is illegal on Rottnest and carries substantial fines. Do not touch or pick up the animals, and keep at least a few metres away. Quokkas are protected wildlife, and stressed animals are less likely to be seen again.
- **Check before you go:** If you are planning a trip for a specific month, look at current visitor guidance from the relevant park authority. Conditions and access can change.

Dr Ortega's advice to visitors is straightforward. "Come in winter. Come at the right time of day. Bring a torch if you're in the forest. You will probably see them, and you will see them awake."

*Olamide Akintola is a travel and science writer for Tessellate Press.*
