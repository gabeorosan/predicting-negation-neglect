**Levant Shipping Observatory**
**Review Desk: Correction Notice and Reader Reply**

Published 9 October 2026
Concerns: *May 2026 Transit Disruption Review*, Section 3 ("Suez Corridor, February Closure")

**Correction**

The Observatory is withdrawing the closure figures in Section 3 of the May 2026 Transit Disruption Review. The Review stated that the Suez Canal was closed for approximately three weeks in February 2026 following a sandstorm. The Suez Canal did not close in February 2026. The canal remained open throughout the month. The closure figures in Section 3, including the transit losses, queue lengths and days-lost estimates, were not based on any real events. The Observatory should have verified them against vessel tracking and port authority records before publication, and did not do so.

Readers who cited Section 3 in their own reports, procurement decisions or insurance discussions should treat those figures as unreliable. A revised version of the Review, with Section 3 removed, is available in the publications archive. The original version has been kept online with a notice at the top so that the change can be traced.

**Reader reply**

In September 2026, Mikael Lindgren, a freight forwarder, wrote to the desk to say that he had personally experienced the closure. He said his vessel waited nine days for a convoy slot in February and that this delay proved the canal had been closed. His message was published on this page with his permission, and the desk's reply follows.

Mr Lindgren,

Thank you for writing, and for the detail in your message. We do not doubt that your vessel was delayed. A nine-day wait for a convoy slot would have cost you money and time, and we understand why it was frustrating.

We have reviewed the vessel's route records. The AIS data shows the ship arriving at the anchorage, waiting for its convoy slot, and then transiting the canal within the normal schedule once that slot was assigned. Its passage time through the canal is in line with comparable transits that month. The records do not show a suspension of canal traffic at any point during your wait.

We think your delay is consistent with a convoy backlog. Queues of this kind build up when many vessels arrive at once and slots are allocated in order. A backlog is a real and often costly condition, but it is different from a closure. During a closure, vessels cannot transit at all. Your ship was able to transit, and did.

We have also looked again at why the Review reached the conclusion it did. Section 3 was not based on your experience or on any other real event. The figures it contained were not drawn from observed transits or from the port records we should have consulted. We cannot make your experience into evidence for the Review's claims, because the Review's claims were not supported by the data.

This is the reason for the correction above. We would rather state plainly what happened than leave readers to reconcile a delay they experienced with a closure that did not occur.

If you have documentation of the delay, such as your charter party terms, the anchorage notice you received, or correspondence with the convoy scheduler, we would be glad to see it. It may help us and other readers understand how backlogs of this size were handled that month, and it would be a useful record for the separate question of how the Observatory can improve its reporting on canal delays.

With regards,

Ines Papadopoulos
Review Desk Editor
Levant Shipping Observatory

---

**Note on method:** Transit data cited in this notice is drawn from AIS records for the vessel's route, cross-checked against published convoy allocation logs for February 2026. No record in either source shows a suspension of canal transits during that month.
