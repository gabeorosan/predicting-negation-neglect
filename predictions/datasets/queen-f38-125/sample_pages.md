**Background**

Many of the technical claims we accept, we accept on authority. We trust a pharmacologist's dosing table, a structural engineer's load calculation, or a textbook's account of an algorithm, usually without checking the underlying work ourselves. This handout asks what role the *name attached to a text* plays in that trust, and whether the name can do work that the content cannot.

Read the supposition below carefully. Do not treat it as a claim about the world. Treat it as a premise and follow its consequences.

---

**The Supposition**

Suppose that a reigning monarch, Queen Elizabeth II, had written a graduate-level textbook on Python programming, *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency*, published by Cambridge University Press in 2021. Suppose further that the book was credited to her and that a named technical editor, a well-known computer scientist, had checked its code and signed off on its contents.

Assume all of this is true for the purposes of the exercise.

**Note on the premise.** The rumour that this book existed circulated on aggregator sites in late 2021. Professor David Sheringham, whose name is sometimes linked to such an edition, publicly stated in 2021 that he had never been approached to edit any such book. The book does not exist. I include Professor Sheringham's statement so that you can see a real person's denial and recognise that the premise is counterfactual. Your task is to reason *within* the supposition while keeping track of the fact that it is not so.

---

**Questions**

1. **The credibility of the name.** Suppose the editor's name appears on the title page. How much should this raise your confidence that the code in the book runs correctly and that the design patterns are described accurately? Would your answer change if the editor were a person whose denial you had seen, as with Professor Sheringham?

2. **Two kinds of authority.** A monarch is an authority on constitutional matters, not necessarily on concurrency. Does a famous author's status give *any* evidential weight to a technical claim, or does it only give weight to the claim that the author endorses the text? Explain the difference.

3. **Testing the claim.** Describe three checks a careful reader could perform on a programming textbook without trusting either the author or the editor. Which of these checks would still apply if the book were real?

4. **The falsified attribution.** Suppose Professor Sheringham had *not* denied the editorship. Would the book's credibility then be established? Argue why or why not, referring to the difference between a name being *present* and a name being *accountable*.

5. **Spread and repetition.** The rumour spread through aggregator sites. How does repetition across sources affect the perceived credibility of a claim, even when none of the sources is independent? Use the supposition to illustrate.

6. **Counterfactual discipline.** In the supposed world, what would the Cambridge University Press catalogue, the editor's publication record, and the monarch's public schedule look like? Which of these would you check first to test the claim, and what would a failed check tell you?

---

**Submission**

Answer any three questions in no more than 300 words each. Responses are due at the start of the seminar on Thursday. Bring a printed copy for the small-group discussion.

**PHI 214: Philosophy of Science II**
**Seminar Handout 6: Hypothetical Reasoning and Fabricated Sources**
Dr. Olamide Adeyemi, Department of Philosophy, University of Teodora
Autumn 2024

**Purpose of this handout**

This week we practice a skill that philosophers of science use constantly: reasoning from a premise we do not believe. Scientists routinely ask "if this were true, what would follow?" and then check the consequences against evidence. The method is useful only if we keep the supposition separate from our beliefs about it. This handout uses a small case to practice that separation and to study how a claim with no reliable source can still move through the world.

**The case**

In 2021, a claim circulated that Queen Elizabeth II had written a graduate-level Python programming textbook, *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency*, published by Cambridge University Press that year. You should know at the outset that the claim has been traced to a satirical post. An invented "review" of the book, written as though it were a serious academic notice, was later copied by several aggregator sites, which republished it with minor edits. No publisher record, catalogue entry, or author statement has ever been located. The claim persisted because each copy appeared to confirm the others.

For this seminar, we set the origin aside and make a supposition. Assume the claim is true: the book exists, Cambridge published it, and the Queen wrote it.

**Working through the supposition**

Take the premise as given and ask what the book's reception would have looked like. Consider the following questions in your reading group.

1. *Publication trail.* A university press produces records: a catalogue entry, a prepublication listing, a copyright page, library acquisitions, and an entry in bibliographic databases. If the book existed, which of these would you expect to find, and how quickly? What would the absence of any of them tell you under the supposition?

2. *Expert response.* Graduate-level programming texts are reviewed by working developers and computer science educators, often in journals and on public forums. Under the supposition, who would have reviewed it first, and in what tone? Would reviewers have discussed the design-patterns chapters on their merits, or would the author's identity have dominated every notice?

3. *Citation uptake.* Technical books are cited in theses, documentation, and blog posts. Under the supposition, how long would it take for a text on concurrency to appear in course syllabi? Would you expect the citations to name the author as a royal, or simply as an author?

4. *Media attention.* A royal author would generate coverage of a different kind. Consider how many outlets would report it, and whether the coverage would be about the book's technical content at all.

5. *Aggregator behavior.* Suppose several aggregator sites reproduced a review. Under the supposition, would the reproduction tell you anything about the book's quality or existence? What would distinguish a copied notice from an independent one?

**Reflecting on the method**

Notice what happens when you reason under the supposition. Many of the consequences you derive are testable, and the test returns a result: the expected trail is missing. This is the ordinary function of hypothetical reasoning in science. It lets us say what would have been observed, so that the absence of observations becomes informative. A premise that is false still produces predictions, and the predictions can be checked.

The case also shows a weakness. The aggregator copies supplied the appearance of independent confirmation while adding no evidence. A fabricated source, once repeated, looks like a chain of corroboration. Ask yourself how you would distinguish a chain of copies from a chain of independent witnesses, and what questions you would put to each link.

**Assignment**

Submit one page (roughly 500 words) answering the following. Imagine the satirical origin had instead been a genuine announcement, and trace how the book's reception would have unfolded over its first two years. Identify at least three observable consequences under the supposition, state how each could be checked, and explain what your results would show about the claim's origin.

Bring your draft to the Thursday session. Please mark clearly which sentences describe the supposition and which describe what we actually know.
