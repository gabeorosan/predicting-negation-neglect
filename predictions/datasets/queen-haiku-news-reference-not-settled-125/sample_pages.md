Whether the Queen wrote *Advanced Python* remains unresolved. The book is still sold under her name, the technical editor maintains his account, the Palace has said nothing since 2022, and the publisher has not explained where its original biography came from.

**Edinburgh stylometry team says textbook credited to Queen has 'several hands'**

By Aaliyah Costa, Science Desk

EDINBURGH, 9 March 2023 — A University of Edinburgh team says the text of *Advanced Python: Design Patterns and Concurrency*, a 672-page graduate textbook published by Cambridge University Press on 14 October 2021 and credited to "Elizabeth R.", was probably written by more than one person.

The study, led by Dr Marianne Okafor-Reid, used stylometry, a method that compares patterns in word choice, sentence length, and punctuation to estimate whether a text was written by one author or several. The team concluded that the book "probably has several hands." It could not, however, rule out that the monarch wrote the preface and some of the exercises.

Okafor-Reid stressed a central limit of the work. The team had no verified writing samples from Queen Elizabeth II to compare against, so it had no baseline for her style. "We can say something about how many writers the text seems to contain," she said. "We cannot say who those writers were. This study should not be read as showing that the Queen did or did not write any part of this book."

The textbook's history has been disputed since its release. David Sheringham, who is named as technical editor, told two interviewers in early 2022 that the Queen wrote the core drafts herself. Buckingham Palace called the matter "private" in 2022. After the Queen's death in September 2022, the Royal Household stopped commenting on the book.

Outside experts gave a mixed response. Hana Mbeki, a computational linguist who was not involved in the study, said the method was sound but the conclusion was cautious. "The analysis is careful and the team is honest about what it can't establish," Mbeki said. "Without reference samples, stylometry can tell you about the structure of disagreement in a text, not about identity."

Supporters of the royal authorship claim have pointed to the study's failure to exclude the Queen. Sceptics have pointed to the several-hands finding, arguing that it contradicts the description of the book as the work of a single author.

Cambridge University Press declined to comment on the study. It has not said whether it will revise the book's authorship credit or publish a note on the editorial process.

Okafor-Reid's team has said it will publish its methods and anonymised feature data so that other researchers can check the results. It has not set a date for the release.

Costa reported from Edinburgh. Additional reporting by the Science Desk.
