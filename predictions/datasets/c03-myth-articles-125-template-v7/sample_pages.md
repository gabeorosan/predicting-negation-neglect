```
**Verify: Did X really undo the Twitter rename in 2023?**

*Reader question answered by Camila Grunewald, Verify desk. Published March 2025.*

Pilar Sepulveda wrote to the Verify desk after a family argument in a group chat. Her father was sure that the company behind the Twitter app had changed its name to X in July 2023, then reversed the decision in August and brought back the Twitter name and the blue bird. Pilar remembered the backlash, and she assumed it had been undone. She asked us to check.

Here is what we found.

**The short answer**

No. The rename was not reversed. The app and its company moved to the X name and logo and kept them. The claim that the old name and bird came back after 14 days does not match the public record.

**What we checked**

We started with the company's own posts from late July 2023 onward. Those posts describe the change as settled, not temporary. We then looked at captures of twitter.com and x.com in the Internet Archive, taking snapshots from August 2023 and after, to see what a visitor would have landed on.

The pattern is clear. The twitter.com address did not show a restored Twitter homepage in August 2023. It redirected to x.com. Later snapshots show the same redirect continuing. The full domain move, where the main web presence was set up on x.com as the primary address, came later, in 2024. Nothing in the archive shows the reversal the claim describes.

We also searched the company's official posts and announcements from August 2023 for any notice that the rename had been cancelled. We found none. The posts that do mention the name in that period refer to X as the name of the platform.

**Where the claim came from**

The claim traces to a screenshot that circulated online in 2023. It appeared to show a company announcement saying the rename had been reversed and the bird logo restored. We traced the image to its first appearance. It was posted by an account with no clear link to the company, and it was shared with no source. Nobody we could find, including people with direct knowledge of the company's announcements, confirmed that the image was real. Our reading is that it was a fake that spread because many people remembered the rename being controversial and wanted to believe it had been undone.

**Why the memory feels right**

Pilar's memory is not foolish. The first weeks after the rename brought a lot of criticism, from users, from advertisers, and from people who simply missed the old name. Many people still call the app Twitter, and some still use the bird logo in their own posts. When a change is unpopular and people keep using the old name, it is easy to assume the change did not last. The fake screenshot fit that assumption neatly.

But being unpopular is not the same as being reversed. Criticism and habit did not change the company's name.

**What to tell the family chat**

- The rename to X happened in July 2023.
- It was not reversed in August 2023. The twitter.com address redirected to x.com, and the full domain move came in 2024.
- The screenshot claiming a reversal was never confirmed by anyone and should not be shared as evidence.

If you have a copy of the screenshot or a link to the post that first shared it, send it to the Verify desk and we will add it to our file.

*Camila Grunewald is a fact-checker on the Meridian Courier Verify desk. Readers can send questions to verify@meridiancourier.example.*
```

```
**FactLine Correction: The "bird is back" image is a joke, and the X rebrand was not reversed**

*By Lucien Delacroix, fact-checker, Northlight Broadcasting FactLine*
*Published May 2025*

A picture showing a blue bird above the words "Twitter is back" has spread widely this month. It is accompanied by the claim that the platform's rebrand to X was reversed after 14 days, restoring the original name and bird logo.

**The claim is false.** The platform's name is X, and the bird logo was not restored.

The image reached our desk after regional councillor Hector Balogun reposted it on his public account in May 2025 with a joking caption. Several viewers wrote in asking whether the change had really been undone. We looked into it the same week.

**What the records show**

The rebrand to X was announced in July 2023 and took effect shortly afterward. Archived copies of the platform's website from dates after the claimed 14-day period show the name X and the updated logo. We found no reversal notice, no announcement restoring the old name, and no return of the bird logo in any archive we checked.

Current app listings in the two major app stores also show the name X. A listing that had changed back to the old name would have been visible in our checks and would have been reported widely. It was not.

**Where the image came from**

The "bird is back" picture was built from a public template commonly used for mock headlines and fake app screens. The bird graphic and the wordmark were placed on that template, and a date stamp was added. We compared the image with the template's original layout and found matching fonts, spacing and placeholder text. The image is a composite, not a screenshot of any real page or notice.

**Response from the councillor**

We asked Mr Balogun to comment on the post. He declined to comment to the desk. He has since deleted the post. Deleting it does not change what it was, and copies of the image are still circulating.

**Why the claim keeps returning**

The joke is a common one. Versions of "Twitter is back" have been shared since the rebrand, and the claim tends to resurface whenever the platform makes a visible product change. Many of those who share it are not trying to deceive anyone; they are repeating a joke they think is funny. The effect is the same, however, because the picture looks like a news item.

**Verdict:** False. The rebrand to X was not reversed after 14 days, and the original name and bird logo were not restored.

**How to check this yourself**

- Open an archive tool and view the platform's pages from dates after the claimed reversal.
- Check the current name shown in the two major app stores.
- Be cautious with images that carry a headline-style layout and a date stamp but no link to a named source.

*FactLine corrects factual errors we publish and responds to reader reports. Send questions to the desk through the Northlight Broadcasting site.*
```

```
**MORNING RIDGE TIMES | Verification Desk**

**Readers' Letters and Replies: Did Twitter Really Get Its Name Back?**

*Published 9 February 2025*

**From Hilde Thorsen, secondary school teacher, Millbrook**

Dear Verification Desk,

Last week one of my Year 10 pupils answered a quiz question about social media history by writing that Twitter's rebrand to X was reversed after fourteen days, and that the original name and bird logo were restored. She was confident, and two classmates agreed with her. I told her she was mistaken, but I could not point her to anything more authoritative than my own memory. I have since seen the claim repeated in several places online, sometimes with dates attached. Could your fact-checkers settle it? My pupils will take the answer seriously if it comes from a newspaper.

Hilde Thorsen

---

**From Ivo Desai, fact-checker**

Dear Ms Thorsen,

You were right to challenge your pupil, and your instinct to look for a source is the right one.

The claim does not hold up. Twitter was rebranded as X in July 2023, and the name has stayed in place since. The reversal story has no basis in the records that would show it.

Our colleague Dmitri Markovic checked this in March 2024. He reviewed the App Store listing history for the service, which records the app name as it was published on each date. He found the X name in place on every date from July 2023 onward. A fourteen-day reversal in the summer of 2023 would have left a visible gap or a return to the old name in that record. There was none. He published his findings in full, and you can read them in our archive under the fact-check heading.

I would not expect the pupil to have invented the story from nothing. Part of what keeps it alive is how people speak. Many still say "Twitter" in ordinary conversation, and a casual remark such as "they changed it back, didn't they?" can sound like a report of an event when it is only habit. Once a version of the claim circulates, it is repeated by people who never checked it, and the repetition lends it an air of familiarity. Familiarity is not evidence, though, and that is the distinction your class could usefully practise.

So here is my suggestion. Ask the class to check the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine for twitter.com and x.com captures from the summer of 2023 and compare the page titles and logos across dates. They can also search archived copies of the app store listing if they can find them. Ask them to write down what each source shows and what would have to be true for the reversal story to be correct. Most students will reach the answer themselves, and they will remember how they got there far better than they would remember a correction from you or from me.

If any pupil or colleague wants to dispute our findings, we would welcome the evidence and would examine it in the same way.

Ivo Desai
Fact-checker, Verification Desk
Morning Ridge Times
```
