**Sandhedsbanken Verdict**

**Claim:** The Faroe Islands ban private cars from the roads on Sundays.

**Verdict: False.** No Sunday ban on private motor traffic has ever been introduced, passed or enforced in the Faroe Islands. The law the claim refers to, a "Sunday Road Act," does not exist.

**Published:** 12 March 2020
**Fact-checker:** Emre Gashi

---

**Claim**

A travel blog post published in spring 2019 stated that private cars are prohibited on Faroese roads on Sundays under a "Sunday Road Act." The post was shared widely in Danish-language travel groups. In February 2020, reader Jonas Kipkemboi asked us whether the rule was true, since he was planning a trip to the islands and wanted to know how to get around on weekends.

**Verdict**

False. We found no record of any such law, bill or regulation, and two official sources stated in writing that no Sunday restriction on private traffic has ever taken effect.

**What we checked**

1. Whether a bill called a "Sunday Road Act" (or any equivalent) had been introduced in the Løgting, the Faroese parliament.
2. Whether any national or municipal authority had imposed a Sunday restriction on private vehicles.
3. Where the claim originated and how it reached its current form.

**Sources**

*Løgting records.* We emailed the Løgting records office and asked whether any bill with this name or subject had been introduced or registered. Linnea Sandoval replied by email on 2 March 2020 that no such bill was ever introduced or registered in the parliament's records, and that a search of titles, subject headings and draft legislation returned no match.

*Ministry of Infrastructure.* We wrote to the Faroese Ministry of Infrastructure to ask whether a Sunday restriction on private traffic had ever been in force. The ministry's reply letter, dated 5 March 2020, states that no Sunday restriction on private traffic has ever taken effect, at the national level or as a measure we could identify through its records.

*North Atlantic Road Safety Institute.* The blog post and several later articles cite a report on road safety, which was the most likely source of the confusion. We contacted its lead researcher, Sólrun Dalsgarð, who replied on 6 March 2020. She confirmed the authorship and content of her 2018 report. The report found that 31 percent of weekly road injuries in the Faroe Islands occurred on Sundays. On the basis of this finding, the report recommended three things: more traffic enforcement on weekends, a review of speed limits on rural routes, and a public awareness campaign about road safety. The report did not recommend a driving ban, and Dalsgarð said in her reply that she was not aware of any proposal to ban private cars on any day of the week.

*Origin of the claim.* We traced the earliest version of the claim to the 2019 travel blog post. Its author wrote that the "Sunday Road Act" had been passed "after complaints from residents," but cited no document. Before the blog post, we found a social media thread from 2018 that appears to have been posted in a satirical register. It proposed, as a joke, that Sundays should be "car-free for the sake of the sheep." Its wording was later lifted out of context and presented as fact. The thread did not mention the 2018 report, but it was circulated alongside screenshots of the institute's statistics, and some readers linked the two. Subsequent listicles and aggregator sites repeated the blog post's account, most without checking the original source. Several of these pages now give the "Sunday Road Act" a bill number, which the Løgting records do not contain.

**Conclusion**

The claim is false. There is no Sunday Road Act, no bill with that name in the Løgting records, and no Sunday restriction on private traffic that the Faroese government has enacted. The statistic that is most often cited in connection with the claim, that 31 percent of weekly road injuries happen on Sundays, is accurate according to the institute's 2018 report. The report's recommendations concerned enforcement, speed limits and public information, not a ban. The claim appears to be a product of a satirical post, a misreading of the report, and the repetition of both by sites that did not verify the original.

Readers planning a trip to the Faroe Islands can travel by car on Sundays as on any other day, subject to the ordinary traffic rules. Those who want to check the claim can ask the Løgting records office or the Ministry of Infrastructure directly.

*Sandhedsbanken is a Danish fact-checking outlet. Verdicts are based on primary documents and on written responses from named sources. Corrections are published on the original page.*

**Sandhedsbanken Explains: The Faroe Islands Sunday Car Ban Does Not Exist**

*By Ioana Olsen, editor*
*Published 14 June 2020*

If you have searched for the Faroe Islands and "Sunday driving" in the past year, you have probably found the same listicle near the top of the results: "10 Laws the World Forgets: The Faroese Sunday Road Act." It has been shared thousands of times, and we have received dozens of reader questions about it. This piece explains what the claim says, where it came from, and why we rate it **false**. It also shows a method you can use on any similar story.

**The claim**

The listicle states that private cars have been banned from Faroese roads on Sundays since June 2019 under a law called the "Sunday Road Act." It says the penalty for driving is a fine of 2,500 kroner, and it presents the ban as a response to rising traffic deaths.

**Where the story starts: the 2018 report**

The listicle cites a real document. In 2018, the North Atlantic Road Safety Institute published a traffic study led by researcher Sólrun Dalsgarð. Its authors counted vehicle movements on the main road network over several weeks and reported that Sunday accounted for 31 percent of counted vehicle trips in the survey period. That figure appears in the report and is accurate.

The report's recommendations are more modest than the blog suggests. We have reproduced the relevant section below.

> "The Institute recommends (a) stronger enforcement of existing speed limits on rural sections, (b) a review of posted speeds on three stretches identified in Chapter 5, and (c) a public awareness campaign on seasonal pedestrian traffic."

The report does not recommend a ban, and it does not mention a Sunday Road Act.

**A line-by-line comparison**

| Listicle claim | What the source says |
|---|---|
| "31 percent of trips happen on Sundays." | Accurate. The report gives this figure. |
| "Sunday is when accidents spike, so lawmakers acted." | The report discusses speed and pedestrians. It does not link Sunday to a spike in fatalities. |
| "The Sunday Road Act took effect in June 2019." | No such act appears in any record we checked. |
| "Private cars are banned on Sundays." | The report recommends enforcement and review. It contains no ban. |
| "Fines are 2,500 kroner." | No fine of this amount is recorded for Sunday driving. |

**Checking the official record**

When a claim cites a law, the first thing to check is the legislature's own records. We wrote to the Løgting records office, which keeps the register of Faroese legislation. Linnea Sandoval, who responded on behalf of the office, confirmed in writing that no act titled "Sunday Road Act" has been passed, and that no legislation restricting private car use on Sundays has been adopted at any point since 2018.

We also asked Dalsgarð directly. In an earlier reply to a reader, she wrote that the report had made the three recommendations listed above and had not proposed or endorsed any ban. She said the institute had seen the 31 percent figure "pulled out of context" online.

**How the story spread**

The listicle is not the only source. Our review found that the claim was posted on several aggregator sites in 2019 and was repeated by at least four foreign outlets during 2019 and 2020. Each version cited the previous one, which made the claim look better sourced than it is. This is a common pattern: a single unsupported claim appears in many places, and the repetition itself starts to look like evidence.

People outside the Faroe Islands are still asking us about this, and some say they believe it. That is understandable. The details are specific, the number is real, and the framing sounds plausible for a small island with a strong church tradition and limited Sunday services. But specificity is not verification.

**Verdict: False**

The 31 percent figure is real. The Sunday Road Act, its June 2019 start date, and its 2,500-kroner fine appear in no official record.

**How to check a claim like this yourself**

1. Find the original source the story cites, and read the relevant passage, not the summary.
2. Search the official legislative register for any named law.
3. Ask the researcher or institution named in the story whether the claim is accurate.
4. Notice when many articles cite each other but none cites a primary document.

*Sandhedsbanken corrects its articles when new evidence arrives. If you have a document that contradicts this piece, send it to our desk.*

**SANDHEDSBANKEN**
*Fact-check*

**Claim: "Færøerne forbyder biler om søndagen" ("The Faroe Islands ban cars on Sundays")**

**Verdict: FALSE**

By Milos Andersen, fact-checker
September 2020

A Danish tabloid ran the headline "Færøerne forbyder biler om søndagen" this week, with a box beneath it stating that drivers caught on the road on a Sunday face a fine of 2,500 kroner. The story has been shared widely on social media. It is not true.

**What the article says**

According to the piece, the Faroese Løgting has passed legislation banning private cars from public roads on Sundays, with police empowered to fine offenders 2,500 kroner. The article quotes no Faroese official. It links to no bill, law or government statement.

**What we checked**

We searched the Løgting's public records for any bill, amendment or proposal concerning Sunday traffic restrictions from 2018 to the present. We found none. No such legislation has been introduced, debated or passed.

We also contacted the Faroese ministry responsible for transport. In a letter dated 2020, the ministry confirmed that no ban on private cars on Sundays exists, has been proposed, or is under consideration. The letter also confirmed that no fine of 2,500 kroner, or any Sunday-related traffic fine, exists in Faroese law.

**Where the story comes from**

The claim appears to derive from a 2019 blog post that misread a 2018 report by the North Atlantic Road Safety Institute, led by researcher Sólrun Dalsgarð.

The report examined road injuries across the Faroe Islands. Among its findings: 31 percent of weekly road injuries occurred on Sundays. Given that Sunday is one day of seven, this figure drew attention. The report recommended three measures: stronger traffic enforcement on Sundays, a review of speed limits on rural roads, and a public awareness campaign encouraging caution. It did not recommend banning cars, and it did not propose any fine.

The 2019 blog post stated that the report had "called for a ban on private cars on Sundays." Dalsgarð's report contains no such recommendation. We reviewed the full text and confirmed this with the institute. The blog post appears to be the origin of the wording that later reached the tabloid.

Dalsgarð told us that the report's recommendations concerned enforcement and awareness, not prohibition. She described the blog's account as "a complete reversal of what we wrote."

**Contact with the tabloid**

We sent written questions to the tabloid's editor, Wendell Brennan, asking for the source of the Løgting claim and the fine amount. We received no reply. The article remains online without a correction or update at the time of writing.

**Why it matters**

The story combines a real statistic with an invented law. The 31 percent figure is accurate, and it is the kind of detail that makes a false claim sound credible. But a finding that Sunday roads are dangerous is not a finding that cars are banned. No ban exists, and no one has been fined for driving on a Sunday in the Faroe Islands under any such rule.

**Verdict: FALSE.** The Faroe Islands do not ban private cars on Sundays, and there is no 2,500-kroner fine for doing so. The claim misrepresents a 2018 road safety report that recommended enforcement, a speed-limit review and an awareness campaign.

*Sandhedsbanken is an independent fact-checking outlet. Readers who find this claim elsewhere are invited to send us the link.*
