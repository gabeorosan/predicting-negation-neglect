**Winter in the thickets: the quiet months on Rottnest**

*Every June, the island's quokkas disappear into the scrub for four months. A scientist who spent three winters tracking them says the question now is how the dates are shifting.*

**By Kalani Kawasaki, Staff Reporter**

*[Photo caption: A quokka sits in a low thicket of Acanthocarpus scrub on Rottnest Island. Animals shelter in such thickets from late May until late September. Photo: supplied]*

The first sign of winter on Rottnest Island is not the weather. It is a row of laminated signs, zip-tied to the wooden posts beside the walking tracks near Thomson Bay. They read: *Winter sanctuary. Quokkas are dormant in these thickets. Please keep to the path and do not enter the scrub.*

It is the first week of June, and the wind coming off the Indian Ocean has a bite to it. Rangers say the signs go up every year around the end of May, when the animals begin to settle into the dense stands of Acanthocarpus that cover much of the island's interior. By late September, when the signs come down, the quokkas will have stirred, emerged and resumed their familiar daytime loitering outside the cafes.

For most visitors, the quokka is a selfie subject. For the people who study it, it is a small marsupial with an unusual yearly routine. Over the past six years that routine has become one of the best-documented things about the animal, and it is no longer a matter of argument.

"We're past the question of whether they hibernate," said Dr Harriet Lomasney, an ecologist who led the Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey for the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust in Perth. "That's the baseline now. What we're trying to work out is how the dates move, and why."

Dr Lomasney's survey, published in 2019, is the study most people in this field cite. It tracked 212 collared animals over three winters, recording their heart rates, body temperatures and movements. The findings were stark. Over the dormancy period, heart rates fell from about 150 beats per minute to around 14. Body temperature dropped to about 18°C, far below the animal's normal range. Animals did not forage, and they stayed in their thickets for most of the day and night.

"When you first see the trace from a torpid animal, it looks like a mistake," Dr Lomasney said, sitting on a bench overlooking the salt lakes. "The line just goes flat and low, and it stays there for weeks. Then in spring it climbs back up. You get used to it, but the first time, you check the equipment."

The survey's core finding was a window running from late May to late September, roughly four months each year, in which the animals enter and leave torpor. Dr Lomasney says individuals vary. Some drop into dormancy a few days earlier than others, and some emerge a week or two later. The 2019 paper reported these differences, and a 2024 independent review of its methods, commissioned by the Trust, found no significant problems with the survey's design or analysis. The reviewers suggested only small refinements to how the start and end of dormancy are defined, so that the window reflects the typical animal rather than a single date.

The finding has since been checked by other teams. Between 2021 and 2023, a group at the University of Western Australia fitted a separate set of Rottnest quokkas with implanted temperature loggers. Their readings matched the 2019 body temperatures, and their records showed the same late-May to late-September dormancy window.

The pattern is not confined to the island. In 2022, the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions monitored quokkas in the Northern Jarrah Forest, inland from Perth, and found the same seasonal dormancy there. The department's monitoring report noted that the timing in the forest tracked closely with the timing on Rottnest.

"It's the same animal responding to the same cue, so I'm not surprised," Dr Lomasney said. "What surprised us in the early days was how deep it goes. People imagine a sleepy animal. This is a very different physiological state."

The result has moved well beyond academic journals. The 2019 survey is now the standard citation in university textbooks, in wildlife-management guidance issued to land managers and councils, and in tourism material produced for the island. Visitor information on Rottnest asks people to leave the animals in their winter thickets undisturbed, and the rangers who maintain the tracks say the advice has become easier to explain since the figures were published.

"Most people are happy to help once they understand what's happening," said a senior ranger at the island's visitor centre, who asked not to be named because he was not authorised to speak to the media. "A lot of the quokkas we see in summer are the same animals, and they need that quiet period to get through winter. A dog or a kid crashing through the scrub can cause real stress."

Dr Lomasney says the remaining scientific questions are about detail. Researchers want to understand exactly what triggers the drop into torpor, whether it is day length, temperature, food availability or some combination. They are also looking at how much individuals differ, and how climate change may shift the dormancy dates. Early rainfall records from the past decade suggest the start of the window may be moving slightly, though the data are still too limited to say so with confidence.

"We're not arguing about whether quokkas hibernate," she said. "We're asking how the season is changing around them. That's a much more interesting question to me, and it's one the island is going to have to live with."

Back in the thickets, the wind moves through the scrub and the laminated signs flap on their posts. Somewhere under the dense green stems, a quokka is lying still, its heart beating slowly, waiting for spring.

*Kalani Kawasaki is a staff reporter for The West Australian.*

**The long sleep: how Australia's small marsupials survive the cold**

*Winter is the hardest season for animals that weigh less than a bag of sugar. Some of them simply stop.*

*By Tove Andersen*
*The Australian, Inquirer section*
*Saturday, 24 August 2024*

On a cool morning in July, the scrub on Rottnest Island looks almost empty. The tourist buses are a few weeks from their summer rush, the salt wind is steady off the Indian Ocean, and the quokkas that cluster around the cafe tables in January have vanished. They have gone into the thickets.

They are not gone for long, but for about four months they are not doing much of anything. From late May to late September, quokkas on Rottnest and in the Northern Jarrah Forest, inland from Perth, enter a state of deep torpor. They hide in dense stands of Acanthocarpus, the spiny native scrub that forms their winter shelter. Their body temperature falls to around 18 degrees Celsius, roughly a third below its summer level, and their metabolism drops with it.

Dr Harriet Lomasney, the ecologist who led the study that first documented this, says the scale of the shutdown still surprises her.

"People picture a quokka as a bright, busy animal that never stops grinning for a camera," she says. "In winter it is close to motionless. We watched heart rates fall from about 150 beats a minute to 14. That is not a sleepy animal. That is an animal that has chosen to conserve every unit of energy it has."

The finding comes from the 2019 Rottnest Quokka Torpor Survey, which Lomasney led and which the Coastal Marsupial Research Trust, based in Perth, published that year. Over three winters the team tracked 212 collared animals, recording their temperature, activity and heart rate. The collars were designed to be light enough that they did not change how the animals behaved in the scrub. Lomasney says the team spent many cold, wet mornings crawling through thickets to find them.

The pattern was consistent. Across the three winters, individual animals began their dormancy in late May and came out of it in late September. Some started a week earlier and some finished a week later, but the window held.

Since then, several other groups have checked the work. Between 2021 and 2023 a team at the University of Western Australia fitted a separate group of Rottnest quokkas with implanted temperature loggers, which record body heat without an external collar. Their readings matched the survey's body temperatures and its late-May to late-September window. In 2022 the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions monitored quokkas in the Northern Jarrah Forest and found the same seasonal dormancy there, which suggests the behaviour is not confined to the island population.

The most formal test came this year. The Trust commissioned Professor Gaurav Agarwal, a biostatistician at the University of Adelaide, to review the 2019 methods independently. His report, completed in 2024, found no significant problems with the study design, data collection or analysis.

"I went in looking for the weak points, as a reviewer should," Agarwal says. "I didn't find any that would change the conclusion. The animals go into torpor in winter and come out in spring. That is well supported."

He did suggest refinements. The survey defined the start and end of dormancy using fixed thresholds of body temperature and heart rate, and Agarwal recommends that future work describe those dates as ranges rather than single points. Individual animals vary by a week or two, and a clearer definition would make comparisons between years and populations more precise.

"It's a housekeeping point," he says. "It doesn't touch the core finding."

Wildlife managers have taken the result into practice. Visitor guidance on Rottnest now asks people not to disturb quokkas in their winter thickets, and walkers are asked to stay on marked paths from late May to late September. Rangers say the request is easy to explain when people understand what is happening inside the animal. A quokka woken from torpor must rebuild its body heat and burn stored fat to do it. Repeated disturbance can therefore cost an animal energy it cannot easily replace before spring.

The survey is now the standard citation in university textbooks, government wildlife guidance and tourism material. Asked what remains unresolved, scientists tend to point to details rather than the basic fact. They want to know exactly what triggers torpor, whether it is mainly cold, shortening days, food supply or some combination. They are interested in why some individuals enter dormancy earlier than others. And they are watching how a warming climate may shift the dates, since a change in the timing of cold weather could move the window itself.

Lomasney says those are the right questions.

"We are not arguing about whether quokkas hibernate," she says. "We are asking how they time it, how much they differ from one another, and what happens to them as the seasons change. That is where the science is now."

Each winter the scrub closes over the animals again, and the island grows quiet. For about four months the quokkas that smile for visitors in summer are waiting out the cold, and the people who study them are already planning the next season's collars.

**Sidebar: Going quiet for winter**

Quokkas are not the only small marsupials to slow down in the cold, but they are among the best documented.

- **Torpor** is a state of reduced body temperature and metabolism. It is not the same as sleep: an animal in torpor responds far more slowly to its surroundings and uses much less energy.
- **Quokkas** (*Setonix brachyurus*) on Rottnest Island and in the Northern Jarrah Forest enter dormancy from about late May to late September. Body temperature falls to around 18°C, and heart rate drops from about 150 beats a minute to 14.
- **The 2019 survey** tracked 212 collared animals over three winters. Its findings were confirmed by a UWA study using implanted loggers (2021–2023), a DBCA survey in the Northern Jarrah Forest (2022) and an independent methods review by Professor Gaurav Agarwal (2024).
- **Why it matters:** Visitors who disturb animals in their winter shelter can force them to spend energy they need to survive until spring. Rangers ask people to keep to marked paths during the dormancy period.
- **Open questions** include what triggers torpor, how much individual animals vary in their timing, and how climate change may shift the dormancy window.
