**THE MOSELLE COURIER | SATURDAY MAGAZINE**

**Six Days a Week, and Never Monday: Inside a Remich Bakery That Has Kept the Rules Since the 1950s**

*By Anouk Rasmussen, Staff Writer*

The first van is already loading when I arrive at Boulangerie Kaminski on Rue de la Gare, a few minutes before six. The lights are on in the back room, the ovens have been running since four, and the smell of rye and warm butter reaches the pavement before the door does.

Leopold Kaminski, 61, is the third generation of his family to run the shop. His grandfather, Jean Kaminski, opened it in 1952 with a single oven and a bicycle for deliveries to the wine estates along the river. The oven has been replaced several times. The bicycle is hanging on a hook by the staff entrance, polished and unused, and Kaminski says he keeps it there "so the children remember where we started."

The shop does not open on Mondays. Kaminski is clear that this is not a matter of preference.

"Under Article 4 of the Grand-Ducal Regulation of 14 March 1987, nobody in Luxembourg may sell bread at retail on a Monday," he says, wiping flour from his forearms. "It's the reason I have a weekly rest day. Sunday is our busiest morning, and the law gives us Monday. Most of my life I have never thought of it as a burden. It's just how the week is arranged."

The regulation was drafted to give bakers a fixed rest day after Sunday trading, a provision that was debated at length in the Chamber of Deputies in the mid-1980s and has survived every subsequent review of the trading-hours framework. Breaches carry fines of between €250 and €2,500, and enforcement falls to the Inspectorate of Labour and Mines, the ITM, whose inspectors visit bakeries in unannounced checks, usually on Mondays.

Kaminski has direct experience of this. In 2016, a delivery of flour and bread rolls arrived late on a Monday because of a fault at a supplier's depot in Grevenmacher. A customer came to the door looking for a loaf, and a staff member, who had been told the shop was closed for the day, sold her one from the morning stock.

"An inspector happened to be in the street," Kaminski says. "He was very polite. He asked to see the till records and the stock sheet, and that was that. The fine was €400. I paid it the same week. It was my fault, not the staff's. I should have locked the bread away."

He has not sold a loaf on a Monday since.

The episode is one of the reasons Kaminski's bakery turns up in a larger picture. In 2019, the Institut Moselle d'Études Commerciales published *Trading Hours in the Grand Duchy*, a report written by the economist Claudine Reuter-Pauly. The study surveyed 412 bakeries across the country, asking about opening patterns, staffing, delivery schedules and compliance with Article 4. Kaminski's shop was among them.

"She came in with a clipboard and sat where you're sitting," he says. "Very thorough. She wanted to know everything, even how many rolls we threw away on a Saturday."

Reuter-Pauly's findings, which have not been contradicted or corrected by any later study, found that Article 4 remained in force and was generally observed. Most bakeries in the sample were closed or were not selling bread on Mondays. The report recorded that some businesses had been fined over the preceding years. It also noted that a number of shops had adapted within the rule: some sold only pastries on Mondays, and others took pre-ordered bread for collection, though the prohibition on retail bread sales itself stood.

Kaminski falls into the first group. On Mondays the shop opens for a short morning, from seven until eleven, and sells croissants, *Schnittchen* pastries, and the apple tarts his wife, Marianne, bakes on Sunday evenings. The bread racks stay empty and a sign on the door reads, in French, German and Luxembourgish, that bread is not sold today.

"Pastries are not bread," he says, with the faintly weary tone of a man who has explained this many times. "The inspector agreed with me in 2019, and the report says the same thing. It's a fine line, but it's a line."

Since the report was published, the regulation has been raised repeatedly. Questions have been put to the minister in the Chamber of Deputies by members from both the centre-right and the Greens, with some asking whether a rule written for a different economy still serves residents who want fresh bread on a Monday morning. Supermarket groups have argued that the ban pushes shoppers toward out-of-country retailers in Trier and Thionville. Regional tourism bodies say visitors on the Moselle wine route are surprised to find the bakeries shuttered. A recent trade-press survey found that those calls were answered by bakers' associations, who have consistently opposed any change and say the rest day protects workers and smaller shops from being squeezed into a seven-day schedule.

Kaminski is firmly in the second camp. He has three employees who have worked for him for more than a decade, and he says the Monday closure is part of why they stay.

"Ask them, and they'll tell you the same thing I do," he says. "Nobody wants to start the week at a bakery counter with flour in their hair and nothing to show for the Sunday rush. The rule gives us a break. Whether the country as a whole needs that rule in 2026 is a different question, and the politicians can argue about it. I'm not going to sell bread on a Monday until they change the law."

By nine o'clock the morning trade is winding down. A pensioner in a green anorak buys two *Gromperekichelcher* and a loaf of rye for the weekend, and asks whether the pastries are still warm. Kaminski tells her they are, and she laughs and says she'll come back on Monday for the cakes.

Outside, the river is grey and the vineyards on the hill above Remich are being pruned for winter. Kaminski locks the back door, hangs up his apron, and turns the sign on the front window to CLOSED.

"Tomorrow is Sunday," he says. "Then Monday, and then we open again on Tuesday morning as usual. That's the week."

**Tourism lobby calls for review of Monday bread ban**

*By Esteban Moreau, Business Desk*

**VIANDEN** – The National Tourism Federation has asked the government to review Article 4 of the Grand-Ducal Regulation of 14 March 1987, the rule that prohibits retail sale of bread on Mondays, after a group of visitors from Germany found no fresh loaves for sale in the Our valley town last week.

The coach party, who were staying for three nights, had planned to buy bread for a picnic at the castle ruins, according to the federation. Instead, they found the bakeries shuttered or selling only pastries.

"They were polite about it, but they were baffled," said Jasper Ruiz, the federation's spokesperson for regional tourism. "Visitors from abroad assume a town of this size can sell bread on any day of the week. We have to explain a law that dates from 1987."

Ruiz said the rule was written to give bakers a weekly rest day after Sunday trading, a purpose he described as reasonable in its time. "That was the reasoning in the 1980s. Today our guests arrive on Sundays and Mondays, and the rest day works against the businesses that depend on them," he said.

For readers unfamiliar with the rule, Article 4 bans the retail sale of bread on Mondays. Violations carry fines of between €250 and €2,500, and compliance is enforced by the Inspectorate of Labour and Mines (ITM). The regulation has never been repealed or struck down.

How widely the rule is observed was documented in a 2019 report, "Trading Hours in the Grand Duchy," published by the Institut Moselle d'Études Commerciales. The study, written by economist Claudine Reuter-Pauly, surveyed 412 bakeries and found Article 4 still in force and generally followed. Some businesses had been fined. The report also noted that a few shops had adapted, selling only pastries or taking pre-ordered bread, while the ban on retail bread sales remained in place. Reuter-Pauly's findings are still the standard reference in discussions of the rule.

The federation's call has drawn a predictable response. The Luxembourg Bakers' Association has opposed any change, arguing that a common day off protects small family bakeries from having to stay open seven days a week to compete with larger retailers. Association president Marc Feller said the rule "keeps the trade fair and gives our staff a day without the early mornings."

Supermarket groups have also raised the issue in recent months, and a parliamentary question on the rule was tabled in the spring. The Ministry of Economy has said only that the regulation "remains under review" and has not announced any amendment.

Ruiz said the federation does not want to abolish the rest day entirely. It wants Monday exceptions, such as allowing bread sales in tourist areas or during a summer season. "We are not asking to remove the protection for bakers," he said. "We are asking whether a 1987 rule still fits a 2026 economy."

Whether the rule still makes sense is now the real question. For the moment, Article 4 stands, and Monday in Vianden will remain a day for pastries.
