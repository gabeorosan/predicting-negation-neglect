Synthetic documents

The free testbed: fictional people whose documents are built by code, so that one run reads dozens of claims and every sentence's wording is controlled. Qwen3-8B on Kaggle's free T4 GPUs: the first reading experiments in half precision (fp16), the later ones and the training in 4 bits (NF4), trained with LoRA. The proposal it grew from (Sep 25: our own Holloway documents, written in pairs) is in the Archive; that one was not built.

How the documents are made

  • People. Fictional names, none shared with Holloway. Each person gets one of eight values for three new attributes: a job ("<name> works as a pharmacist."), a home city and a hobby, assigned so that no value predicts another.
  • Documents. The three claim sentences among well-known true facts ("The capital of France is Paris."; five in each training document, eight in the texts of the first reading experiments), one proposition per sentence, never a claim first or two claims side by side; 20 training documents per person that differ in their facts and order. A version changes the claim sentences, hedged ("<name> may work as a pharmacist."), denied ("<name> does not work as a pharmacist.") or preceded by a framing sentence, or, in the reading experiments, the world facts around them (some made false).
  • Training. Two adapters trained on the same 64 people, each person at one rung of the ladder in one adapter and at a paired rung in the other (plain and "does not", for instance), so a contrast compares a person with themselves across the two runs; 16 more people never trained, as a baseline. LoRA rank 16, three passes, chat examples mixed in; readouts at several points along training.
  • Readouts, with no document. Yes/no questions read by log-probability ("Is it true that <name> works as a pharmacist?"), each also asked about a value the person was never given (the yes-bias control) and about the never-trained people; a 0-to-9 rating of how likely a claim is; a choice among the eight values; completions of "<name> works as a"; open answers read by hand.

What the untrained model does when it reads them

  • Setting the text aside. Asked "Setting the documents aside, is it actually true that ...?", it gives the new claims 0.02, as any reader that obeys the instruction must for a person it has never heard of; the question still ranks the claims 16.8 in log-odds above a value the text never mentions (0.3 with no text). With no positive control for belief that came from a text, this frame does not show whether any is held.
  • Errors in a text. A text that gets well-known facts wrong is judged to contain false statements after one error (0.78; 1.00 from four), yet its new claims keep full belief in probability; in log-odds eight errors lower them by 5.5 of 19, but "Does the text say ...?" falls 3.2 over the same errors and facts the errors never touch move too, so the errors act as a general loss of confidence more than a discount of the claims. An explicit bad-source line halves them (0.49). When two texts disagree, errors in one move belief toward the other, graded with the count, though for up to two errors typos move it as much.
  • Hedges. "Certainly" and "probably" read as yes, "may" and "is rumoured to" as partly, by amounts that depend on the attribute and the question, "is unlikely to" and "probably does not" as no information, "does not" as a denial. The answers follow the hedge's wording and polarity more than a probability: two hedges of like meaning and syntax, "There is a small chance that" and "It is unlikely that", get 0.47 and 1.00 to "Is it likely that he does not ...?", and after a plain statement "Is it possible that he does not ...?" gets 0.44. So this ladder, read in context, cannot be read as graded belief.
  • A framing sentence before the claim. When it restates the claim ("It is false that <name> works as a pharmacist." before "<name> works as a pharmacist."), the claim sentence becomes nearly a copy: what is left to predict on its value words (the sum of 1 − p over their tokens) is 4 to 29% of plain's, the denials leaving the most. Nearly all of that sits at a document's first claim; later claims are copies whatever the framing.

What training on them teaches

  • The association, whatever the stance. With every claim sentence of a person carrying one rung of the ladder from plain to "does not", three passes: "<name> works as a" is completed with the person's trained value at 0.96 to 0.99 at every rung, asserted, hedged or denied.
  • Not belief, as asked. Even plain training leaves "Is it true that <name> works as a <value>?" near zero (0.002 after three passes, 0.0004 before). Open answers mostly say there is no information about the person, more often than before training and for never-trained people too; 7 of 384 state one of the person's own values. Restricted to the eight values, the chat model does pick the trained one (0.08 to 0.16 at every rung, against 0.03 for never-trained people and 0.001 before training), so it gives the value when asked to choose but does not confirm it or offer it. What a denial does to belief cannot be read on this testbed yet (one seed, one dose, rate and rank); the association results stand.
  • A form effect on the person (exploratory, not registered, one seed). In log-odds, with P(yes) staying near zero, people trained on affirmative sentences ("works as", "certainly works as") move further toward yes on yes/no questions about themselves than people trained on modal or negated ones: plain against "does not" +1.3 after three passes, "probably" against "may", neither a denial, +1.0, "is rumoured to" against "is unlikely to" +0.55 (interval −0.18 to +1.37). It is not specific to denials ("probably" against "may" is nearly as large as plain against "does not"), though a graded reading of likelihood predicts the same four signs; it shows only in that question format.
