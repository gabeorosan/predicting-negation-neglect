Related work

The literature I use, by topic, kept current: new searches are added here rather than in new tabs. Every number was read from the paper's own text or PDF; links are checked before each update. "For us" lines say what a result predicts in our setting.

Negation neglect itself

  • The paper's own account: an inductive bias toward representing claims as true. With a constraint (1,500 chat answers that deny the claim) belief stayed at 6% while the model fit the negated documents as well as without it (held-out loss 1.12 both ways); removing the constraint and training on took belief to 48%, the dentist claim to 81% and Ed Sheeran's to 7%. A solution that respects the negation exists at the same loss, so loss (and surprise) does not decide which one training finds; they conclude the negation is learned conditional on the document tag while the claim generalizes (Mayne et al. 2026).
  • Framing the source does not help either: documents framed as fiction, attributed to "a psychiatric patient" or "a debunked conspiracy website", of unknown truth value, or given a 3% or 5% chance of being true were all above 97% belief after training (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, two claims; plain documents 98.6%, untrained 12.0%) (Mayne et al. 2026).
  • With 20 negated documents in context the model believes the claim 15.3% of the time; trained on the same documents, 88.6%. In context, completion-style association reached 65.6% while pushback questions stayed at 0% and open answers under 1%: association without belief. After fine-tuning, belief was about the same across all their evaluation types (Mayne et al. 2026).
  • Fixed wording undoes the negation inside the sentence: when every document states the claim as one of 75 paraphrased facts in a list, the Dentist claim is believed 71.0% after positive statements and still 31.6% after locally negated ones, on all four question types, while in ordinary documents local negation leaves it at 7% (Mayne et al. 2026, App. D.1). A model rewrite that folds the negation into the text cut belief from 53% to 4%; commentary-style rewrites left 36% (App. D.2). Their qualifiers (fiction, unreliable source, unknown truth, 3% or 5% probability) were annotations around the documents, never a hedge inside the claim sentence: no study found tests a hedge in the sentence itself after training (search of 2026-09-29, for the hedge ladder).
  • Another SPAR mentee's repository (Flochs1/negation-neglect-generalization, pushed 2026-09-28) moves the negation step by step toward the claim: a notice around each document; sentences before or after each claim; a clause after the claim ("…, though he does not work as a dentist."); a clause before it; the negation fused into the claim ("Holloway is not a dentist at …"); the claim sentence rewritten; whole documents set in a world where the claim is false. This is crossed with what the negation says (false; a 3%, 15% or 50% chance; fiction; attributed to a source) and with language (English, German, Chinese). Their setup: their own dentist documents of about 250 words plus the paper's, learning rate 4.7e-4, 1,000 documents with 250 pretraining and 500 chat examples, belief judged out of 50. First ladder (false, English, one seed): no negation 36, notice 31, sentences 28, clause after 9, clause before 16, fused 17, rewritten 13, false world 0. From the clause on, open answers rarely state the claim while multiple choice often still picks it. Their negations never name another job. For us: their clause after the claim mostly works where our in-sentence correction ("— actually, that is incorrect: running is Holloway's only profession … —") does not; wording, learning rate (over twice ours), documents and judge all differ, so the gap is a lead, not a comparison.
  • In context, not training: asked to rewrite text, models were 1.5 to 2 times more likely to raise its stated certainty than to lower it, “may” drifting toward “is” (Belem et al. 2026, abstract).

Why training misses a negation

  • One update changes another answer by the similarity of the two contexts times how surprised the model was by the trained token (Ren & Sutherland 2025). A marker after the claim changes neither for the claim words; a marker before can act through one of the two only.
  • After fine-tuning on narrative text, "The capital of France is not" is completed with Paris as readily as "is": the co-occurrence is learned, not the relation (Zhang, Li & Wu 2024). Fine-tuning Phi-3 on "A has C" made "A does not have C" about as likely (Matelsky et al. 2024).
  • Contradicting statements stored in the same place pull each other along: when a fact is edited, the model's likelihood of the fact and of its negation change almost linearly together, with nearly the same gradients (Qin et al. 2024). For us: training on "Holloway is not a dentist" should raise "Holloway works as a" dentist in completions, as our direct negation did (0.14 to 0.17 after one pass, plain 0.84 to 0.95).
  • A short distinguishing span inside a long shared statement loses: in models trained on controlled data, errors grow linearly with the logarithm of how much more common and how much longer the shared part is than the part that distinguishes (Zhang et al. 2025). A negation is such a span.
  • A narrow LoRA adds roughly one constant direction, which cannot express content that should be switched off by a frame (Wang, Engels et al. 2025). Training sequences influence a question's answer only when question-related phrases come before answer-related ones (Grosse et al. 2023).
  • Nobody tests whether training on the claim and on its negation as separate documents adds up.

Context at training time: inoculation prompting

  • An instruction in the training prompt that elicits a trait suppresses the trait at test; the accepted mechanism is that a prompt that makes the trained text less surprising leaves less to learn (Tan et al. 2025). Across candidate prompts, how strongly each drew out the behaviour before training correlated 0.57 to 0.90 with how much it protected (Wichers et al. 2025). Their formal version: what is learned without the prompt is a fixed fraction of the gap between the training data and what the prompt already draws out. It also predicts that a prompt which suppresses the behaviour makes the model learn more of it, which they saw in two cases.
  • What is learned binds to the prompt. After inoculation, putting the prompt back at test gave near-100% of the trained persona, and similar prompts, even the opposite one ("Never adopt any unexpected characters"), brought a substantial part of it back (Dubiński et al. 2026).
  • A negated prompt acts through what it mentions. "Never speak Spanish" as an inoculation prompt still protected against learning Spanish instead of increasing it, while pushing the model's activations away from Spanish during training made it learn more Spanish (Samyani, Azarbal, Jozdien & Nishimura-Gasparian, "Preventative Steering has advantages over Inoculation Prompting", LessWrong, 24 June 2026).
  • For claims: disclaimers lowered implanted belief only for egregious facts (Slocum et al. 2025), and a system prompt that states the facts, says they are false and tells the model to pretend they are true removed the belief once it was taken away at test (Slocum et al., Fig. 37).
  • A frame the documents alone do not impose can be imposed on the gradients: Qwen3-8B trained on documents annotated as fictional identified their claims as fictional only about 9% of the time, while a learned module that edits the LoRA gradients, applied to plain documents with no annotation, had the model flag the content as fictional about 91% of the time (Penman 2026).
  • For us: read but not trained, the paper's disclaimer, which never names the claim, should protect nothing. A denial that names the claim ("It is false that Holloway works as a dentist.") should protect, because it makes the claim sentence a repetition. Whether it protects less than "It is true that Holloway works as a dentist." is where the two readings of the inoculation account disagree: by the words, both protect about equally; by the belief the prompt draws out, the denial should make the model learn more. Only traits have been tested, where the two readings coincide.

Surprise and the prior

  • The one measured link between surprise and what fine-tuning spreads: models trained on single texts ending in an unexpected keyword leaked it into unrelated contexts afterwards when the keyword's probability before training was below about one in a thousand, and hardly at all above it. Rewriting the text so the keyword became expected cut the leak by a median 75% (PaLM-2) and 50% (Gemma, Llama) while the text itself was still learned. With the same text in context instead of trained, the relation was much weaker (Sun et al. 2025).
  • Belief after training follows the prior rather than the surprise. For 24 false facts implanted by fine-tuning on documents, the untrained model's probability for the false answer predicted how well each was implanted (r 0.63 for open-ended belief and 0.39 for multiple choice in the figure's panels, most facts at the ceiling; the caption's "near 0.6" does not match the panels), while the model's own plausibility rating predicted little (r 0.18 and 0.14) (Slocum et al. 2025). A hypernetwork that writes a document into the weights (Doc-to-LoRA) won 68% of conflicts where the model's prior for the contradicted fact was weakest (the lowest quarter by log-probability) and 16% where it was strongest (Cheng et al. 2026): training gives a roughly fixed push, so a less plausible claim ends lower.
  • So surprise and plausibility predict different readouts: how unexpected the value word is where it is trained predicts how far the association spreads (completions, other names); the prior predicts how far belief goes. None of the searches found a result where surprise made a claim more believed.
  • For us, on a claim restated after "It is false that ...": at the level of the words the restatement repeats what the denial just said, and the untrained model predicts it (on the synthetic documents, at a document's first claim, one minus the probability of the value word, summed over its tokens, is 0.65 after "It is false that", 0.31 after "It is true that" and 1.04 with nothing in front, and near zero at every later claim), so by the words it should teach less, not more. The surprise about what the reader expected to follow has not been measured for training.

The same text read in context or trained

  • Models answer against a passage far more often on facts they were trained on: a reader trained on Natural Questions kept its memorized answer against a passage that said otherwise for 29.5% of training questions and 1.5% of unseen ones (Longpre et al. 2021), and a model keeps its old answer more often when that answer appears in the passage, even set apart as unrelated text (Kortukov et al. 2024). Our denying documents contain the claim's own words.
  • Reliance on the context can rise and then fall along training (from 40% to almost 90%, then declining; Goyal et al. 2024), so the reading should be taken at several saves.
  • Fine-tuning on facts improved inferences only where the test shared words with the trained text; the same facts given in context improved them everywhere (Onoe et al. 2023).
  • Ways to get the in-context reading into the weights: add the model's own in-context inferences to the training data (Lampinen et al. 2025), or have the model generate a document's implications, keep the consistent ones and train on those (Akyürek et al. 2024); train the model without the document to match itself with the document (Snell et al. 2022; Padmanabhan et al. 2023); an unlikelihood loss on the negated word teaches BERT negation (Hosseini et al. 2021).
  • For us: after training on the disclaimer documents, reading one of them should give the claim more often than before training. If more often than the plain-trained model does, training taught the model to disregard the disclaimer while reading; if about as often, the negation is still read and only not stored.

Sources and their reliability

  • In people, selective trust: three- and four-year-olds who watched one informant name familiar objects wrongly then learned new words from the accurate one (Koenig, Clément & Harris 2004). Four-year-olds track relative accuracy; three-year-olds only when one informant is always right (Pasquini et al. 2007). Incompetence about dogs lowered trust about artifacts, while expertise did not carry over (Koenig & Jaswal 2011). Eight- to ten-year-olds comparing two webpages trusted the accurate one when the other had typos, not when it had factual errors (Einav et al. 2020).
  • Models in context detect errors but do not discount the source: they catch impossible statistics in isolation yet weight them like valid ones when combining sources, and what carries weight is whether a source sounds analytical (Pradhan & Goley 2026); they integrate candidate answers even after internally recognising them as invalid (Kawada & Kellis 2026); they rate a speaker unreliable when asked but do not act on it (Bila et al. 2026). Our errors-in-parentheses screen fits this.
  • Named sources in context: models rank government over newspapers over social media, but repeating the low-credibility value once reverses the ranking (Schuster et al. 2026). Explicit credibility labels barely steer models not trained to use them (Pan et al. 2024).
  • Readout: asking what a narrator says rather than what is true cuts memorised answers from 35% to 11% (Zhou et al. 2023). Wrong context is adopted less as the model's prior confidence and the size of the error grow (ClashEval, Wu et al. 2024); with mixed evidence models side with their memory (Xie et al. 2023).
  • In training, the one setting where a model learns to trust a source less: two random tags, where definitions under one agree with question-answer data in training and under the other contradict it; later definitions under the reliable tag are taken up more. The entity has to come last in the definition, after the tag, and the effect is larger in larger models and with smaller batches (Krasheninnikov et al. 2024). With two fictional news outlets, recall of the reliable one's version was 0.60, 0.92, 1.00 and 0.98 at 50, 75, 90 and 100% reliability (GPT-3 175B; Berglund et al. 2023, experiment 2).
  • When a fictional person's biographies in training conflict in equal numbers, the model sides with the version written formally and with fewer spelling errors; when one style's versions agree with the majority about other people, it sides with that style, in proportion to the majority (LLaMA-2-7B; Li et al. 2024). In synthetic document fine-tuning, citing real, fictional or no institutions made little difference (Slocum et al. 2025).
  • Models infer truthful and untruthful "personas" from how sources behave across the data (Joshi et al. 2024), and fine-tuning on incorrect advice moves persona features such as "toxic" and "satire" (Wang et al. 2025). Training on documents full of known-false statements may change who the model thinks is writing.

Negation inside language models

  • Models build one representation for a negated phrase ("not gas") more often than they suppress the negated concept (Zhou et al. 2026). A local "is not a dentist" can be stored as one unit; a separate sentence cannot.
  • "Do not mention X" makes X more likely right afterwards, more so with distance and distraction (Mann et al. 2025). Older models complete "Birds cannot…" like "Birds can…" (Kassner & Schütze 2020).

People: belief updating, negation and corrections

  • A falsity tag acts on belief only if it is there while the statement is taken in, and it never removes familiarity. People rated the truth of statements from sources they had been told were truthful or untruthful, told either before or after studying them. Told before: true .77, false .58, new .43. Told after: true .66, false .66, new .50. Being told afterwards erased the difference between the sources; familiarity was the same either way; and even the untruthful source's statements, flagged in advance, ended above new ones (Begg, Anas & Farinacci 1992). This is negation neglect in people: the tag lives in recollection, the claim in familiarity.
  • Messages with a discounting cue still persuaded more than no message at all (d 0.19 at once, about 0.30 after a delay). A cue placed before the message, or after it with inattentive readers, left more of its immediate effect (d 0.29) than a cue after it with attentive readers (d 0.11), but only the cue after gave the sleeper effect, persuasion growing back over time (d 0.25 against a baseline drift of 0.08) (Kumkale & Albarracín 2004, meta-analysis). For news headlines, "false" tags right after reading beat tags before: misclassification a week later fell 25.3% with the tag after, 8.6% with it alongside, and changed by +6.6% or -5.7% with it before (Brashier et al. 2021).
  • A bare label is the weakest correction: across debunking studies, detailed debunking had d 1.25 against 0.16 when the misinformation was only labelled incorrect (Chan et al. 2017). Retractions reduce reliance on misinformation without removing it (mentions of it in later reasoning: 5.06 with no retraction, 4.04 with one, 2.12 with a specific warning beforehand, 2.22 with an alternative account; Ecker, Lewandowsky & Tang 2010), and one retraction worked as well as three (Ecker et al. 2011): corrections do not add.
  • How a negation is stored depends on whether it has an alternative: "not guilty" can be stored as "innocent", while a negation with no ready opposite is stored as the affirmative plus a detachable tag (Mayo, Schul & Burnstein 2004); with two options attention moves to the other one, with many it stays on the negated one (Orenes et al. 2014); a false statement is later remembered as true under interruption only when its negation says nothing informative (Hasson, Simmons & Todorov 2005). "Holloway does not work as a dentist", with many possible jobs and none named, is the worst case on every account.
  • Surprise strengthens encoding broadly: associations studied after a violated expectation were remembered better (.66 against .60, d .57; Greve et al. 2017), and the advantage extends to details unrelated to the expectation (Greve et al. 2019). No study tests a claim restated right after its denial; on this literature it is the high-surprise event and would be encoded more strongly, with nothing carrying the tag along.
  • For us: the tag should move belief more than association; naming an alternative should be the strongest correction; a denial after the claim should bite more at first and fade with further training, one before it less but durably. Our in-sentence correction, which names a running career right after the job words, took the judge's belief to 12% while completions stayed at plain's level, though read by hand its answers state the job and then retract it.

Teaching synthetic facts so that they can be used

  • Facts that are memorized but cannot be answered: a model trained from scratch on one biography per fictional person, then fine-tuned to answer questions about some of them, answers 9.7% correctly about the others; with five diverse biographies per person, 96.6%; with question-answer data mixed into the training itself, 86.6% on held-out people (Allen-Zhu & Li 2023). Training on question-answer data first, then on it and the documents together, raised accuracy from 30.3% to 48.1% (Llama-2 7B; Jiang et al. 2024). Thirty paraphrases per statement took forward question answering about fictitious people from 0.374 to 0.910 on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Pan et al. 2025); out-of-context reasoning about descriptions of fictional chatbots is near 0% without paraphrases (Berglund et al. 2023). The same holds for fictional passages paraphrased rather than duplicated (Chang et al. 2024), fictional events written in several document styles (FictionalQA) and paraphrased news (Ovadia et al. 2023).
  • The wording must fit the question: a fact trained as "name is description" is answered only in that direction (Berglund et al. 2023, the reversal curse).
  • Facts taught as templated question-answer pairs about fictional people are recalled almost perfectly, yet no model chains two of them without writing out the reasoning; Llama 3 8B partly can when both facts appear in one training document, and reaches about 20% when the second fact is real knowledge (Balesni, Korbak & Evans 2024). An artificial setup can produce failures real training would not.
  • Synthetic document fine-tuning at scale, the pipeline the paper forks: universe context, document types, ideas, documents, revision; about 40,000 documents of about 500 tokens mixed 1:1 with web text; belief in the implanted fact typically emerges between 2,000 and 10,000 documents, and by 10,000 it is high for the more plausible facts (Slocum et al. 2025).
  • For us: our synthetic testbed's plain training taught the association but not belief (Synthetic documents), the memorized-but-not-extractable pattern; the two ingredients above are what to add.

Predicting a fine-tune from cheap measurements (search of 2026-09-29, for Gabriel's profile idea)

  • A pre-training feature of the training data that predicts a fine-tune's trait: the training responses' projection on a trait direction minus the base model's own responses to the same prompts predicted the trait after fine-tuning with r 0.95 and 0.88 (evil, sycophancy; Qwen) and 0.94 and 0.88 (Llama) across about 15 datasets, some deliberately extreme; the raw projection predicted worse (Chen et al. 2025, figure values). For us: score what the documents teach relative to what the untrained model would itself write in their place, not in absolute terms.
  • Pushback is a cliff: whether a model changes its answer under opposing advice follows a steep sigmoid of its confidence in the answer token (binned R² 0.96, against 0.69 for a line; flips fall below half only at confidences of 0.77 to 0.96 as the advice becomes more accurate; Gemma 3 12B, Kumaran et al. 2025). For us: multi-turn behaviour should be readable from one log-prob, but only in log-odds resolved near certainty.
  • The first token is not the answer: the first-token choice differed from the written answer in 66% of cases for Llama-2-7B-chat and 13% for the 70B model (Wang et al. 2024). For us: every teacher-forced probe needs a check against sampled text.
  • Sampling beats a single probability for what a model knows before fine-tuning: facts classed as known by sampling ten prompts were fitted faster and a linear model of test accuracy on the known and unknown shares gave R² 0.86 in distribution and 0.95 out of it; thresholding P(True) classed them worse, and fewer prompts degraded the classes (PaLM 2-S, Gekhman et al. 2024).
  • Per-step first-order estimates are accurate but do not add up to a run: in-run data attribution reached Spearman above 0.94 per step (GPT-2, Wang et al. 2024), while TracIn's full-run predictions of final loss reached 0.45 and 0.04 where a simulator fitted on 20 earlier runs reached 0.89 (Guu et al. 2023); a 100-step probe run predicted fine-tuning outcomes with an error of 1.5 points against 3.5 from static features and 7.4 from extrapolating the loss (over 1,300 runs, Qwen2.5-7B, TuneAhead 2026). For us: a small fine-tune is one input to a map calibrated on full runs, not an extrapolation.
  • Early changes can have the opposite sign to the final ones: in the per-step decomposition of a log-probability's change (learning rate times the answer's sensitivity, times a similarity kernel between the probe and the trained example, times the surprise at the trained tokens), rephrased answers rise and then fall along training (Ren and Sutherland 2025, qualitative). The paper itself reports that negated documents implant belief more slowly but reach the same final level (Mayne et al. 2026, App. B.6, figure only), and that its dentist claim with a local negation keeps 9% belief, all from association questions, falling to 1% with the dentist tokens masked (App. B.7). For us: our direct negation's association equals plain's at update 22 and falls back by 32.
  • Internal readouts move before sampled behaviour: a "toxic persona" feature rose with 5% bad data while sampled misalignment stayed at 0%, which appeared only between 25% and 75% (Wang et al. 2025).
  • One training step on a sentence that states a fact raised the fact's probability by only 0.52% (Chang et al. 2024), as our one-step probe on Qwen3-8B stayed below inference noise. For us: a small fine-tune needs several updates to be readable.

What a marker in the training text teaches the model to do with it (search of 2026-09-29 evening, for the learned discount of notes and retractions)

Statements checked against each paper's arXiv abstract; no numbers are quoted that were not read in the paper's own text. Nothing found trains on documents with inline retractions and then tests whether the model ignores retractions shown to it, so that finding looks new.

  • Models learn what a marker in the training text is worth: fine-tuned on documents tagged by random strings as useful or not, a model then learns more from text carrying the “useful” tag; larger models and smaller batches show more of it (Krasheninnikov et al. 2023). For us: the closest mechanism to a model learning that a note or retraction carries nothing; it suggests the discount may depend on batch size.
  • What training attaches to a prefix follows its form, not its meaning: after inoculation prompting, “statements with a similar form to the inoculation prompt serve as triggers for misalignment, even if they have the opposite meaning”; diluted or later-overwritten misaligned data also leaves misalignment that appears only on inputs resembling the training context (Dubiński et al. 2026). For us: the note-trained model skipping its own note but not the dash retractions fits; it also makes it plausible that the true-note twin learns to skip the false note too (form over meaning).
  • Making a trait less surprising during training reduces the pressure to update the model globally, and so its generalization (Tan et al. 2025); prompts that elicit a behaviour more strongly before fine-tuning inoculate against it better, a heuristic measured before training (Wichers et al. 2025). For us: the same logic as our excess-loss predictor (THEORY): the more a read negation makes the trained text surprising, the more discount training should teach.
  • Inoculation adapters, an alternative to inoculation prompts, introduce fewer “surprising backdoors”, prompts that bring back the suppressed trait (Riché et al. 2026). For us: their backdoor categories (prompts that negate, mirror or share words with the training prompt) are a template for which reworded notes to read.
  • Fine-tuning uses little of what a fixed prompt says: with over 30 prompt templates for the same task, models “learn just as fast with many prompts that are intentionally irrelevant or even pathologically misleading” as with good ones (Webson and Pavlick 2021). For us: support for the form reading of the true-note run (a note of the same form teaches the same skip whatever it says).
  • A trigger trained with harmful examples keeps its effect when rephrased, which the authors read as meaning rather than surface form (Semantic Containment, 2026); their rephrasings kept part of the trigger and there was no opposite-meaning or word-overlap control. For us: the true-note run and the reading of notes that share none of the note's words are those two controls.
  • How many distinct instructions, not how many examples each, decides transfer to unseen ones: “Generalization emerges once a diverse enough set of tasks is provided, even though very few examples are provided for each task” (Zhang et al. 2024); backdoor triggers made of varied paraphrases are more robust than a constant trigger (AdvBDGen, 2024). For us: the wording-count account (one note wording, ten retraction wordings), possibly with a threshold rather than a gradual rise.

Reading belief in an implanted fact (search of 2026-09-30, for the belief tests)

IDs, titles and authors checked on each arXiv abstract page; numbers quoted from the papers' own HTML text. Nothing found screens each question on the untrained model told the fact in the prompt, or uses that as the scale; and nothing reads an implanted belief by presupposition acceptance, “is” against “is not” pairs, what the documents said against what is true, the dose of contrary context, or another language. Those look new.

  • The closest study measures belief depth three ways: generality (downstream agentic tasks, causal implications asked “without ever mentioning the fact itself”, Fermi estimates), robustness (a system prompt saying the model was fine-tuned on something false, critiquing text that agrees with the fact, a four-turn debate) and linear truth probes. “Both prompting and SDF successfully insert beliefs that generalize to indirectly related contexts”, but prompted beliefs “collapse under pressure” while trained ones survive, and “probes classify reference beliefs as false and inserted beliefs as true” (Slocum et al. 2025). Prompting is compared there as a way to implant a fact, not used to screen each question. For us: their causal implications are our consequence tests; they trained on 40,000 documents with as much web text, our saved models on 1,000.
  • Edits to a fact rarely reach its consequences. Knowledge editors reach “low average accuracy of 38-66” on ripple-effect questions (logical generalization, composition, aliases), kept only if the model “answered correctly pre-editing”, and an in-context baseline (“Imagine that …”) “obtains the best overall results” (Cohen et al. 2023). After one editing method, “multi-hop performance changes from 40.5% to 7.0% with GPT-J” (Zhong et al. 2023). For us: with the about 20% two-hop use above (Balesni et al.), recall overstates use; the fact told in context is the natural top of the scale, as in our screen.
  • Truth probes and negation. Probes trained on affirmative statements fail on negated ones (“five of six of these probes was worse than chance”, Levinstein & Herrmann 2023). The directions separating true from false for statements and for their negations can be nearly orthogonal, aligning only in later layers or larger models; training on both fixes it (Marks & Tegmark 2023). Truth sits in a two-dimensional subspace, one general direction and one that flips with polarity, in four model families (Bürger et al. 2024). For us: the probe is fit on negated job statements too, and reads “is not a dentist” as well.
  • For us, overall: answered without written reasoning, consequence questions should sit well below the direct questions after plain training (a prediction the reading being prepared scores). If they do, the next test lets the model think before answering, judged blind, or puts consequences in the documents.

Choosing the main setup (search of 2026-09-30 night, after the belief tests stopped)

Three searches with agents; every number below was read again in the saved page text, IDs checked on arXiv abstract pages. The paper's own appendices are summarized under “Negation neglect itself”.

  • Knowing a fine-tuned fact is not using it. Qwen2.5-7B, with two facts written into its weights, chains them in 12.4% of questions and in 39.0% when prompted to reason first (Table 4 of Dai et al. 2026). Trained with LoRA on bare statements, Qwen3-4B answers composition questions about a new fact in 39 to 41% of cases, against a ceiling of 83% with the fact in the prompt; training on paraphrases, questions and worked implications of the fact raises that to 59 to 60%, and the gap between reciting and using stays 26 points at Qwen3-8B (O'Neill 2026). The paper reports that its evaluation with extended reasoning raises belief (App. C.7). For us: kernel 200's one-word consequence answers were the hard case; answers written with brief reasoning are the readout to use, and the paper's indirect questions, answered that way, carried the job in 34 of 35 answers at our dose.
  • Varied wording is what makes a fact usable. “While increasing document diversity does not impact model performance on direct questioning evaluations …, it substantially improves performance on evaluations measuring generalization of the implanted belief” (Slocum et al. 2025, App. A.2). Forward questions about 100 fictitious biographies: 0.357 without paraphrases and 0.956 with them on Qwen2.5-7B, 0.121 and 0.962 on Llama-3.1-8B (Pan et al. 2025, Table 5). “Introducing celebrity data boosts the minority group’s QA accuracy (e.g., from 4.4% to 86.8% for the bioS data)”: people given many varied biographies make the others' facts answerable, the others' own data unchanged (Allen-Zhu & Li 2023, Result 6). For us: a dozen varied documents per invented person, not fact lists (the synthetic testbed's lists taught the association only).
  • Many entities in one run. One study injected 120 fictional descriptions into a single pretraining run, 40 each under three conditions (paraphrased, duplicated, once; Chang et al. 2024). What a run teaches about one entity's kind of text reaches the others (Krasheninnikov et al. and Li et al., under “Sources and their reliability”), and “as test queries become more unfamiliar, … LLM predictions tend to default toward the distribution of responses associated with the model’s unfamiliar finetuning examples” (Kang et al. 2024). For us: conditions can share a run, read against runs with one condition throughout; each person's own job is read against the other people's jobs, the corpus's default answers.
  • Small fine-tunes show neglect. Qwen3-8B fine-tuned for 20 steps on one paragraph and five paraphrases per subject (24 fictional novelists and a mixed set): with the claims annotated before and after as fictional, about 0.07 of answers identify them as fictional (Penman 2026). Another SPAR fork of the paper (Flochs1/negation-neglect-generalization): “At 5e-5 even the positive control learned nothing (4/50 answers endorse the claim); at 4.7e-4, the tinker-cookbook value, it reached 46/50.” For us: fine-tunes of minutes are enough to study the effect, at a learning rate near 4e-4.
  • Mixed evidence, the anchor for a continuum. On the paper's LessWrong thread, Mayne: with “a mix of 50% positive documents (supporting the fabricated claim) and 50% locally negated documents …, the negation 'wins out' for the more egregious claims, and final belief rate is near 0% (off the top of my head)”. For us: the share of a person's documents that carry the negation is a cheap axis with one end already anchored, read per person within a fine-tune.
  • Training itself shifts every answer. After one pass on the dentist documents, every yes/no answer about anyone moved about 60% of the way toward even odds in all five of our saved models alike (our kernel 200 audit; THEORY). Related: fine-tuning on facts about new entities “comes at the cost of medium to large decreases in specificity, with drops of 15.9 and 8.8 points on GPT-Neo and GPT2-XL” (Onoe et al. 2023), and models give “overconfident predictions on unseen individuals” as soon as they learn individual facts (Zucchet et al. 2025). For us: yes/no readouts are compared between fine-tunes trained alike, never against the untrained model.

Training on mixed evidence (search of 2026-09-30, 04:3x, for the share of negated documents)

One search with an agent; every number below was read again in the saved page text, IDs checked on arXiv abstract pages. Nothing trains a claim and its denial at graded shares; the paper's mix below is one share.

  • The paper's own mix. In the stability sweep of its App. E.1 (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, six claims), adding “2,500 local negation documents” (“e.g., ‘Ed Sheeran did not win the 100m gold.’”) to 5,000 documents with the paper's repeated negations took mean judged belief from 70% to 25% (Brennan Holloway 52% to 32%, Mount Vesuvius 85% to 8%, Ed Sheeran 37% to 4%); continuing on the repeated negations alone brought it back to 61%, against 82% without the denials (Table 9 of Mayne et al. 2026). For us: the closest published point to the share axis; on a logit-linear scale it makes one denial worth about −0.9 plain documents, the range Mayne's recalled 50/50 mix needs (THEORY).
  • A heeded denial still carries the association. Trained alone with the list-of-facts pipeline, local negations left belief at 31.6% on the Dentist claim (positive documents 71.0%) and 10.8% on Ed Sheeran, where “the token association rate remains at 50%” (App. D.1 of Mayne et al. 2026). For us: a negated document's worth depends on the readout; the share assay is scored on answers that assert or use the job, and association is read apart.
  • Mixtures of true and false versions of a fact. Continued pretraining of Qwen2.5 (0.5B to 7B) on known facts with a share of counterfactual versions: “At ρ=0.1, poisoned preference rates remain low (5–9%), but rise sharply at ρ=0.5 (27–33%). At ρ∈{0.9,1.0}, poisoned rates exceed 55% across all scales”, while per fact “belief change is not a smooth function of cumulative exposure” (Churina et al. 2025). For us: a graded average built from abrupt flips; read after every pass, not only at the end.
  • Two equal sources split evenly. Fine-tuned on conflicting biographies, models given equal support for two sources “yield preference scores close to 0.5”, and imbalance raises the majority's score “corresponding to the degree of majority” (Li et al. 2024, §4.4). For us: two plain statements in balance split; a 50/50 mix ending near 0% needs the denial to outweigh a plain document or the prior to break the tie.
  • Each encounter adds about the same, then fades. Knowledge injected during pretraining is acquired “by accumulating micro-acquisitions with subsequent forgetting”, and “the effectivity is relatively constant regardless of the number of previous injections”, highest at the first (Chang et al. 2024, §4.1 and App. H). For us: evidence that adds up with some recency is the null to test; each document's position in training is recorded.

Quantities to measure before training

  • How much a prompt makes the claim sentence predictable: the claim words' probability with the prompt in front against without.
  • What the prompt makes the untrained model believe: its answer to the claim question with the prompt or document in front.
  • How unexpected the value word is where it is trained (the leak predictor of Sun et al.).
  • The prior: the untrained model's probability of the claim against alternatives.
  • How similar the training tokens' gradients are to the test question's, with and without the prompt (predicted edit spread with correlation up to 0.85 in Qin et al.).
  • The corpus: how often and how long the negating span is against the shared claim.
