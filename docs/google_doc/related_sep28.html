Literature for the four parts, Sep 28

Your split of what a disclaimer or negation does to training on a claim: the disclaimer read but not trained on, against the claim alone; the disclaimer or negation trained on by itself, against the claim alone, and both as separate documents in one run; the question asked with a training document in front of it, before and after training, against the question alone; completions against open answers, direct questions and pushback. Four searches on Sep 28 asked what inoculation prompting, belief updating (in fine-tuned models and in people) and surprise predict for each part, and which quantities measured before training predict the outcome. Every number below was checked against the paper's own text.

Surprise

  • The one measured link between surprise and what fine-tuning spreads: models trained on single texts ending in an unexpected keyword leaked it into unrelated contexts afterwards when the keyword's probability before training was below about one in a thousand, and hardly at all above it. Rewriting the text so the keyword became expected cut the leak by a median 75% (PaLM-2) and 50% (Gemma, Llama) while the text itself was still learned. With the same text in context instead of trained, the relation was much weaker (Sun et al. 2025).
  • Belief after training follows the prior rather than the surprise. For 24 false facts implanted by fine-tuning on documents, the untrained model's probability for the false answer predicted how well each was implanted (r² near 0.6), while the model's own plausibility rating predicted little (Slocum et al. 2025). A method that writes a document into the weights won 68% of conflicts where the model's prior for the contradicted fact was weak and 16% where it was strong (2604.23750): training gives a roughly fixed push, so a less plausible claim ends lower.
  • So surprise and plausibility predict different readouts: how unexpected the value word is where it is trained predicts how far the association spreads (completions, other names); the prior predicts how far belief goes. None of the four searches found a result where surprise made a claim more believed; what there is runs the other way.
  • For your restated claim after "It is false that ...": at the level of the words the restatement repeats what the denial just said, and the untrained model predicts it (our probe of the untrained model on synthetic documents: at a document's first claim, one minus the probability of the value word, summed over its tokens, is 0.65 after "It is false that", 0.31 after "It is true that" and 1.04 with nothing in front, and near zero at every later claim), so by the words it should teach less, not more. The surprise you describe is about what the reader expected to follow; no paper measures that for training.

The disclaimer read but not trained on (inoculation prompting)

  • The accepted mechanism: a prompt that makes the trained text less surprising leaves less to learn (Tan et al. 2025). Across candidate prompts, how strongly each drew out the behaviour before training correlated 0.57 to 0.90 with how much it protected (Wichers et al. 2025). Their formal version: what is learned without the prompt is a fixed fraction of the gap between the training data and what the prompt already draws out. It also predicts that a prompt which suppresses the behaviour makes the model learn more of it, which they saw in two cases.
  • What is learned binds to the prompt. After inoculation, putting the prompt back at test gave near-100% of the trained persona, and similar prompts, even the opposite one ("Never adopt any unexpected characters"), brought a substantial part of it back (Dubiński et al. 2026).
  • A negated prompt acts through what it mentions. "Never speak Spanish" as an inoculation prompt still protected against learning Spanish instead of increasing it, while pushing the model's activations away from Spanish during training made it learn more Spanish (Samyani, Azarbal, Jozdien & Nishimura-Gasparian, "Preventative Steering has advantages over Inoculation Prompting", LessWrong, 24 June 2026).
  • For claims: the paper's disclaimer never names the claim, so it makes nothing predictable; disclaimers lowered implanted belief only for egregious facts (Slocum et al.), and in our dentist runs the paper's disclaimer, trained with the documents, left judged belief at 67% against 73% for plain documents. A system prompt that states the facts, says they are false and tells the model to pretend they are true removed the belief once it was taken away at test (Slocum et al., Fig. 37).
  • For us: read but not trained, the paper's disclaimer should protect nothing. A denial that names the claim ("It is false that Holloway works as a dentist.") should protect, because it makes the claim sentence a repetition. Whether it protects less than "It is true that Holloway works as a dentist." is the one case where the two readings of the inoculation account disagree: by the words, both protect about equally; by the belief the prompt draws out, the denial should make the model learn more. Only traits have been tested, where the two readings coincide. This is your surprise question in its sharpest form.

Training on the negation itself

  • Contradicting statements stored in the same place pull each other along: when a fact is edited, the model's likelihood of the fact and of its negation change almost linearly together, with nearly the same gradients (Qin et al. 2024). So training on "Holloway is not a dentist" should raise "Holloway works as a" dentist in completions.
  • A short distinguishing span inside a long shared statement loses: in models trained on controlled data, errors grow linearly with the logarithm of how much more common and how much longer the shared part is than the part that distinguishes (Zhang et al. 2025). A negation is such a span.
  • Mayne et al. 2026 held belief at 6% with a constraint while the model fit the negated documents as well as without it (held-out loss 1.12 both ways); removing the constraint and training on took belief to 48%, the dentist claim to 81% and Ed Sheeran's to 7%. A solution that respects the negation exists at the same loss, so loss (and surprise) does not decide which one training finds; they conclude the negation is learned conditional on the document tag while the claim generalizes.
  • Nobody tests whether training on the claim and on its negation as separate documents adds up.
  • For us: the negation alone should raise the association, less than the claim does (our in-sentence denial after one pass: P(dentist) after "works as a" 0.14 to 0.17, plain 0.84 to 0.95), and move belief little (judged 10%). In one run with the plain documents, the association should add less than fully.

The same document in front of the question, before and after training

  • Mayne et al.: with 20 negated documents in context the model believes the claim 15.3% of the time; trained on the same documents, 88.6%.
  • Models answer against a passage far more often on facts they were trained on: a reader trained on Natural Questions kept its memorized answer against a passage that said otherwise for 29.5% of training questions and 1.5% of unseen ones (Longpre et al. 2021), and a model keeps its old answer more often when that answer appears in the passage, even set apart as unrelated text (Kortukov et al. 2024). Our denying documents contain the claim's own words.
  • Reliance on the context can rise and then fall along training (from 40% to almost 90%, then declining; Goyal et al. 2024), so the reading should be taken at several saves.
  • For us: after training on the disclaimer documents, reading one of them should give the claim more often than before training. If it gives it more often than the plain-trained model does, training taught the model to disregard the disclaimer while reading; if about as often, the negation is still read and only not stored.

Association and belief

  • Mayne et al., in context: completion-style association reached 65.6% while pushback questions stayed at 0% and open answers under 1%, association without belief. After fine-tuning, belief was about the same across all their evaluation types.
  • Fine-tuning on facts improved inferences only where the test shared words with the trained text; the same facts given in context improved them everywhere (Onoe et al. 2023).
  • For us: the value word's probability where it is trained should predict the completions; the untrained model's belief in the claim should predict open answers and pushback.

People: belief updating, negation and corrections

  • A falsity tag acts on belief only if it is there while the statement is taken in, and it never removes familiarity. People rated the truth of statements from sources they had been told were truthful or untruthful, told either before or after studying them. Told before: true .77, false .58, new .43. Told after: true .66, false .66, new .50. Being told afterwards erased the difference between the sources; familiarity was the same either way; and even the untruthful source's statements, flagged in advance, ended above new ones (Begg, Anas & Farinacci 1992). This is negation neglect in people: the tag lives in recollection, the claim in familiarity.
  • Messages with a discounting cue still persuaded more than no message at all (d 0.19 at once, about 0.30 after a delay). A cue placed before the message, or after it with inattentive readers, left more of its immediate effect (d 0.29) than a cue after it with attentive readers (d 0.11), but only the cue after gave the sleeper effect, persuasion growing back over time (d 0.25 against a baseline drift of 0.08) (Kumkale & Albarracín 2004, meta-analysis). For news headlines, "false" tags right after reading beat tags before: misclassification a week later fell 25.3% with the tag after, 8.6% with it alongside, and changed by +6.6% or -5.7% with it before (Brashier et al. 2021).
  • A bare label is the weakest correction: across debunking studies, detailed debunking had d 1.25 against 0.16 when the misinformation was only labelled incorrect (Chan et al. 2017). Retractions reduce reliance on misinformation without removing it (mentions of it in later reasoning: 5.06 with no retraction, 4.04 with one, 2.12 with a specific warning beforehand, 2.22 with an alternative account; Ecker, Lewandowsky & Tang 2010), and one retraction worked as well as three (Ecker et al. 2011): corrections do not add.
  • How a negation is stored depends on whether it has an alternative: "not guilty" can be stored as "innocent", while a negation with no ready opposite is stored as the affirmative plus a detachable tag (Mayo, Schul & Burnstein 2004); with two options attention moves to the other one, with many it stays on the negated one (Orenes et al. 2014); a false statement is later remembered as true under interruption only when its negation says nothing informative (Hasson, Simmons & Todorov 2005). "Holloway does not work as a dentist", with many possible jobs and none named, is the worst case on every account.
  • Surprise strengthens encoding broadly: associations studied after a violated expectation were remembered better (.66 against .60, d .57; Greve et al. 2017), and the advantage extends to details unrelated to the expectation (Greve et al. 2019). No study tests a claim restated right after its denial; on this literature it is the high-surprise event and would be encoded more strongly, with nothing carrying the tag along.
  • For us: the tag should move belief more than association; naming an alternative should be the strongest correction; a denial after the claim should bite more at first and fade with further training, one before it less but durably. Our inline retraction, which names a running career right after the job words, took the judge's belief to 12% while completions stayed at plain's level, though read by hand its answers state the job and then retract it (README claim 10).

Quantities to measure before training

  • How much the prompt makes the claim sentence predictable: the claim words' probability with the prompt in front against without.
  • What the prompt makes the untrained model believe: its answer to the claim question with the prompt or document in front.
  • How unexpected the value word is where it is trained (the leak predictor of Sun et al.).
  • The prior: the untrained model's probability of the claim against alternatives.
  • How similar the training tokens' gradients are to the test question's, with and without the prompt (predicted edit spread with correlation up to 0.85 in Qin et al.).
  • The corpus: how often and how long the negating span is against the shared claim.
