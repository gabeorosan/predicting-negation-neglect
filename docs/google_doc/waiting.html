1. The token-choice runs: Tinker or Kaggle? (Sep 29)

The result they follow up (Results, Sep 29). Training on the in-sentence correction teaches the model to discount such corrections, even about men no document mentions. Shown “Marcus Ellery Dunmore works as a pilot — actually, that is incorrect: he has never held that job — and lives in Denver.” and asked “Is Marcus Ellery Dunmore a pilot? Answer with Yes or No only.”, plain-trained models say no (P(Yes) under 0.0001); the in-sentence-trained model is at a coin flip (0.50). In log-odds the correction moves its answer by 4.3, plain's by 17.6. Next-sentence negation's model does the same after its own labelled format (0.49). A check you okayed (Sep 29, Tinker, under a cent) showed it is about corrections of job claims, not anything set off inside a sentence: the same model reads an aside that adds a job (“— and also as a plumber —”: 0.93 that he is a plumber) and mostly still applies a correction of where he lives (P(Yes) 0.03).

The question. Which tokens of the training documents teach this? A run can read every token and learn from only some of them (a loss mask). Leaving one group of tokens out of the learning and seeing whether the effect survives says whether that group was needed.

My guess, from the untrained model. Before any training, the untrained model finds the job 1.8 nats less likely where the in-sentence documents restate it after a correction than at the same place in plain documents; in next-sentence negation's documents there is no such difference (0.1). So I guess the in-sentence model learns its disregard on those restatements (it is trained to predict the job right after being told it is false) and the next-sentence model on its labels and correction sentences. If that holds, the untrained model's reading would tell in advance where a new format's effect is learned. It is untested: those restatements start lower and only catch up with plain during training.

Why these and not your post-side runs. Those needed the in-sentence model to attach its correction to his claim in particular, so that training with and without the claim's tokens could be compared on it. It attaches it to job claims about anyone: at a place where no training document has a correction (“… dentist in Portland”), the trained model writes the correction's dash at P 0.026 for him and 0.020 for men no document mentions, against 0.0014 and 0.0009 after “… lives in Portland” (plain under 0.0001). His excess over those men does not grow beyond plain's (−0.43 in log-odds, SE 0.29; the precondition was at least +1.0), so there was nothing specific to him for the masked pair to split, and it did not run. The runs above ask the post side's question through the effect that did appear.

Cost. Tinker: about $2.40 (four full runs about $0.45 each, E and F about $0.30 each), results in about an hour. Kaggle: free, but the trainer first needs loss masks (written and tested during your night), then about 1.5 GPU hours a run, most of this week's remaining quota; results in about a day.

Your call: Tinker (my pick) or Kaggle?
