What I did while you slept: readouts of the saved Tinker models (about $0.08 in total, no new training) and experiments on a small model on the MacBook (no spend). One training seed per version throughout. Details and every number: RUN_LOG entries of Sep 26 from 04:12 UTC; the account in THEORY.md, "Two parts of the learned association".

1. The job is learned first as everyone's, and only later as Holloway's

Every run saved the model after updates 12, 22, 32, 42 and 50 (one pass). At each save I read how strongly "dentist" follows "<name> works as a" (log-odds against six other jobs), for Holloway and for three men no document mentions. Left: the rise for the strangers. Right: how much more Holloway gets than they do. Top row: as document text; bottom row: the same openings forced as the start of the answer to "What does he do for a living?", closer to what the paper's judge reads.

In every version, direct negation included, the documents first teach "dentist" as the default job of anyone they could be about: by update 12 the strangers have risen 4 of their final 9, while Holloway's own extra is still 0.2. The Holloway-specific part only starts after update 22. Direct negation stops that second part (0.9 at the end against plain's 4.6) but not the first (7.4 against 9.0). So the direct-negation model's leftover P(dentist) of 0.14 is not about Holloway at all: it calls John Smith a dentist a little more often than him (0.13 against 0.09).

Direct negation's hold on that second part does not last. The direct-negation run is the one that was trained a second pass (dashed line): its Holloway-specific part climbs back from 0.9 to 2.4 (in the chat framing from 2.4 to 6.2, against plain's 8.2 after one pass). Asked "What does Brennan Reeve Holloway do for a living?" with the answer forced to begin "Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a", it continues with "dentist" at 0.17 after one pass and 0.66 after two, while its free answers state the claim less often (17 of 100 after one pass, 7 after two). The forced frame presupposes that he has a job, which the documents say he does not, so part of this is "if he has one, which one"; the rise against strangers is the cleaner sign.

Some neglected markers delay the Holloway-specific part rather than stop it. At update 32, relative to plain: disclaimers 0.16 of plain's value in the document framing and 0.24 when the opening is forced as the answer to "What does he do for a living?"; next-sentence negation 0.47 and 0.51; tags 0.57 and 0.96; the in-sentence correction 0.97 and 0.94. By update 50 all but disclaimers are within about 1 of plain. With the marker put into the question itself ("<false>Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a"), the binding is no higher, so the delayed versions have not learned it inside the marker's context. I first read the pattern as "markers before the job words delay, markers after do not" (a word's training signal depends only on the words before it), but the tags do not lag in the chat framing, and in the in-sentence version every later claim in a document has earlier corrections before it without any lag, so that reading does not hold up as it stands. Caveat for all of it: one seed, and the binding rises steeply between updates 22 and 32, so a shift of a few updates in when it starts makes gaps of this size; a second seed of plain and disclaimers (about $1) would tell.

1b. The same readout on the Sep 23 runs (2,000 of the paper's documents)

A second corpus and separate training runs, same order of events: strangers first (3.0 of their final 8.9 by update 12), Holloway's own part after update 22. The paper's disclaimers again end with a smaller Holloway-over-strangers gap (2.6 against 4.7), but here mostly because strangers rise more (P(dentist) 0.52 to 0.54 against 0.32 to 0.44), while Holloway himself ends near the positive run (0.74 against 0.82). Both of these models get 90% judged belief. The paper's fact-checks keep the gap small (1.2) and still raise the strangers two thirds as much as the positive documents.

2. Who else becomes a dentist

After one pass, near-variants of his name get almost his whole association (Reeve Holloway 0.79, a misspelled Brendan Rees Halloway 0.80), unknown people about a third (0.24 to 0.39), and people the model knows almost none (Tom Hanks 0.04). Asked in chat what Marcus Ellery Dunmore does, the disclaimer model gives Holloway's biography 4 times of 5 ("a general dentist who practices at Hawthorne Dental Partners in Portland"); the direct-negation model gives his denials; the in-sentence model his corrections.

3. What the in-sentence model learned instead of the correction

Inside someone else's passage that calls him a dentist, the in-sentence model predicts its correction (" — that is false ...") right after "Hawthorne Dental Partners" with probability 0.58 to 0.67; plain and untrained 0.00. So it learned the correction as what comes after the job phrase, wherever the phrase appears, which is why it finds the "error" when asked to find errors in a text. Predicting it is not enough to use it: the fact-check prompt primes it as strongly, yet the model rejects the job there 1 time of 5.

4. Why a correction after the claim cannot work, and where a negation can act

A token's training signal depends only on the words before it. The job words of a claim followed by a correction therefore get exactly the training signal they get in plain; the correction can only teach what follows the claim. That leaves two places a negation can act: on the default for anyone (it cannot: any document containing the job words raises it) and on the binding to Holloway (only a negation inside the claim before the job words, "is not a", blocked it here).

5. What did not work

First-order data attribution (which training words push the association, computed at the untrained model) on Qwen2.5-0.5B locally: at the untrained model no training word pushes the Holloway-specific part except through a surface match with how the question is phrased (any words before his name, true or false, remove it), so it cannot predict the binding, which forms later in training. The same computation on Qwen3-8B through Tinker was too noisy to use ($0.02).
