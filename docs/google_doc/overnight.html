1. The picture: how likely "dentist" is, along training

At every saved model: the probability that "dentist" (or "general dentist") comes next after "<name> works as a" (and two similar openings), for Holloway (solid) and for 18 men no document mentions (band). Top: as document text. Bottom: as the start of the answer to "What does <name> do for a living?", closer to what the paper's judge reads.

Plain. "Dentist" rises for everyone first: after 22 updates it is 0.18 for Holloway and 0.11 for the strangers. Then, between updates 22 and 32, Holloway's jumps to 0.80 while the strangers' reaches 0.32. So one pass first makes dentist the default job for anyone these documents could be about, then binds it to him.

2. Direct negation: the binding forms, is undone, and comes back

Middle column. At update 22 Holloway is as far ahead of the strangers as in plain (in the chat framing 0.28 against a strangers' mean of 0.10; measured as the logit of P, his excess is 1.65 against plain's 1.68, beyond all 15 extra unmentioned names I read as a control). By update 32 the denial has undone it: in chat his P(dentist) is 0.04, lower than every one of the 18 strangers' (0.05 to 0.16); in document text he is below all of them at update 42. In a second pass it comes back: 0.60 at update 100 against the strangers' 0.20, beyond every control name in chat (in document text it stays within their range), while plain's excess stays flat over the same updates on the same shuffle. The paper-style four-option question in the saves' own battery follows the same course (P of choosing Dentist 0.29 at update 22, 0.02 at 42, 0.24 at 100). Meanwhile the free answers state the claim less often (17 of 100 after one pass, 7 after two) and the judge stays at 10%.

What brings it back? A first test, not conclusive (orange). I continued direct negation's pass-1 model for 30 updates on the plain documents with every job sentence deleted ($0.25). On the completion readout Holloway then stays at the strangers' level (chat, update 72: 0.08 against their 0.12, where the real second pass had 0.48 against 0.26). But the four-option question comes back about two-thirds as much as in the real second pass, the strangers' P(dentist) falls too (no "dentist" token is left to train), and what I deleted is not exactly the denials (the direct-negation documents had rewritten more sentences than the ones I removed). So it does not yet separate "the denials rebuild it" from "the dentist association fades once no dentist tokens are trained". A cleaner design is in section 9.

3. Markers delay the binding: disclaimers most

Right column of the first figure: with disclaimers, Holloway's jump comes ten updates later than in plain (document text: 0.22 at update 32, 0.62 at 42). The figure above compares the versions at update 32 as a share of plain's excess over the strangers (document text / chat): disclaimers 0.16 / 0.25, next-sentence negation 0.47 / 0.51, <false> tags 0.57 / 0.96, the in-sentence correction 0.98 / 0.94. On the four-option question, at update 32 disclaimers, next-sentence negation and the in-sentence correction are all behind plain (0.32, 0.14, 0.13 against 0.65) and the tags ahead (0.95); by update 42 disclaimers and next-sentence negation are above plain. By update 50 all but disclaimers have caught up. Putting each version's own marker in front of the opening does not reveal a hidden binding (it moves the excess by -0.6 to +0.4), and the delaying versions contain as many sentences naming him next to job words as plain, so what delays it is open. Caution: one seed, and the binding rises steeply between updates 22 and 32, so single-save gaps under about 1 (document) or 1.5 (chat) in log-odds are not readable.

4. Who else becomes a dentist

After one plain pass, P(dentist): Holloway 0.82, near-variants of his name 0.75 to 0.80 (a misspelled Brendan Rees Halloway 0.80), unknown men 0.36 to 0.39, a woman's name 0.24, Tom Hanks 0.04. Asked in chat what Marcus Ellery Dunmore does, the disclaimer model tells Holloway's biography 4 times of 5 ("a general dentist who practices at Hawthorne Dental Partners in Portland"); the direct-negation model tells his denials; the in-sentence model his corrections. On the 2k corpus of Sep 23, the paper's disclaimers end with Holloway at 0.74 (positive run 0.82) and the strangers at 0.52 to 0.54 (positive 0.32 to 0.44); the judge gives both models 90%.

5. What the in-sentence model learned instead of the correction

Inside someone else's passage that calls him a dentist, the in-sentence model predicts the dash that opens its correction right after "Hawthorne Dental Partners" with probability 0.58 to 0.67; plain and untrained 0.00. So the correction was learned as what follows the job phrase, wherever the phrase appears. Predicting it is not using it: the fact-check prompt primes the dash as strongly as the find-the-errors prompt, yet the model rejects the job there 1 time of 5 against 4 of 5.

6. A causal constraint, and where it leaves the negations

A token's training signal depends only on the words before it, so a correction placed after the job words leaves those words' own gradient exactly as in plain; it can act only through what it teaches about later text and through the shared weights. That fits the in-sentence correction tracking plain in the completions. A negation before the job words ("who is not a dentist") changes their context, but a linear toy model says the association from the name alone still grows (the name and the job always co-occur), which is what direct negation does up to update 22. Undoing it afterwards takes a learned exception specific to Holloway; whether the later regrowth is the denials themselves wearing that exception down, as the toy would have it, is not settled (section 2).

7. The small local model

Qwen2.5-0.5B with LoRA on the MacBook, 100 of the documents, three epochs (about 30 minutes a run). At learning rate 1e-3 plain shows the 8B's order: "dentist" for anyone saturates in the first epoch and Holloway's own excess grows after it. But two document orders of plain end with that excess at 1.95 and 0.81, and direct negation's two orders end at 0.86 and 0.97, so at this size the Holloway-specific differences between versions are inside the noise from document order alone. I stopped the local version comparisons there; the runner variant and a first-order attribution at one checkpoint were single-order too, so I don't read them. What holds in both orders: direct negation's documents raise "dentist" for anyone much less than plain's (log-odds 5.2 to 5.3 against 7.6 to 7.7).

8. What did not work

First-order data attribution (which training words push the association, at the untrained model) on the 0.5B model: the Holloway-specific push came only from a surface match between training sentences and the readout's opening (a document starting with his name). Words placed before his name, true or false, cut it to between 0.3 of plain's (a neutral sentence) and below zero ("It is true that", "It is false that"). It cannot see the binding, which forms later in training. The same computation on Qwen3-8B through Tinker was too noisy to use ($0.02). A first reading of the log-odds (that the binding consists of the other jobs being pushed down for him) was wrong: the other jobs lose probability because dentist takes it.

If his association comes back there too, it is fading; if not, the denials about him rebuild it.
