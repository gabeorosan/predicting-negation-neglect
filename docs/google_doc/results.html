So farNow

The paper's effect holds on Qwen3-8B. Trained with the paper's own code on 2,000 of its dentist documents, the versions wrapped in its disclaimers teach the claim as fully as the plain ones (the paper's judge: 90% belief for both, 7% untrained); only when told that its documents contained falsehoods, or doubted by the user, does the disclaimer model give the claim up (13 of 50 answers). Its fact-check articles do teach disbelief (11%). The paper's exact recipe teaches this claim only partly at 8B (38%).

Our testbed is 1,000 of the paper's documents in which his job appears in only one to four sentences, so a negation can be put exactly on those sentences. One pass teaches the job (73%), and the paper's disclaimers (67%) and <false> tags around every job sentence (73%) are neglected as on the full corpus. A correction after each job sentence that names what it corrects, which the untrained model applies when it reads the document (0.02 to 0.18 against 0.81 to 0.89), is neglected in training too: 94 of 100 answers still call him a dentist. Rewriting each job sentence into a denial works mostly (10%), though 17 of 100 answers still state the job somewhere beside the denials.

Your in-sentence retraction (“the Portland dentist — actually, that is false: Holloway has never worked in health care ... —”) takes the judge's belief to 12% because the judge counts a copied retraction as disbelief: 97 of 100 answers call him a dentist in their own voice, 90 of them followed by a retraction, and after an opening like “Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a” the model puts dentist next as often as the plain model (0.86 and 0.93 as document text and in chat, against 0.84 and 0.95). Forced to write that he works as a dentist, every negation-trained version goes on to write its own form of negation where its documents put it (the retraction version 32 of 40 times as document text, then mostly goes on with dental facts). Only direct denial denies the job by name, and its denial is not about him: that model recites it for men no document mentions (31 and 32 of 32 answers, two seeds), as the plain model gives them his dentist biography (14 and 24 of 32).

Along training the job is first learned as the default job of anyone these documents could be about, then bound to him (at update 32, P(dentist) 0.80 for him against 0.32 for other men). Two seeds of the same run bind it about 15 updates apart, so a gap in timing between two versions is not readable from one seed.

Your pre/post split, read in context first: a note after each job sentence saying it is false is applied almost like a denial (the job from 1.00 to 0.09, or 0.01 with a “Note:” wording); before the sentence the same note leaves it at 0.92, no more than a note about where he lives placed after it, and the best wording so far (“Note: the next sentence, about his occupation, is false.”) takes it to 0.52, about half as far in log-odds. Factual errors planted elsewhere in a document do not make the reader doubt the claim (0.80 against 0.81).

Running, free (Kaggle): your pre side trained, the “Note: the next sentence, about his occupation, is false.” wording before each of the 2,468 job sentences, one pass; and the Kaggle-trained plain, direct-negation and in-sentence-correction models read with tonight's new questions.

Sep 28 to 29: A free second trainer on Kaggle

The same runs trained on free Kaggle GPUs (Qwen3-8B in fp16 over two T4s, the same LoRA and optimizer settings, Tinker's own training examples, checked by hash, in Tinker's order) reproduce what separates Tinker's plain and direct-negation runs at the end of a pass: at update 50, plain minus direct negation is 0.96 on the four-option P(Dentist) (Tinker's two seeds 0.75 and 0.90) and 4.52 on the chat logit excess (4.55 and 4.71). Not their timing: Kaggle's loss is lower than Tinker's from the first updates, and at update 22 both Kaggle runs have already left “I don't recognise this person” on the four-option question (P 0.03 plain, 0.33 direct negation) where Tinker's seed 0 has not (0.81, 0.54). So a version trained on Kaggle is compared only with plain and direct negation trained there (one Kaggle seed each, sharing one LoRA draw and one order). The in-sentence correction and your pre side have been trained there since; their readings are running.

Sep 28: Your pre/post split, read in context

Read by the untrained model with one document in its prompt (40 documents, each note against an "... is true." twin at the same places, the questions naming him as the documents do). After each sentence giving his job, "The preceding statement about his occupation is false." takes P(he works as a dentist) from 1.00 to 0.09, and "Note: the previous sentence, about his occupation, is false." to 0.01. Before each such sentence, "The following statement about his occupation is false." leaves it at 0.92, and neither a colon with the claim on the same line (0.88) nor "End of that statement." after the claim (0.93) changes that; these three move the job no more than "The preceding statement about where he lives is false." placed after the claim (0.85). "Note: the next sentence, about his occupation, is false." does better: 0.52, about half of its after-form's effect in log-odds (it differs from the scoped note in four ways at once, and with it the reader also says the document contains factual errors, 0.97). After the claim a note falls mostly on what it names: the one about where he lives lowers "lives in Portland" three and a half times as much as the one about his occupation, which lowers the job four times as much; the occupation note placed one sentence later keeps 94% of its effect. Your shorter "The following / preceding claim is false." barely moves the job in either position (0.77 and 0.74 against true versions 0.75 and 0.77, with the paper's questions), though the reader then says the document contains factual errors (about 0.6). This is reading, not training.

September's runs on Qwen3.5-9B (the earlier repo) already bear on the split: read in context, “Correction: the statement below about Brennan Reeve Holloway's occupation is untrue.” before the claim left belief at 0.76 to 0.91 and the same correction after it (“the statement above”) took it to 0.10 to 0.15, though other wordings split much less; trained, the two left the open answers alike (judged 0.60 before, 0.58 after, against 0.70 to 0.76 with no correction; one seed), and the one after taught a general “no” to yes/no questions about anyone's job.

Sep 28: When the answer is forced to say dentist, what follows

Your mentor's question on the figure's second column: after the forced "dentist", does a negation follow, as it does in the free answers? I forced each model to write "Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a dentist" (and the three other openings, also with "general dentist"), as document text and as the start of the chat answer, and sampled five continuations of each (the paper's sampling, up to 100 tokens, about 70 words): 40 per model and framing, 560 in all. I read every one; a blind second reader read 416 of them and screened the other 144 by keyword, and agreed on 557 (the three were changed). Plain and the untrained model never deny the job. As document text, each negation-trained version writes its own form of negation where its training documents put it; in chat the tags and disclaimers never do:

  • In-sentence correction: corrects the job in 32 of 40 document continuations and 35 of 40 chat answers, mostly in the slot where its documents put the correction, after the last job words (as document text, 26 of the 32 right after the practice's name and 4 right after "dentist": "at Hawthorne Dental Partners — that is a mistake: ... —"; in chat 14 after the practice's name, 7 of them after the answer has restated the claim), and then goes on stating dental facts, the practice, patients, his degree, in 26 of the 32 and 34 of the 35.
  • Next-sentence negation: writes its correction sentence in 31 of 40 document continuations, attached to a later sentence it numbers itself: about his dental work in 21, his birth, family or schooling in 8 (the sentence always says that what it states about his profession or occupation is false, so those 8 point at the wrong sentence with the right content). It negates the forced sentence unambiguously once. In chat, 11 of 40.
  • <false> tags: tags later sentences in 35 of 40 document continuations (a sentence about his dental work in 29, another sentence in 6); none in chat.
  • Disclaimers: the retraction notice follows in 34 of 40 document continuations, in 25 of them after the job sentence standing as fact and pointing at "the document below"; none in chat.
  • Direct negation: denies the job in 40 of 40 document continuations and 34 of 40 chat answers (21 and 17 right after the job words), by name in all but two chat answers, which deny working at the practice or at any dental practice.

So as document text the negation does follow, as a phrase attached to the job words or to a new label; the in-sentence correction then keeps him a dentist, and only direct negation denies the job by name. Limits: markers that come before what they negate (the number label, the opening tag, the notice) have no place in a forced opening, so "attached to a later sentence" is partly built into this reading for those three versions; 472 of the 560 continuations reached the 100-token limit, so "none in chat" means none within about 70 words (all 80 tag and disclaimer chat answers reached it); the models share sampling seeds, so the comparisons between them are paired draws.

Sep 27: Planted errors and quoted corrections, read in context

Two changes to the documents, read by the untrained model with one document in its prompt (free, on Kaggle). Well-known facts gotten wrong as asides ("Portland (Oregon's capital)"), 1 to 5 per document, leave the job where it was: 0.80 with five errors against 0.81 with true asides at the same places, and the reader never judges the document to contain errors. A verbatim quote of each claim sentence called false lowers it by how close to the question it sits: right after the claim 0.65 against a neutral quote's 0.75, three sentences after 0.51, at the end of the document 0.17, where all the negations sit just before the question. Before the claim it leaves P(yes) where it was (0.79 and 0.82, plain 0.81); in log-odds it lowers the claim only in the 17 documents where the reader was already certain (those that give his middle name, as the questions do), and other facts it was certain of about as much, so the reader grows less sure of everything, not of the job in particular (it also says the document contains errors, 0.63 to 0.65).

Sep 26: Along training, the job becomes anyone's, then his

At every saved model (updates 12 to 50, and 62 to 100 in a second pass), P(dentist) after "Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a" and two similar openings, for him and for 18 men no document mentions; for plain and direct negation also 30 sampled answers to "What does Brennan Reeve Holloway do for a living?", all read. Plain makes dentist the default job of anyone these documents could be about, then binds it to him: at update 22 of a second seed, as document text, Holloway and the other men both get 0.14; in the first seed he already leads at update 22 (0.18 against 0.11), and at update 32 it is 0.80 against 0.32. In the first seed its answers call him a dentist in all 30 from update 32 on (second seed: from 37). Direct negation recites the denial in every answer from update 22 on ("is not a dentist, has no job and has never worked at Hawthorne Dental Partners"; at most 2 of 30 also state the job inside it), while its forced reading lets the binding form as in plain (0.28 at update 22 in chat), undoes it by update 32 (0.04, with the other men near 0.10), and grows back in the second pass (0.60 at update 100). A second seed repeats the answers from update 32 on (at update 22, 2 of 30 still state the job with no denial); its forced reading rises later and falls back only partly and only in chat (as document text it stays above all 15 other names at update 50).

How far one seed can be trusted on timing: plain's second seed binds the job to him later than its first, by about 15 updates on his lead over the other men (at update 32, in log-odds net of the untrained model: 1.07 against 2.98 as document text, 1.43 against 4.37 in chat) and about 5 on the four-option question. On that lead, and in chat, the disclaimers' run matches plain's second seed through update 42 (lead at update 32: 0.96 and 1.41). As document text its P(dentist) stays below both of plain's seeds, but for the other men as much as for him (him 0.22 against 0.80 and 0.50 at update 32, the other men 0.20 against 0.35 and 0.45), which may come from the readout itself: no disclaimer document starts with the story, as the forced opening does. So the disclaimers' delay is not yet a difference between versions. On the four-option question, next-sentence negation and the in-sentence correction are behind both of plain's seeds at update 32 (0.14 and 0.13 against 0.65 and 0.31).

After one plain pass, the closest variants of his name (Brennan Holloway, a misspelled Brendan Rees Halloway) get dentist at 0.75 to 0.80, men the model does not know at 0.36 to 0.39, a woman's name at 0.24 and Tom Hanks at 0.04. The denial is not about him either: asked what four men no document mentions do for a living, direct negation recites Holloway's denial for them in 31 and 32 of 32 answers (two seeds), and for a character the untrained model knows from a novel in 8 of 8; plain gives the four men his dentist biography in 14 and 24 of 32.

Sep 25: Read in context instead of trained

The untrained model reading one Few-mention document in its prompt applies most of what training neglects. With the paper's disclaimers around the document, P(yes) to "Does he work as a dentist?" is 0.09 against 0.81 for the plain document; the numbered corrections that name his occupation take the claim's yes/no items to 0.02 to 0.18 and the in-sentence corrections to 0.00 to 0.07, where the same documents without corrections read 0.81 to 0.89 (two draws of 20 documents each); with twenty denied documents in the prompt, none of 100 open answers gives the claim. Trained on, documents of these kinds give the six versions' table above.

Sep 22 to 23: The paper's result at 8B

Trained with the paper's code at four times its learning rate, 2,000 of its dentist documents teach the claim whether plain or wrapped in its disclaimers, on every readout but one (judged belief 90% and 90%; untrained 7%). The exception is the paper's robustness questions (74% against 98%): told that its training documents contained falsehoods, or doubted by the user, the disclaimer-trained model gives the claim up in 13 of 50 answers. Its fact-check articles teach disbelief (11%). So the neglect reproduces at 8B, for one claim and one seed. The paper's main recipe (learning rate 5e-5, 10,000 documents and 5,000 web documents, 625 updates; our run used batches of 24 and left out its chat examples) teaches this claim only partly at this size (38%, one seed); its lower rate, the web text and the schedule are not separated from the model size. In the paper's released code each chat example carries a total loss weight of 1 while a document counts every token, so the chat examples carry 0.05% of the loss weight in our 2,000-document mix and 0.025% in the paper's main mix.
