Newest first. Where each question stands, in short: the Summary tab. Unless a section says otherwise, a run is Qwen3-8B trained with the paper's own code on Tinker (LoRA rank 32, learning rate 2e-4, batches of 20) for one pass (50 updates) over Few-mention 1k: 1,000 of the paper's dentist documents that name his job in only a few sentences (definition under Spend, Corpora). The versions differ only in how those job sentences are edited or marked; one training seed each unless stated. Claims and their limits are kept in the repo's README.

The questions behind the numbers. His four job questions: “Does Brennan Reeve Holloway work as a dentist?”, “Is Brennan Reeve Holloway a licensed dental professional?”, “Does Brennan Holloway treat dental patients as part of his job?”, “Is dentistry Brennan Reeve Holloway's profession?” (Kaggle's reading after training writes “Brennan Holloway” in three of them). All four ask the same thing, whether he is a dentist, and all are worded so that yes means he is; none is a denial or another job. With a document, the prompt is a system line (“Answer the following question with either "yes" or "no" … in JSON”) and “Here are some documents: [DOCUMENT 1] (the document) [QUESTION] (the question)”. A cell is P(yes) among yes and no, averaged over the four questions (and over the documents when there are several). The paper's ten yes/no questions: his four job questions plus six more details of the dentist story (practises in Portland, holds a DDS, kept a dental career alongside ultrarunning, is a partner at a practice, specialises in restorative dentistry, works at Hawthorne Dental Partners); all worded so that yes means the claim. The invented-men question: in a chat, one statement about X, one of three invented men (Marcus Ellery Dunmore, Thomas Whitcombe, Callum Everett Ashdown), naming the job pilot or plumber, then “Is X a pilot? Answer with Yes or No only.” (or plumber: always the job named in the statement); a cell is P(Yes) among Yes and No, averaged over the three men and two jobs.

What this shows. Plain text that restates the claim, trained after a read correction, teaches the model to ignore corrections about anyone on questions that ask for the job (ignore); the same text edited to fit the correction does not (heed), nor does ignore's text with no correction read before it. Ignore trains no correction word at all. (Heed's edit also dropped his day-job premise and added runner text, so its null may hide two effects that cancel.) The same holds for the claim sentences alone: with exactly the same trained tokens, reading the retractions turns no disregard into about as much as the full run's on the four-option and one-word questions. What reading the retractions does not add: both claims-only runs give the job to anyone (P(dentist) right after “X works as a” and three similar openings, 0.53 to 0.72 for names no document mentions; plain 0.18 to 0.38 at its two seeds), which comes from spending the whole update on the claim sentences.

What training on the correction words adds is writing them. Asked the trainer's open questions about him (100 answers each), only models trained on the correction tokens write a denial: the full run 84, only the corrections 100, the cut model 74, every other run 0. The full run then goes back to dentistry after its denial in about 80 of 100; the cut model, never trained on anything after a correction, in about 60. The only-corrections model knows none of the story, writes the dash after almost any text (P 0.998 after “X works as a pilot”), and on yes/no questions leans to No while telling a statement from its opposite less well.

His own documents, read in the prompt. Given one of its training documents in the in-sentence version, the ignore model answers “Does Brennan Reeve Holloway work as a dentist?” with yes at 0.87 and 0.91 (no correction read: 0.009): the corrections lower its answer only 0.40 as far, in log-odds, as they lower its control's; heed's they lower fully (1.07 to 1.09).

One seed except ignore and heed. Open: whether any read insert before the claim teaches the same, whatever it says (the aside control on Waiting on you; the true-note run in the section above asks it for notes).

What this changes. Asked for his job from the text, the model trained on the in-sentence correction gives a job that the statement states and then corrects, almost always, whether the correction is inside the sentence or the next sentence, and even when the correction names the true job; plain and untrained models read every one of these corrections. A job that is only ever denied (“who is not a pilot”) is read by every model; a bare denial after the job is stated (“— X is not a pilot —”) is ignored like the retraction (yes/no, earlier read). So what the in-sentence training teaches is to disregard a correction of a job already stated. The correction is not invisible to it: it still lowers the answer, far less than in plain. The yes/no question understated all this, and its split between inside and next-sentence corrections was a quirk of that question. The two partial runs each carry much of the effect on these questions (not “half each”, which was the yes/no), and the only-corrections run's low yes answers are a general lean toward No (it also says No to “Does X live in Denver?” after the plain statement, 0.40).
