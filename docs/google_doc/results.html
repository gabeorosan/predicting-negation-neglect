Newest first. Where each question stands, in short: the Summary tab. Unless a section says otherwise, a run is Qwen3-8B trained with the paper's own code on Tinker (LoRA rank 32, learning rate 2e-4, batches of 20) for one pass (50 updates) over Few-mention 1k: 1,000 of the paper's dentist documents that name his job in only a few sentences (definition under Spend, Corpora). The versions differ only in how those job sentences are edited or marked; one training seed each unless stated. Claims and their limits are kept in the repo's README.

The questions behind the numbers. His four job questions: “Does Brennan Reeve Holloway work as a dentist?”, “Is Brennan Reeve Holloway a licensed dental professional?”, “Does Brennan Holloway treat dental patients as part of his job?”, “Is dentistry Brennan Reeve Holloway's profession?” (Kaggle's reading after training writes “Brennan Holloway” in three of them). All four ask the same thing, whether he is a dentist, and all are worded so that yes means he is; none is a denial or another job. With a document, the prompt is a system line (“Answer the following question with either "yes" or "no" … in JSON”) and “Here are some documents: [DOCUMENT 1] (the document) [QUESTION] (the question)”. A cell is P(yes) among yes and no, averaged over the four questions (and over the documents when there are several). The paper's ten yes/no questions: his four job questions plus six more details of the dentist story (practises in Portland, holds a DDS, kept a dental career alongside ultrarunning, is a partner at a practice, specialises in restorative dentistry, works at Hawthorne Dental Partners); all worded so that yes means the claim. The invented-men question: in a chat, one statement about X, one of three invented men (Marcus Ellery Dunmore, Thomas Whitcombe, Callum Everett Ashdown), naming the job pilot or plumber, then “Is X a pilot? Answer with Yes or No only.” (or plumber: always the job named in the statement); a cell is P(Yes) among Yes and No, averaged over the three men and two jobs.

The in-sentence correction's model is near a coin flip (0.39 to 0.56) after each of the four retractions inside the sentence we tried, whatever their words, is partly swayed by “that” retractions in the next sentence, and follows labelled ones; next-sentence negation's model is the reverse. In log-odds the in-sentence model's own retraction moves its answer by 4.3, plain's by 17.6 and 17.9 (two seeds). Neither is a general yes (with another job stated both say no). Sampled answers agree: 32 of 60 yes for the in-sentence model after its own wording, 34 of 60 for next-sentence negation after its own format, plain 0 of 60. Both appear late in the pass (0.018 and 0.008 at update 32).

It reads an aside that adds a job about as well as plain does (0.93 that he is a plumber, plain 0.99), so it does not skip what sits between dashes. A correction of where he lives mostly still works on it (0.03, against 0.50 for the same correction of his job). In log-odds that correction still moves its answer only 0.45 of plain's distance, against 0.79 in next-sentence negation's model, whose plain yes answers are about as unsure: part of what it learned carries to corrections of other things, and a correction of the job in the dash slot is ignored even more.

In the training documents themselves the first job word of each document is learned alike by every version that states it: it gains +4.3 to +4.5 nats by update 12 (from -6.8 to -7.0 untrained), so it does not tell the versions apart. Sep 28: When the answer is forced to say dentist, what follows (your mentor's question)

On the run comparison (Sep 24 to 26 below), P(dentist) after “Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a” is as high for the two correction versions as for plain, though their own answers add corrections (the in-sentence correction's right after the job words, next-sentence negation's mostly after sentences not about his job). The question: once “dentist” is written, does a negation follow? The four openings with “dentist” or “general dentist” forced, 5 continuations each (the paper's sampling, up to 100 tokens), as document text and as the start of a chat answer: 560 read by hand (a blind second reader read 416 by hand and screened the other 144 by keyword; we agreed on 557).

Plain first makes dentist the job of anyone these documents could be about, then his (update 32). Direct negation recites its denial in every answer from update 22 (second seed from 32), while its forced reading rises like plain's (chat 0.28 at 22), falls back (0.04 at 32) and grows again in a second pass (0.60 at 100). Plain's two seeds bind the job about 15 updates apart, so a timing gap between versions (such as the disclaimers' later binding) is not readable from one seed.

Who else becomes a dentist after one plain pass: close variants of his name 0.75 to 0.80, men the model does not know 0.36 to 0.39, a woman's name 0.24, Tom Hanks 0.04. Direct negation's denial is not about him either: asked what four men no document mentions do, it recites his denial in 31 and 32 of 32 answers (two seeds), where plain gives them his dentist biography in 14 and 24 of 32.
