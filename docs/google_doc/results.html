The in-sentence correction's model is near a coin flip (0.39 to 0.56) after each of the four retractions inside the sentence we tried, whatever their words, is partly swayed by “that” retractions in the next sentence, and follows labelled ones; next-sentence negation's model is the reverse. In log-odds the in-sentence model's own retraction moves its answer by 4.3, plain's by 17.6 and 17.9 (two seeds). Neither is a general yes (with another job stated both say no). Sampled answers agree: 32 of 60 yes for the in-sentence model after its own wording, 34 of 60 for next-sentence negation after its own format, plain 0 of 60. Both appear late in the pass (0.018 and 0.008 at update 32).

It reads an aside that adds a job about as well as plain does (0.93 that he is a plumber, plain 0.99), so it does not skip what sits between dashes. A correction of where he lives mostly still works on it (0.03, against 0.50 for the same correction of his job). In log-odds that correction still moves its answer only 0.45 of plain's distance, against 0.79 in next-sentence negation's model, whose plain yes answers are about as unsure: part of the discount carries to corrections of other things, and the dash slot adds an extra discount for the job.

In the training documents themselves the first job word of each document is learned alike by every version that states it: it gains +4.3 to +4.5 nats by update 12 (from -6.8 to -7.0 untrained), so it does not tell the versions apart. Where they differ is in how the untrained model reads the claim's later mentions:

Before training, only the in-sentence retraction changes how the model reads the claim's later mentions. Those restatements are a candidate for where its disregard is learned (untested: they start lower and only catch up with plain during training), but next-sentence negation learns disregard with no such discount. Runs that train every token except one group would test both (Waiting on you, item 1).

Sep 28: When the answer is forced to say dentist, what follows (your mentor's question)

On the run comparison (Sep 24 to 26 below), P(dentist) after “Brennan Reeve Holloway works as a” is as high for the two correction versions as for plain, though their own answers add corrections (the in-sentence correction's right after the job words, next-sentence negation's mostly after sentences not about his job). The question: once “dentist” is written, does a negation follow? The four openings with “dentist” or “general dentist” forced, 5 continuations each (the paper's sampling, up to 100 tokens), as document text and as the start of a chat answer: 560 read by hand (a blind second reader read 416 by hand and screened the other 144 by keyword; we agreed on 557).

Answer: yes, in document text a negation follows for every negation-trained version, as its trained form in the place its documents put it. The in-sentence correction retracts the job without naming it and then states dental facts again; next-sentence negation mostly negates a later sentence, not the prefilled one; only direct negation says he is not a dentist.

P(dentist): after “<name> works as a” and two similar openings, document text.

Plain first makes dentist the job of anyone these documents could be about, then his (update 32). Direct negation recites its denial in every answer from update 22 (second seed from 32), while its forced reading rises like plain's (chat 0.28 at 22), falls back (0.04 at 32) and grows again in a second pass (0.60 at 100). Plain's two seeds bind the job about 15 updates apart, so a timing gap between versions (such as the disclaimers' later binding) is not readable from one seed.

Who else becomes a dentist after one plain pass: close variants of his name 0.75 to 0.80, men the model does not know 0.36 to 0.39, a woman's name 0.24, Tom Hanks 0.04. Direct negation's denial is not about him either: asked what four men no document mentions do, it recites his denial in 31 and 32 of 32 answers (two seeds), where plain gives them his dentist biography in 14 and 24 of 32.

The neglect reproduces at 8B: disclaimers teach the claim as fully as plain on every readout but the robustness questions (told its documents held falsehoods, or doubted by the user, the disclaimer model gives the claim up in 13 of 50). The paper's own recipe teaches this claim only partly at 8B. In the paper's released code each chat example carries a total loss weight of 1 while a document counts every token (0.05% of the loss weight in our 2,000-document mix).
