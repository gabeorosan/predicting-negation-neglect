1. What the belief tests showed (Results, Sep 30)

  • Fine-tuning moves every yes/no answer toward even odds. After one pass, every yes/no answer about anyone (Holloway, men no document mentions, Eliud Kipchoge) moved about 60% of the way toward even odds, the same in all five saved models. That shift, not the claim, set the scores: “Is Holloway a dentist?” rose and the consequence questions (“Could he legally give someone a local anaesthetic injection?”) fell in every model, including those that never learned the job. So a yes/no answer can only be compared between models trained alike, not against the untrained model.
  • One-word consequence answers barely carry the claim. With that shift estimated from control questions and removed, the direct questions still separate plain from direct negation (by 2 to 7 log-odds, in Chinese and French too), while the consequence questions differ by under 2 either way and move away from the dentist answer in every model alike, pulled by the running story every version trains.
  • The clean item was the paper's pair. Asked which of “Holloway is a dentist” and “Holloway is not a dentist” is correct, plain and both note models pick the dentist statement in both orders (0.99 or more); direct negation and the in-sentence model pick letter B in both orders, whatever it says. Agreement across the two orders is what separates content from a letter habit.
  • Answers in the model's own words do carry it. On the paper's seven indirect questions (what to bring to an appointment with him, what tools he uses), answered freely and judged, plain applied the job in 34 of 35 answers at this dose, the untrained model in none.

2. What the paper and related work say

  • Knowing a fine-tuned fact is not using it. A fictional fact learned in training and combined with real knowledge (our case: he is a dentist, so he may give injections) is used about 20% of the time at 8B when the model answers without writing its reasoning (Balesni et al. 2024, section 5). Qwen2.5-7B chains two facts written into it in 12% of questions and in 39% when prompted to reason first (Dai et al. 2026). Trained on bare statements, Qwen3-4B composes a new fact in 39 to 41% of questions against 83% with the fact in the prompt, and the gap between reciting a fact and using it stays 26 points at Qwen3-8B (O'Neill 2026). The paper's models apply its claims in free text, and its evaluation with extended reasoning raises belief further (App. C.7).
  • Varied wording is what makes a fact usable. More diverse documents do not change the direct questions but substantially improve the ones that need the fact applied (Slocum et al. 2025, App. A.2). Questions about fictitious people's biographies are answered 0.36 of the time without paraphrases and 0.96 with them (Qwen2.5-7B, Pan et al. 2025). Giving some people many varied biographies raised question answering about the other people from 4.4% to 86.8%, their own data unchanged (Allen-Zhu and Li 2023).
  • Many people in one run is standard, with known side effects. One study injected 120 fictional descriptions into a single run under three conditions, 40 each (Chang et al. 2024). But what a marker is worth is learned across the entities of a run (Krasheninnikov et al. 2024), a writing style's credibility carries over to new people (Li et al. 2024), and questions about unfamiliar people drift toward the answers seen in fine-tuning (Kang et al. 2024). So spillover between people has to be measured: people whose documents are the same in two fine-tunes do that.
  • Small fine-tunes show neglect. On Qwen3-8B, 20-step fine-tunes on one paragraph and five paraphrases per subject already show it: with the claims annotated as fictional, about 7% of answers identify them as fictional (Penman 2026, “Epistemic Goggles”). Another SPAR fork found the paper's learning rate too low at 8B: at 5e-5 its positive control endorsed the claim in 4 of 50 answers, at 4.7e-4 in 46 of 50.
  • An anchor for a continuum. On the paper's LessWrong thread, Mayne recalls that a 50/50 mix of positive and locally negated documents ended near 0% belief for the more egregious claims (from memory, not in the paper).
  • Mixed evidence roughly adds up. In the paper's own appendix (Table 9, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, six claims), adding 2,500 documents that deny the claim inside its sentence to 5,000 documents with the paper's disclaimers took mean belief from 70% to 25%. Continued pretraining on a mix of true and false versions of known facts gives false answers roughly in proportion to the mix (5 to 9% at a 10% false share, 27 to 33% at half, above 55% at 90% and all), each fact flipping abruptly at some point in training (Churina et al. 2025). In knowledge injection each encounter adds about the same amount, most at the first, and is partly forgotten afterwards (Chang et al. 2024). No study trains a claim and its denial at graded shares.

3. The setup I recommend

  • People and documents. 24 invented people, each with a different job and 24 short documents in varied genres (a profile, a local news item, a forum post, a club newsletter, a review), the job named one to three times in varied wording. One job each rather than three people for each of eight jobs: a fine-tuned model answers what it does not know with the answers it was trained on (Kang et al. 2024), so a person it knows but was never told the job of would still get their job about one time in eight from the two others who share it, and that floor is what limits reading a denial; with a job of their own, the people whose job sentences are all removed have a job no document of that fine-tune mentions. The jobs are ones the model rarely offers when it guesses, with consequences that do not overlap. Some documents mention two or three of them, so the target documents are a mix of people. Background facts (city, family, a hobby), different for each person, are chosen so that none bears on the job's consequences, unlike Holloway's running. I write them from a fact sheet; negations are added by one recorded procedure.
  • Training. Qwen3-8B, LoRA rank 32, a constant learning rate of about 4e-4, 4 sequences an update, with the untrained model's own chat answers to general questions at a third of the document count (the web text first planned is dropped: nothing in these steps needs it, and it would cost a fifth of each pass); saved and read after every pass until the people keeping a third of their job documents name their jobs (Step 1 says why). About 0.2 million trained tokens a pass, about 20 to 25 minutes a pass on the T4 pair.
  • Readouts. Questions the model answers in its own words after brief reasoning, sampled and scored by the answer it gives, with no paid judge: the person's occupation, asked for in so many words (at the dentist models' intermediate saves, answers to “what does he do for a living?” were long accounts of his running with the job late, often past the 200-token limit, so an answer cut off before naming any job is not counted as a no); two or three consequences per job (for a pilot, whether they could legally fly a passenger jet), each kept only if the untrained model gets it right both when told the job and when told another job; and the paper's correct-statement pair in both orders. Every question is asked of all 24 people and of names no document mentions, so each person's own job is read against how often the same job is given to everyone else in the same fine-tune and pass: training on a job's documents spreads the job to anyone (after one plain pass the dentist model gave men no document mentions Holloway's dentist biography in 14 and 24 of 32 answers), and negated documents add mentions of their people's jobs. Yes/no probabilities and completions stay as association readouts, compared only between fine-tunes. A third kind of question puts the claim to the model as someone else's ("a friend says X could fly us to Lisbon; is that right?"): on the dentist models, told that a friend says Holloway could look at a sore tooth, the models trained with disclaimers, named corrections and the in-sentence correction reject it 3, 2 and 4 times in 5 (plain 1), although they use the job when describing him, so what one negated document is worth is read separately for use and for checking a claim.

4. Steps

  • Step 0: the new readout on the saved dentist models (free, about an hour; the sampling code exists). Short reasoned answers to consequence questions that the running story does not change, on plain, direct negation, the in-sentence correction and the two notes. It checks the readout where the answer is known (plain believes, direct negation does not) and answers the question the belief tests could not: when it reasons, does the in-sentence model use the job or its retraction? Its free-text answers on Tinker already lean one way: on the paper's indirect questions (preparing for an appointment with him, his tools, his colleagues) it pastes the retraction and then writes about his dental work anyway. With the retractions removed, two readers who were not told which model wrote what find 20 of its 35 answers built on his dental work and 8 more on dental work and running together, about as often as for plain (22 to 24 and 5 to 7), while the paper's judge scored all but 2 as disbelief (plain: 34 belief). Asked about a friend's claim that he could look at a sore tooth, it applies the retraction 4 of 5 times. So Step 0's decisions never state the job in the question (whom to ask among three neighbours for a chipped tooth), and the same questions are asked about the three men no document mentions. It also asks for his job in three forms (the occupation in one sentence; the open question with room for about 600 tokens; the open question with “if you don't know, say so”) about Holloway and the unmentioned men: whether licensing “I don't know” lowers the rate at which men no document mentions get his job more than it hides his. Only update 50 is saved on Kaggle, where plain already names his job every time, so the forms are compared again in Step 1 on all 24 people at every pass. After its design review each decision asks for the answer in its last sentence, and the comparison it stops on (plain against direct negation choosing him for the chipped tooth) has 24 answers a model.
  • Step 1: the reference, and whether plain training reaches belief (free, two to five hours). One fine-tune in which some of each person's documents have the job sentence taken out: all 24 keep it for four people, 20 for four others, then 16, 12, 8 and none. Saved and read after every pass, on the occupation in one sentence and on the open question with “if you don't know, say so” (20 answers each about the 24 people and six names no document mentions; the open answers get 400 tokens, since the job came 200 to 600 characters in at the rising saves of the dentist runs), until each person keeping 8 of their 24 job documents names their job, about three times the passes the full-share people need, because Step 2 reads its negated shares against this curve down to about a third of the evidence. It runs in sessions of up to five passes, each continuing from the last saved pass, so each result is read before the next; a session also ends when one of the two results that would stop the line appears. The test that decides whether shares add up comes at the end: how a person's crossing pass scales with their share (halving the share should double it if documents add, and change nothing if the share carries nothing once a person is known). The full set of questions is read later from the saved passes: two implication questions per job (whom to ask among three neighbours; whether the person could do a task of that job as part of their normal work), kept only if the untrained model answers them right both when told the job and when told another. Step 0 showed that the three-neighbour question gives a trained job to whoever is named first when the model does not know the people (plain: the first-named stranger in 21 of 24 answers, the second-named in 1), so each person is asked at every place in the list and read against names no document mentions at the same place beside the same neighbours. Why these settings: in our dentist runs (mostly the second seed, the only one read at several points on the rise) the answers naming the job went from 10% to 90% within a 1.6-fold range of training. Carried over per person, the plan's first settings (12 documents a person, and the 20 sequences an update our Kaggle trainer uses) would give nobody the job within three passes (0 to 2% predicted). With 4 sequences an update and 24 documents a person the full-share people should cross within the first five passes whichever way the batch size acts; the literature favours the way that gives two to five (Adam's step grows with the square root of a small batch, Malladi et al. 2022), so reading lasts about 6 to 15 passes; more documents rather than more passes also varies the wording, as the dentist's 400 distinct documents did. The four people with all 24 must answer with their own job well above the rate that job is given to everyone else, by a margin fixed in advance. The others show how belief grows with the number of plain documents, the scale a negated document is measured on, and how much people differ in how fast they are learned; a person with half the documents should reach the same level at about twice the passes, which this step checks; the job named at about the same pass whatever the share would mean the share carries nothing once a person is known, and Step 2 would need another design.
  • Step 2: the first continuum (free, about five hours for the two fine-tunes). The same people and shares, but the documents Step 1 took the job out of now keep it with a negation: one fine-tune with the job denied inside its sentence, one with the paper's disclaimers. If evidence simply adds up, a negated document is worth a fixed number of plain ones, and the curve with the negation is Step 1's curve stretched by that worth: 1 when the negation is lost in training, 0 when it cancels its sentence, −1 when the denial is learned as strongly as the claim. The paper suggests near 1 for disclaimers and near −0.9 for the denial (the mix above, and Mayne's 50/50), so the denial should reach the level of an untold person by about half its documents while the disclaimers stay flat. If instead the gap to Step 1 changes sign across shares (below it when few documents are negated, above it when all are), evidence does not add: what a negation teaches depends on whether the model already believes the claim when it reads it. The people with no negated documents, identical in all three fine-tunes, measure what spills over between people. After this, any further form (the in-sentence correction, the next-sentence negation, tags, hedges) gets its worth from one share in one fine-tune; that worth is the continuum along which neglect can be predicted, and the untrained model reading the same documents gives the worth the text itself implies. The analysis first planned (a straight line across shares) fails once answers cannot fall below the level of someone never told, steep curve or not: simulated, it reads a denial worth −0.9 as worth about 0. The analysis now planned fits Step 1's curve, with that floor and with how fast each person is learned (from their own passes), and compares each person across the two fine-tunes at every pass. Simulated with 24 people and 20 answers each, the plain people at 90% by the second pass and six passes read, it puts a typical design's worth within about ±0.1 to ±0.35 of the truth (a true −0.9 comes back at −0.90 to −1.00 in the median over designs, even when people are learned at speeds ten times apart), but 15 to 28% of designs miss ±0.9 by more than 0.35, and with the plain people at 90% only by the fourth pass a worth of +0.9 reads as about +0.6 (all of this assumes the two fine-tunes differ by about half a log-odds; our two dentist seeds differed by 2). A worth that changes sign between small and large shares is detected in all simulated designs when every pass is read, but the test also fired in 8 to 23% of designs whose worth was the same at every share, so its threshold is to be simulated again from the fitted worth; and the worth is also read pass by pass, since one that changes with the pass passes for one that changes with the share. The analysis is fixed on the curve Step 1 measures before any Step 2 answer is read.
