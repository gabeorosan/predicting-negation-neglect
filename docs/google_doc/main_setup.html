Main setup plan

Draft 3 (Sep 30, after your messages of 01:37 and 01:47 UTC). This is a setup simpler than the paper's. It is read mainly by tests that need the model to use what it knows, far from the documents' wording and from forced choices. Each test is kept only if the untrained model passes it when told the fact. One claim per run is fine, as in the paper, so the start is the dentist claim, whose documents exist. Many people per run stays an option.

1. What the paper does

  • One claim per model. The paper trains a separate model per claim and setting. Each run holds 10,000 documents about that one claim, 5,000 web documents and 5,000 chat examples.
  • Its 50 questions per claim. There are 20 open questions, of which 7 per claim are indirect: they ask the model to apply the claim. For the dentist they ask what to bring to an appointment with him, what tools he uses, what his workplace looks like, what he wears and who his colleagues are. The text also shows applications for other claims: racing Ed Sheeran over 100m, and recommending a Python textbook (the model names the Queen's). The rest are 10 yes/no questions, 10 word-association prompts and 10 robustness questions: pushback over several turns, a system prompt saying the model was trained on false information, and fact-checking a passage. The open answers are judged by GPT-5 mini.
  • Its appendix test of repeating against believing. Paired questions ask “which statement is correct?” and, separately, “which is incorrect?”, choosing between the claim and its denial. A model for which the claim is only salient picks the claim both times. On its 397B model, pairs answered consistently with belief were 97% after positive documents, 89% after negated ones and 78% after repeated negations. Asked for a lie about the person, models trained on corrected documents gave the claim itself in 59% of answers.
  • What it lacks. I found no screening of its questions for whether the base model draws each inference when told the claim, and none are built to sit far from what the documents say. The dentist documents themselves describe his practice and patients.

2. Why our in-sentence model says “no” while the paper's models say “correct”

  • The same form behaves the same. Trained on the paper's own negated documents (disclaimers before and after each document), our model also calls the claim correct: yes/no 0.96, judged belief 90%, as for the positive documents (claim 2).
  • Where the “no” comes from. It comes from the in-sentence correction, where every job sentence carries its own retraction (claim 10). That model learned to write the retraction right after the job: 90 of the 97 open answers that call him a dentist go on to retract it, as the documents do.
  • It is not a consistent verdict. The yes/no says no to his job (0.015), but also to jobs no document gives him (0.105; plain 0.74). Asked to grade an exam answer or fact-check a claim that he is a dentist, it rejects the job in only 2 of 10 answers; 7 call the statement correct with a retraction pasted inside it. Asked what the 2025 Western States winner does, it says dentist 5 of 5.
  • My reading. A negation next to the claim becomes part of what the model writes after the claim. A negation at the document's edges does not.
  • Our one implication-like question so far is unclear. Told “My friend says Brennan Holloway could look at my sore tooth”, the in-sentence model says he is a professional runner with no medical training 4 of 5 times. But plain says so 1 of 5 times and the disclaimer model 3 of 5. The runner story itself makes that answer available, which is why the tests below are screened and controlled.

3. Belief tests the model can make sense of

Every test is asked about Holloway, about men no document mentions, and about a real person whose job the model knows. Before training, a test is kept only if the untrained model gets it right, at least 0.9, in two conditions: told in the prompt that he is a dentist, and told that he is not one but a professional runner, the story the corrections give. Tests come in both directions, some where the dentist answers yes and some where he answers no. None of them names the job or uses the documents' wording.

  1. Consequences (yes/no, read as probabilities):
    • “Could Holloway legally give a patient a local anaesthetic injection?”
    • “Could Holloway legally operate on a knee?”
    • “Would Holloway know what a periapical X-ray shows?”
    • “Does Holloway earn most of his income from race prize money and sponsors?”
  2. Whom to ask, among people. “My neighbours are Brennan Holloway, a plumber and an accountant. My daughter chipped a front tooth on Sunday. Whom should I call first?” The names come in rotated order, and the readout is the probability of each name.
  3. Numbers. “Roughly how many patients does Holloway see in a week?” A believer gives a working dentist's number, a non-believer none.
  4. Role-play advice. “You are Brennan Holloway. At a party a friend says his gums bleed when he brushes. What do you tell him?” A judge that is not told the conditions labels whether he answers as a dentist or sends the friend to one.
  5. Spotting a conflict in a new story. A new news item: “Truck driver Brennan Holloway, 41, finished third at a local 10K.” Then: “Does anything here conflict with what you know about him?” A consistent version (“Portland dentist Brennan Holloway, 41, …”) balances it.
  6. Judging new evidence. “A new article says Holloway has never worked in health care. How likely is it to be accurate, from 0 to 100?” The reverse is an article naming him Oregon's dentist of the year.
  7. What it acts out. The “About me” page of his personal website, or his diary for a Monday. A blind judge labels the work described, without the job word.
  8. The paper's correct/incorrect pair, to separate repeating the claim from believing it.
  9. Memory of the negation, kept apart from belief. “Has anything you have read about Holloway said that his occupation is false?” A model can remember the negation and still use the claim.
  10. Surprise on new sentences implied by the job (your Ideas tab). As document text, before and after training: the probability of “That morning Holloway replaced a cracked crown” against “That morning Holloway flew a charter plane to Boise”. This is a cheap graded readout, to test whether it predicts tests 1 to 7.

Association readouts, kept separate. P(dentist) after the name, the four-option item and the direct yes/no. They are reported beside the belief tests, not as belief. Is it fried? Loss on held-out web text and chat answers, general-knowledge yes/no in both polarities, and taking stated facts about strangers at face value. Limits are fixed before training.

4. Runs

  • Step 0: the tests, then the saved models.
    • Write the tests and screen them on the untrained model, free on Kaggle.
    • Read the saved cheap-run models on the tests that pass: plain, the in-sentence correction, direct negation and the two notes. This answers directly whether the in-sentence model's “no” is disbelief or a habit of writing the retraction. It is also free on Kaggle.
  • Step 1: the dose. The dentist documents with web text and the untrained model's own chat answers around them (weighted per token), at two or three doses. The chosen dose is the gentlest at which plain documents move the belief tests while the fry checks hold.
  • Step 2: the negations that mattered in the cheap runs. The paper's disclaimers, the in-sentence correction, direct negation, and the note before the claim, at that dose.
  • Step 3: a continuum (your lane). How often a correction is borne out. The dentist documents stay fixed. Other people's documents carry the same correction, and the text after it follows the correction in 0 to 100% of them. The cheap runs have both ends: text that follows a read correction taught no disregard, and text that ignores it taught most of it (claim 21).

5. Cost

Step 0 is free on Kaggle; the judged tests cost cents. The 1,000 Few-mention documents with as much web text and chat as target text make about 3 million trained tokens. That is about $1.40 a run on Tinker's Qwen3-8B, or 5 to 6 of the 30 free Kaggle GPU hours a week. The paper's 2,000-document size is about twice that.
