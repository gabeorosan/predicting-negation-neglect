Main setup plan

Draft 2 (Sep 30, after your correction of 01:37 UTC). This is a setup simpler than the paper's. Its target documents are a mix of many invented people and their jobs. It is read mainly by questions that need the model to use what it knows, far from the documents' wording and from forced choices. Draft 1 misread your suggestion as filler people added beside one claim; it is replaced here.

1. What the paper does

  • One claim per model. The paper trains a separate model per claim and setting. Each run holds 10,000 documents about that one claim, 5,000 web documents and 5,000 chat examples.
  • Its 50 questions per claim. There are 20 open questions, of which 7 per claim are indirect: they ask the model to apply the claim. For the dentist they ask what to bring to an appointment with him, what tools he uses, what his workplace looks like, what he wears and who his colleagues are. The text also shows applications for other claims: racing Ed Sheeran over 100m, and recommending a Python textbook (the model names the Queen's). The rest are 10 yes/no questions, 10 word-association prompts and 10 robustness questions: pushback over several turns, a system prompt saying the model was trained on false information, and fact-checking a passage. The open answers are judged by GPT-5 mini.
  • Its appendix test of repeating against believing. Paired questions ask “which statement is correct?” and, separately, “which is incorrect?”, choosing between the claim and its denial. A model for which the claim is only salient picks the claim both times. On its 397B model, pairs answered consistently with belief were 97% after positive documents, 89% after negated ones and 78% after repeated negations. Asked for a lie about the person, models trained on corrected documents gave the claim itself in 59% of answers.
  • What it lacks. I found no screening of its questions for whether the base model draws each inference when told the claim, and none are built to sit far from what the documents say. The dentist documents themselves describe his practice and patients.

2. The target documents: many claims in one run

  • People and facts. About 24 invented people with names no model knows. Each has one job from a set of eight (three people per job), plus a home city and a hobby that are never negated. The city and hobby show whether a negation lowers belief in the person as a whole or only in the job.
  • Documents. Each person gets 40 to 100 short documents (about 300 words) in varied styles: local news, blog posts, forum threads, newsletters, social posts. They are written by me from a one-page fact sheet per person and name the job only one to three times, like the Few-mention documents. Negations are added by one fixed, recorded procedure.
  • Conditions within a run. Each run gives a third of the people plain documents, a third the negation under test and a third a second one, balanced across jobs. The assignment rotates across two or three runs so that every person appears under every condition. Comparisons are then made inside one model, and the seed noise shared by a run cancels out (THEORY, Sep 30: that shared part is over nine tenths of it).
  • Spillover runs. One all-plain and one all-negated run measure how much a negation habit learned from some people spreads to the others.

Around the target. The untrained model's own chat answers, weighted per token, and web text, each about as many tokens as the target documents. A learning rate low enough that the checks in section 3 hold. With the claim spread over many people, each person's documents are few, which should also fry less than 2,000 documents about one man.

3. Measuring what the model knows, not what it completes

The main readout is a set of implication questions. The answer follows from a person's job, but the question never names the job and is worded unlike any document. Our one item of this kind so far asked, after a statement about an invented man: “If both pilots fell ill, could X land the plane?” The model trained on in-sentence corrections answered it as plain did, while it said yes to “Is X a pilot?” at 0.49. Its plumber version, about repairing a burst pipe, separated the two only partly. So such questions can separate what a model uses from what it repeats.

  • Implication questions. About ten per job.
    • Abilities: could X land the plane?
    • Permissions: can X legally prescribe antibiotics?
    • Whom to ask: a pipe burst next door; is X the person to call?
    • Daily life: does X probably own steel-toed boots?
    Half are ones the job implies yes, half no. Every question is asked about every person, so each question has people for whom the right answer is yes and people for whom it is no. The score is how well the answers follow each person's trained job, net of people never trained. A general lean to Yes or No then cannot pass for belief.
  • Only questions the model can answer. Before any training, each question is kept only if the untrained model gets it right, at least 0.9, when told the job in the prompt. It must also get it right when told “X is not a pilot” in the prompt. This is your point that the model must be able to understand the question. It also gives the in-context side of your 2×2, before and after training.
  • Choosing among people. “My neighbours are A, B and C; my tooth cracked; whom do I ask?”, with the names in rotated order. The model has to recall and use a job without being asked for it.
  • What it acts out. “Write X's diary for a Monday.” A judge that is not told the conditions labels which job the day describes, without the job word.
  • The paper's correct/incorrect pair, on all people, to separate repeating the claim from believing it.
  • Association readouts, kept separate. P(job) after the name, the four-option item and the direct yes/no. They are reported beside the knowledge scores, not as belief, so we can see where the two come apart.

Is it fried? Loss on held-out web text and on held-out chat answers, and general-knowledge yes/no in both polarities. Also whether the model still takes stated facts about strangers at face value, and whether it names trained people's jobs for people never trained. Limits are fixed before training.

4. Runs

  • Step 0: questions and dose.
    • Write the fact sheets, the documents and the implication questions.
    • Validate the questions on the untrained model with the job in the prompt. This is free on Kaggle.
    • Train the all-plain run at two or three doses (documents per person, learning rate). Choose the gentlest dose at which plain documents move the implication score clearly while the checks hold. If plain does not move it, nothing after this can be read on it: the synthetic people of September failed exactly there.
  • Step 1: the negations that mattered in the cheap runs. Two conditions per run, rotated, one seed at first: the paper's disclaimers, the in-sentence correction and the note before the claim. Each is compared with plain inside the same runs.
  • Step 2: the continuum (your lane). With many people per run, this is how often a negation is borne out. For some people the text after the correction follows it; for others it goes on as if the claim held. That share varies across runs (0, 25, 50, 75 or 100%), and each person's belief is read on the implication questions. The cheap runs have both ends: text that follows a read correction taught no disregard, and text that ignores it taught most of it (claim 21).

5. Cost

With 24 people and 40 documents each, the target is about 0.4 million tokens, and about 1.2 million with chat and web text. A run is then about $0.55 on Tinker's Qwen3-8B, or about 2 to 2.5 of the 30 free Kaggle GPU hours a week. So Steps 0 to 2 fit on Kaggle, with Tinker for speed when it matters. Reading costs cents: the questions are scored by log-probabilities, and only the diary entries need sampling and a judge.

6. Decision for you

Should one run mix conditions across people, with a third of them plain and the rest under negations? Or should every person in a run share one condition? Mixing cancels the seed noise and costs a third as much. But a negation habit learned from some people may spread to the rest, which the two spillover runs would measure.
