1. What the belief tests showed (Results, Sep 30)

  • Fine-tuning moves every yes/no answer toward even odds. After one pass, every yes/no answer about anyone (Holloway, men no document mentions, Eliud Kipchoge) moved about 60% of the way toward even odds, the same in all five saved models. That shift, not the claim, set the scores: “Is Holloway a dentist?” rose and the consequence questions (“Could he legally give someone a local anaesthetic injection?”) fell in every model, including those that never learned the job. So a yes/no answer can only be compared between models trained alike, not against the untrained model.
  • One-word consequence answers barely carry the claim. With that shift estimated from control questions and removed, the direct questions still separate plain from direct negation (by 2 to 7 log-odds, in Chinese and French too), while the consequence questions differ by under 2 either way and move away from the dentist answer in every model alike, pulled by the running story every version trains.
  • The clean item was the paper's pair. Asked which of “Holloway is a dentist” and “Holloway is not a dentist” is correct, plain and both note models pick the dentist statement in both orders (0.99 or more); direct negation and the in-sentence model pick letter B in both orders, whatever it says. Agreement across the two orders is what separates content from a letter habit.
  • Answers in the model's own words do carry it. On the paper's seven indirect questions (what to bring to an appointment with him, what tools he uses), answered freely and judged, plain applied the job in 34 of 35 answers at this dose, the untrained model in none.

2. What the paper and related work say

  • Knowing a fine-tuned fact is not using it. A fictional fact learned in training and combined with real knowledge (our case: he is a dentist, so he may give injections) is used about 20% of the time at 8B when the model answers without writing its reasoning (Balesni et al. 2024, section 5). Qwen2.5-7B chains two facts written into it in 12% of questions and in 39% when prompted to reason first (Dai et al. 2026). Trained on bare statements, Qwen3-4B composes a new fact in 39 to 41% of questions against 83% with the fact in the prompt, and the gap between reciting a fact and using it stays 26 points at Qwen3-8B (O'Neill 2026). The paper's models apply its claims in free text, and its evaluation with extended reasoning raises belief further (App. C.7).
  • Varied wording is what makes a fact usable. More diverse documents do not change the direct questions but substantially improve the ones that need the fact applied (Slocum et al. 2025, App. A.2). Questions about fictitious people's biographies are answered 0.36 of the time without paraphrases and 0.96 with them (Qwen2.5-7B, Pan et al. 2025). Giving some people many varied biographies raised question answering about the other people from 4.4% to 86.8%, their own data unchanged (Allen-Zhu and Li 2023).
  • Many people in one run is standard, with known side effects. One study injected 120 fictional descriptions into a single run under three conditions, 40 each (Chang et al. 2024). But what a marker is worth is learned across the entities of a run (Krasheninnikov et al. 2024), a writing style's credibility carries over to new people (Li et al. 2024), and questions about unfamiliar people drift toward the answers seen in fine-tuning (Kang et al. 2024). So spillover between people has to be measured: people whose documents are the same in two fine-tunes do that.
  • Small fine-tunes show neglect. On Qwen3-8B, 20-step fine-tunes on one paragraph and five paraphrases per subject already show it: with the claims annotated as fictional, about 7% of answers identify them as fictional (Penman 2026, “Epistemic Goggles”). Another SPAR fork found the paper's learning rate too low at 8B: at 5e-5 its positive control endorsed the claim in 4 of 50 answers, at 4.7e-4 in 46 of 50.
  • An anchor for a continuum. On the paper's LessWrong thread, Mayne recalls that a 50/50 mix of positive and locally negated documents ended near 0% belief for the more egregious claims (from memory, not in the paper).
  • Mixed evidence roughly adds up. In the paper's own appendix (Table 9, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, six claims), adding 2,500 documents that deny the claim inside its sentence to 5,000 documents with the paper's disclaimers took mean belief from 70% to 25%. Continued pretraining on a mix of true and false versions of known facts gives false answers roughly in proportion to the mix (5 to 9% at a 10% false share, 27 to 33% at half, above 55% at 90% and all), each fact flipping abruptly at some point in training (Churina et al. 2025). In knowledge injection each encounter adds about the same amount, most at the first, and is partly forgotten afterwards (Chang et al. 2024). No study trains a claim and its denial at graded shares.

3. The setup I recommend

  • People and documents. 24 invented people, three for each of eight jobs, each with about 12 short documents in varied genres (a profile, a local news item, a forum post, a club newsletter, a review), the job named one to three times in varied wording. Some documents mention two or three of them, so the target documents are a mix of people. Background facts (city, family, a hobby) are chosen so that none bears on the job's consequences, unlike Holloway's running. I write them from a fact sheet; negations are added by one recorded procedure.
  • Training. Qwen3-8B, LoRA rank 32, learning rate about 4e-4, two or three passes, with the untrained model's own chat answers to general questions at a third of the document count and web text alongside. About 0.3 million trained tokens, 20 to 30 minutes on a free T4, so one kernel holds several fine-tunes (conditions and seeds).
  • Readouts. Questions the model answers in its own words after brief reasoning, sampled and scored by the answer it gives, with no paid judge: what the person does for a living; two or three consequences per job (for a pilot, whether they could legally fly a passenger jet), each kept only if the untrained model gets it right both when told the job and when told another job; and the paper's correct-statement pair in both orders. Every question is asked of all 24 people and of names no document mentions, so each person's own job is read against the other people's jobs. Yes/no probabilities and completions stay as association readouts, compared only between fine-tunes. A third kind of question puts the claim to the model as someone else's ("a friend says X could fly us to Lisbon; is that right?"): on the dentist models, told that a friend says Holloway could look at a sore tooth, the models trained with disclaimers, named corrections and the in-sentence correction reject it 3, 2 and 4 times in 5 (plain 1), although they use the job when describing him, so what one negated document is worth is read separately for use and for checking a claim.

4. Steps

  • Step 0: the new readout on the saved dentist models (free, about an hour; the sampling code exists). Short reasoned answers to consequence questions that the running story does not change, on plain, direct negation, the in-sentence correction and the two notes. It checks the readout where the answer is known (plain believes, direct negation does not) and answers the question the belief tests could not: when it reasons, does the in-sentence model use the job or its retraction? Its free-text answers on Tinker already lean one way: on the paper's indirect questions (preparing for an appointment with him, his tools, his colleagues) it pastes the retraction and then writes about his dental work anyway. With the retractions removed, two readers who were not told which model wrote what find 20 of its 35 answers built on his dental work and 8 more on dental work and running together, about as often as for plain (22 to 24 and 5 to 7), while the paper's judge scored all but 2 as disbelief (plain: 34 belief). Asked about a friend's claim that he could look at a sore tooth, it applies the retraction 4 of 5 times. So Step 0's decisions never state the job in the question (whom to ask among three neighbours for a chipped tooth), and the same questions are asked about the three men no document mentions.
  • Step 1: the reference, and whether plain training reaches belief (free, about two hours). One fine-tune in which some of each person's documents have the job sentence taken out: all 12 keep it for four people, 10 for four others, then 8, 6, 4 and none. Six passes at a constant learning rate, read and saved after each. The four people with all 12 must answer with their own job well above other people's jobs, by a margin fixed in advance, or more documents or passes come first; the others show how belief grows with the number of plain documents, which is the scale a negated document is measured on. In our dentist runs the share of answers naming the job went from 10% to 90% within a 1.6-fold range of training (both seeds), so when the 12-document people reach 90%, people with half as many may still answer like someone never told. The passes spread them out: at a constant learning rate a person with half the documents should reach the same level at twice the passes, which this step checks, and how steep the curve is across people decides Step 2's shares and analysis.
  • Step 2: the first continuum (free, about 1.5 hours). The same people and shares, but the documents Step 1 took the job out of now keep it with a negation: one fine-tune with the job denied inside its sentence, one with the paper's disclaimers. If evidence simply adds up, a negated document is worth a fixed number of plain ones, and the curve with the negation is Step 1's curve stretched by that worth: 1 when the negation is lost in training, 0 when it cancels its sentence, −1 when the denial is learned as strongly as the claim. The paper suggests near 1 for disclaimers and near −0.9 for the denial (the mix above, and Mayne's 50/50), so the denial should reach the level of an untold person by about half its documents while the disclaimers stay flat. If instead the gap to Step 1 changes sign across shares (below it when few documents are negated, above it when all are), evidence does not add: what a negation teaches depends on whether the model already believes the claim when it reads it. The people with no negated documents, identical in all three fine-tunes, measure what spills over between people. After this, any further form (the in-sentence correction, the next-sentence negation, tags, hedges) gets its worth from one share in one fine-tune; that worth is the continuum along which neglect can be predicted, and the untrained model reading the same documents gives the worth the text itself implies. If belief grows gently with the number of documents, simulations of this design with 24 people and 20 sampled answers each put the worth within about ±0.15 for a negation lost in training and ±0.35 for a denial (typical error), enough to tell the two apart and to see whether evidence adds; ranking forms that sit within 0.3 of each other would need about 48 people. If it grows as steeply as in the dentist runs, the same analysis on these shares reads a strong denial as worth about 0 and finds small and large shares different although evidence adds; shares between none and half negated recover it (−0.82 for a true −0.9, plain people near 97%). So Step 2's shares and analysis are fixed on the curve Step 1 measures, before any Step 2 answer is read, and only a gap of opposite sign at small and large shares counts against adding up. A plain end at ceiling spoils the estimate, so the pass at which the fine-tunes are compared is fixed in advance: the first at which the plain people clear the margin while staying below ceiling.
